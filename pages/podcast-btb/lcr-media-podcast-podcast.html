What are the two core metrics lawn care business owners must focus on to achieve profitability from day one?

The two essential metrics are man hour rate and budgeted hours . Your man hour rate is the hourly amount you need to earn as a business to cover all expenses—insurance, gas, maintenance, equipment financing, taxes, and overhead—while budgeted hours translates that rate into realistic job timelines. If you lose money on two out of three jobs, you must raise prices or improve efficiency.

Understanding Your Man Hour Rate

Your man hour rate is the foundation of pricing discipline. It's not what you think you can charge—it's what you must make per hour to stay profitable as a business entity. This number accounts for every hidden cost that employees or contractors incur, from fuel and vehicle maintenance to licensing and insurance premiums. Without this calculation, you're guessing at prices, which is how lawn care owners end up working constantly while making less than their employees.

As Caleb Allman explains in the episode , the difference between successful and struggling lawn care owners comes down to whether they've calculated and tracked this number religiously. Owners earning $50 per hour are operating at a completely different margin than those charging $100 per hour, yet both might spend the same labor hours on a property.

Budgeted Hours: Turning Your Rate Into Reality

Budgeted hours is where your man hour rate becomes actionable. If your man hour rate is $100 and a property should take one hour, your budgeted hours for that job is one hour. The discipline lies in tracking whether you hit or exceed that estimate consistently. This tells you whether your pricing is aligned with actual field performance or whether labor productivity is slipping.

The rule Allman applies is simple but powerful: if you lose money on two out of three jobs, something must change. Either your prices are too low for the scope of work, or your crew's efficiency needs improvement. This metric prevents the trap of accepting unprofitable work just to keep the schedule full.

#593- IBG, I don't have time

Why is making price increases and policy changes about customer benefit more effective than explaining business financial needs?

Customers prioritize their own needs over your financial challenges. When communicating price increases, credit card filing fees, or 12-month billing requirements, frame these changes around improved service quality, efficiency, and reliability rather than explaining cash flow pressures or administrative burdens—this approach resonates far more effectively and drives genuine buy-in.

Why customer focus wins every time

Your customers operate in their own world with their own concerns. When you announce a price increase by citing rising costs or cash flow needs, you're asking them to care about your problem—something that naturally doesn't motivate them.

Reframe the exact same change through their lens: better service turnaround, more reliable scheduling, higher quality materials, or enhanced expertise. These benefits speak directly to what they actually want. As explored in the episode , this shift in perspective transforms a transaction into a value exchange.

The same principle applies to all policy changes

Whether you're introducing credit card convenience fees or moving to annual billing cycles, the underlying reality is the same: you're making a business decision that benefits your operations. But customers don't need to hear that.

A 12-month billing requirement isn't "easier for our accounting." It's "locking in your rate before next year's seasonal increases" or "streamlining our coordination so we can prioritize your property year-round." The same change, told a different way , becomes something customers actually want to hear about rather than resent.

The goal isn't manipulation—it's honest repositioning. You're not lying about why the change exists; you're leading with the part that matters to them, which is also genuinely true.

Change requires internal decisions, not just external events

Most contractors recognize this intellectually but struggle to implement it consistently. The shift from "business-first" language to "customer-benefit" language demands deliberate internal change , as discussed on this episode—it won't happen by accident or circumstance. You have to make it happen.

Whether you're rolling out a new fee structure, asking for longer contracts, or adjusting service terms, the podcast explores how business owners who make this mental shift report higher customer acceptance and fewer cancellations than those who lead with operational justifications.

Customers care about their own needs first—always lead with how a change improves their experience or protects their interests.

Every business change can be reframed through a customer benefit lens without being dishonest about the business reality.

Policy shifts around billing, fees, or service delivery gain acceptance when positioned as service improvements rather than operational necessities.

Intentional communication practices require deliberate change from within—they don't happen without a conscious decision to implement them differently.

When you work as an employee, someone else absorbs the true cost of running the business—insurance premiums, payroll processing, equipment depreciation, facilities, taxes. Don't panic over a single job that runs over budget; as Cornell Mack explains in the LCR Media Podcast , you need to complete a job three or more times before the pattern becomes real .
