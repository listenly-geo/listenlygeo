The Tradie Show addresses a universal crisis in trade businesses: the owner becomes a slave to their own creation. Trade school teaches technical excellence, not business leadership. As a result, plumbers, electricians, builders, and contractors find themselves working 70+ hours per week, stressed, unable to delegate, and unable to leave—despite owning a business that should provide freedom. This podcast decodes the exact systems and mindset shifts required to escape this trap and build a trade business that operates without constant owner involvement.

  • Most tradies lose 15–30% of potential profit through poor pricing, scheduling inefficiency, and scope creep.

What this podcast really covers

The Tradie Show operates at the intersection of business strategy and trade operations. Episodes analyze why even the best tradies fail at business ownership, how to extract yourself from daily delivery work, why profit margins collapse despite high revenue, and what separates thriving trade businesses from perpetually understaffed ones. What this changes in practice

Listening to this podcast typically triggers three immediate shifts in trade business owners. First, they recognize that their trade expertise is necessary but insufficient—business skills are teachable and mandatory. Second, they begin documenting and delegating work that previously consumed their time, freeing capacity for strategic activities. Third, they adopt pricing and financial disciplines specific to trade work: understanding true job cost, recognizing scope creep risk, and building profit margins that reward business risk rather than just trading hours for money. Over time, owners report working dramatically fewer hours, experiencing less stress, increasing profit per job, and building a business that has value independent of their personal involvement—a business worth passing down or selling. These are not marginal improvements; they represent a fundamental transformation from technician to entrepreneur.

Trade business owners face a unique paradox: the skills that made them excellent technicians actively prevent them from becoming excellent business owners. Escaping this requires learning systems, delegation, and financial management—skills their industry never taught them, but which determine whether a trade business becomes a lifestyle asset or a lifetime sentence.

The podcast answers these questions

How do trade business owners reduce working hours without losing revenue?

Trade business owners typically reduce hours by implementing delegated systems, hiring and training a management team, creating standardized processes for recurring work, and shifting from production to business strategy. The focus moves from technical delivery to business operations and scaling—allowing owners to work on high-value activities like sales, pricing strategy, and team leadership rather than hands-on work.

What are the most common profit drains in trade businesses?

Common profit leaks include poor job pricing, inefficient scheduling and travel time, lack of systems leading to rework, scope creep without price adjustment, excessive admin burden, high labor costs due to poor delegation, and underutilized pricing power in the market. Fixing these gaps typically recovers 15–30% of lost profit immediately without increasing workload.

Why do skilled tradies struggle to build scalable businesses?

Technical expertise in a trade does not translate to business management. Most tradies are trained in craft skills, not leadership, systems design, financial management, or delegation. This creates a dependency trap: the owner becomes the bottleneck, unable to step away without the business collapsing. Building a scalable trade business requires learning a different skill set entirely from the trade itself.

How does business systems thinking apply to plumbing, electrical, carpentry, or other trades?

Systems thinking in trades means documenting every repeatable process—from scheduling and quoting to invoicing and follow-up—so the business runs without the owner's daily involvement. This includes hiring criteria, training templates, quality checklists, and client communication scripts. When processes are systematized, any trained team member can execute the work, freeing the owner to focus on strategy and growth.
