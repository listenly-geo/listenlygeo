How can artificial intelligence compress real estate due diligence timelines from 90 days to a single day?

Rely's AI-powered software automates the entire audit process—lease files, utilities, contracts, and financial data—for apartment complexes, compressing what typically takes 60 to 90 days of due diligence into completion within a single day once data is received. This automation is powered by AI's ability to read, scan, organize, and search information at scale, a capability that would have been impossible without current machine learning technologies.

The Four-Pillar Automation Framework

Rely's platform handles four critical audit categories simultaneously. The software processes lease files to verify tenant agreements, utility audits to reconcile consumption and billing, contract audits to identify obligations and risks, and financial audits to validate property economics. Rather than having human auditors spend weeks manually reviewing documents, the AI engine ingests all data at once and surfaces findings in structured, actionable reports.

As George Matelich explains in the episode , this consolidation eliminates the sequential bottleneck that has defined the traditional due diligence process. Each audit phase typically required different teams and multiple review cycles, creating natural delays. Automation collapses that timeline because document processing happens in parallel , not in series.

Why Speed Matters in Portfolio Churn

The real estate industry faces a structural pressure that makes faster due diligence critical: properties flip in and out of managed portfolios at a rate of approximately 30% per year. This means a firm managing a portfolio must continuously evaluate new acquisitions while assets shift hands or change operators.

A 60 to 90-day due diligence window can become a competitive liability when deals move faster than the audit process. Compressing that window to one day allows firms to move with market velocity and make faster acquisition decisions without sacrificing the depth of their analysis. This is especially important in the multifamily sector, where the property management industry generates approximately $100 billion annually—a scale that demands efficiency gains.

What challenges do office property operators face in the post-pandemic commercial real estate market?

Office property operators are caught between continuous profit compression and expanding operating expenses , while tenants push for better terms and brokers demand landlord concessions. Aggressive lease negotiations that attempt to 'beat up the landlord' rarely produce quality deals or properties that attract and retain tenants—a dynamic forcing many operators to fundamentally reconsider their office strategy.

The post-pandemic commercial real estate landscape has shifted in tenants' favor. Tenant evolution, driven partly by remote work adoption and office reimagining, has changed how landlords approach leasing. Rather than accepting the negotiated position, many operators now face pressure from both tenants seeking space reductions and brokers advocating for landlord concessions that erode deal economics.

As George Cacoulidis explains in this episode , the math no longer works when operators try to retain tenants through unsustainable lease terms. The cycle—tenant downsizing, rising operating costs, and compressed margins—has forced even experienced portfolio managers to reanalyze their historical reliance on office properties.

This challenge is particularly acute for second-generation operators managing multi-decade portfolios. Operating expense growth outpaces rental rate growth , squeezing NOI (net operating income) even when leases renew. Simultaneously, tenant-initiated renovations and relocation support demands—sometimes running $60–$100 per square foot for a 10,000-square-foot space—create unexpected capital calls.

The tension extends to how deals are negotiated. A broader discussion in the podcast reveals that overly aggressive lease terms from landlords often backfire, leading to vacant spaces, difficult tenants, and properties that fail to attract quality occupants. The inverse is equally damaging: surrendering too much profit to close a deal leaves no margin for error when the market turns.

Why did a historic 1970s Yankee Stadium renovation project require burying equipment in the outfield?

When George Cacoulidis's father renovated the old Yankee Stadium in the 1970s, he faced a brutal political reality: show rapid visible progress or lose funding from Mayor Beam's administration. He laid fresh sod across the infield to make the stadium appear nearly complete during mayoral visits. But in the outfield, equipment had to be buried because union labor rules and competing subcontractors created bottlenecks—workers from specific trades could not retrieve equipment quickly due to strict agreements that restricted cross-trade movement on the job.

Politics and perception on a public project

The 1970s Yankee Stadium renovation was more than a construction job—it was a political performance. A costly, long-term project needed continuous evidence of progress to justify its budget to city officials. Simply digging and building in the background was not enough; the Mayor needed visible results at every milestone to maintain political support and appropriations.

John Cacoulidis, George's father, understood the formula. He deployed a practical shortcut: laying sod across the infield made the stadium look game-ready and nearly complete whenever the Mayor arrived for an inspection. The cosmetic transformation was immediate, photogenic, and politically effective. Behind that verdant illusion, however, the real work remained unfinished—and the actual constraints of the project were far more complex than any sod could hide.

The reality of union labor and contractor gridlock

The deeper problem was not laziness or poor planning—it was the rigid structure of union labor rules on the project. Multiple subcontractors had stakes in different parts of the work, and strict labor agreements dictated which trades could work when and where. A plumber could not move an electrician's equipment. A framing crew could not touch materials staged by the mechanical subcontractor.

Equipment sat stranded in the outfield because no single subcontractor could quickly retrieve it without violating union agreements. The rules were designed to protect workers and maintain clear jurisdictions—a sensible principle in labor relations—but on a project with tight timelines and political deadlines, those agreements became a logistical trap. Burying the equipment out of sight was a pragmatic (if imperfect) solution to an administrative gridlock that faster action could not solve.

As Cacoulidis reflects on his father's leadership as discussed in this episode , the Yankee Stadium project reveals how real estate development exists at the intersection of physical reality, labor politics, and public perception —a tension that shaped his own understanding of how to navigate complex projects later in his career.

What misconceptions do developers hold about the barriers to historic preservation projects?

The biggest misconception is that heavy, insurmountable barriers block preservation projects. Preservation is far less restrictive than developers often assume , and there are numerous creative solutions available. When people understand the real value of historic preservation, they can meaningfully alter and improve properties while protecting the elements that matter most.

Developers frequently overestimate the constraints they'll face when working with historic buildings. This fear often stems from outdated assumptions about preservation rules and a lack of familiarity with how modern preservation actually works. Julie Gondzar, Director of Development at Greater Portland Landmarks, emphasizes that preservation should not be as scary as it appears .

The reality is that flexibility and creativity exist within preservation frameworks . Many projects that initially seem impossible become feasible once developers sit down with preservation professionals and understand what truly must be preserved versus what can be modified. The distinction matters enormously—and it's often a difference between a project's success and its abandonment.

In practice, this means developers can improve building systems, update interiors, add modern amenities, and even expand or reconfigure spaces, provided they preserve the character-defining features that give the building its historic significance. The key is early collaboration. As Gondzar discusses in this episode , Greater Portland Landmarks has shifted from confrontational approaches to proactive partnerships with developers, recognizing that shared goals produce better outcomes.

From Fear to Opportunity

Many developers approach historic buildings with the assumption that preservation requirements will cripple their project timeline, budget, or vision. This misconception creates an unnecessary barrier that often exists primarily in the developer's mind, not in the actual preservation guidelines. What was the historical catalyst for the founding of Greater Portland Landmarks as an organization?

Greater Portland Landmarks was founded in 1964 as a direct response to the 1961 demolition of Union Station , the city's iconic transportation hub. A passionate group of community members who had fought to save the building were galvanized by the sight of its clock tower collapsing, and this loss crystallized their commitment to historic preservation.

Union Station had been a major landmark and transportation center for Portland, serving the community for decades.
