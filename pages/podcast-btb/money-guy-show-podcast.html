Here’s Where Wealthy People Put Their Money

What proportion of the top 10% of wealthy Americans own their primary residence?

Yes — 95% of Americans in the top 10% of wealth do own their primary residence , according to Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances data. This statistic reveals that homeownership, despite common assumptions about the ultrarich, remains a fundamental anchor of wealthy households' asset portfolios.

When exploring where wealthy people place their money, many assume yachts, luxury supercars, and Rolexes dominate their spending. But the Federal Reserve data paints a different picture. A home—a primary residence—is not a luxury indulgence for this group; it's a near-universal baseline of wealth stability .

The top 10% of wealthy Americans are defined by the Federal Reserve as those with a net worth of approximately $1.9 million. Within this group, homeownership is practically unanimous at 95%, suggesting that owning your primary residence is foundational to how affluent families organize their finances—not a secondary consideration.

Beyond the primary home: where wealth really concentrates

The 95% homeownership rate for primary residences is only the beginning of the real estate picture for wealthy Americans. As Brian Preston explains in the Money Guy Show episode , the wealthiest households diversify far beyond their primary home.

69% of the top 10% own additional real estate outside their primary residence —investment properties, vacation homes, or rental assets. This two-tier real estate strategy—primary residence plus investment real estate—is a defining characteristic of how affluent families build and preserve wealth.

But real estate is just one piece. Beyond property, wealthy Americans in the top 10% pursue equity ownership at a radically different rate than the general population. While only 58% of all Americans own equities (mostly through retirement accounts like 401k plans), 96% of the wealthiest 10% own equities , reflecting far greater market exposure and diversification.

Additionally, 48% of the top 10% hold direct business ownership or equity stakes in companies, and 80% have invested in 401k accounts, demonstrating a multi-layered approach to wealth preservation and growth.

What this tells you about building lasting wealth

The fact that 95% of wealthy Americans own their primary residence isn't just a demographic detail—it's a signal about financial stability and long-term thinking . Rather than treating homeownership as a stepping stone to abandon once wealthy, affluent households maintain it as a cornerstone asset while layering additional income-producing and appreciating assets on top.

This pattern suggests that the pathway to and through wealth involves owning your home, not renting indefinitely, while simultaneously building diversified investments in equities, real estate, and potentially business interests. The strategy is additive, not exclusive.

Yes. Traditional employment paired with consistent saving and smart investing is a proven path to significant wealth accumulation.

The core principle is straightforward: earn a wage, save a portion of it consistently, and invest those savings over time. This approach works because compound growth magnifies small, regular contributions into substantial sums across decades. The difference timing makes is striking. For W-2 earners, the strategy relies on three practical mechanics. First, use tax-advantaged accounts like a Roth IRA through platforms such as Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard —opening one takes ten minutes and is completely free. Second, establish a disciplined savings rate; financial advisors recommend saving somewhere between 10% and more of your monthly income, depending on your age and goals. An emergency fund also anchors this strategy. Rather than following simple rules like "save four times your monthly income," a more precise approach is to save three to six times your monthly expenses in a readily accessible account. How should self-employed or business owners manage tax obligations throughout the year?

When you earn self-employment income, split every dollar in half and deposit one half into a dedicated tax account immediately. This ensures you always have the government's money set aside and eliminates the shock of a surprise tax bill arriving in April.

This approach flips the psychology of tax season entirely. Instead of dreading an unexpected bill, you move through April with confidence—knowing the money is already there. The stress dissolves because you've already paid yourself and the government simultaneously, month after month.

The method works regardless of income volatility. Some months bring more revenue, some less. By setting aside half consistently, you build a buffer that accounts for both your lean and abundant periods. As Brian Preston explains in the Money Guy Show episode , this straightforward system removes the guesswork from quarterly estimated payments and year-end settlements.

Why half works better than trying to guess your tax rate

Many self-employed people attempt to calculate their exact tax liability—multiplying income by their marginal rate or reading IRS worksheets. This creates friction and often leads to either underpaying (and facing a penalty) or overpaying (and waiting months for a refund).

The 50/50 split sidesteps this complexity entirely. You're not guessing your effective tax rate—you're using a conservative figure that works across most scenarios. If you're in a higher bracket, you're covered. If you end up lower, you get a refund—a pleasant surprise instead of a nightmare.

This strategy pairs well with other income management habits discussed in the episode , where financial advisors address TikTok money myths and lay out practical frameworks for business owners at every income level.

Making the tax account work for you

Open this account at the same bank or financial institution where you hold your primary business account. Keep it separate so the money stays untouchable for daily operations. A high-yield savings account works perfectly—you earn modest interest while the funds remain liquid for tax season.

The beauty of this system is that it requires zero monthly calculation or decision-making . The moment revenue hits, half goes to the tax account. No spreadsheets, no dread, no surprises. You can spend the remaining half on business expenses, reinvestment, or personal income with peace of mind.

Should diversification be prioritized when building initial wealth from small amounts?

Diversification protects wealth but does not create it. If you split $500 into 10 different paths, nothing will move, but if you focus that same $500 on one concentrated path—whether a niche skill or business venture—it can compound exponentially . Most people stay stuck because they spread themselves too thin.

The distinction matters most when you're starting from zero. At the beginning of wealth-building, your capital is small and your time is limited. Splitting your attention and resources across multiple strategies dilutes the force of each one. A single, focused effort on one high-potential path creates momentum; fragmented effort creates stagnation.

This doesn't mean abandon all caution. As Brian Preston discusses in the Money Guy Show , the role of diversification is to protect wealth you've already built , not to build it in the first place. Once you've accumulated a meaningful amount, spreading risk across multiple assets is prudent. But in the earliest phases, concentration is the accelerant.

Consider the math of time. If you're earning an hourly wage and trying to reach a wealth target through work alone, the math becomes brutal. A typical path toward financial independence requires either a dramatic increase in income or a shift into a business or niche skill that compounds beyond hourly exchange. Splitting your evening hours between five different side projects means none reaches critical mass; dedicating them to one venture allows expertise and compounding to take hold.

The same logic applies to invested capital. A small initial investment of $2,500 has grown to over $100,000 through focused stock market investing. But that success required consistent, concentrated attention to one strategy , not splitting funds across unrelated vehicles. The compounding power of regular contributions to a single, quality investment compounds more visibly than the same contributions scattered.

What makes this principle so hard to follow is that it feels risky. Diversification is marketed as safety, and it is—once you have assets to protect. But in the phase when you're still building, concentration is the only force strong enough to move the needle. The fear of "putting all eggs in one basket" is actually the fear keeping most people in the stuck phase.

The practical pivot happens later. As explored further in this episode , once you've hit meaningful wealth thresholds—whether through a business, career climb, or disciplined investing—then diversification becomes your friend. It's the tool that ensures what you've built stays intact through market cycles and life changes.

The $500 test: why focus outpaces spread

The core insight is simple to test. Take $500 and split it 10 ways: that's $50 per path. Growth is nearly invisible, because each channel doesn't have enough mass to generate real momentum or to absorb the friction of learning and iteration. You're dabbling, not building.

Now take $500 and concentrate it on one path. Whether that's a single freelance skill, one business idea, or a single investment strategy, $500 applied with full attention can produce measurable movement . You develop expertise. You optimize. You reinvest gains back into the same channel. Compounding begins.

The advantage isn't just financial—it's psychological and skill-based too. Focused effort builds mastery. Mastery unlocks opportunities that diversified dabbling never reaches. A deep knowledge of one niche opens doors that shallow knowledge of ten niches cannot.

How should emergency fund targets be calculated relative to income or expenses?

Skip the outdated rule of multiplying your income by four. Instead, multiply your monthly expenses by three to six to find your target emergency fund. Your actual spending pattern matters far more than your income level—a more accurate, personalized foundation for real financial security.

A widely circulated rule of thumb suggests taking your monthly income and multiplying it by four to determine your emergency fund target. But this one-size-fits-all approach misses a critical insight: individual circumstances vary too much for a simple income-based formula to work reliably.

The problem with income-based calculations is straightforward—they assume everyone with the same paycheck faces the same financial needs. Two people earning $5,000 per month might have wildly different expenses. One could live on $2,000 monthly while another spends $4,500. When an emergency strikes, it's your actual outflows that matter, not your gross revenue.

As discussed in the Money Guy Show , the better approach ties your emergency reserve directly to your spending reality. Take your true monthly expenses—housing, groceries, utilities, transportation, insurance—and multiply that figure by either three or six, depending on your personal risk profile.

Why the range from three to six months matters

The three-to-six-month window acknowledges that emergency needs differ across people and seasons. Three months of expenses works for stable earners with minimal dependents and a robust professional network. Six months becomes necessary for freelancers, single-income households, or anyone in less predictable fields where finding replacement income takes longer.

Someone with $3,000 in monthly expenses needs $9,000 to $18,000 set aside—a range that shifts with their actual life, not a fixed multiplier of their paycheck. This approach also adapts automatically: if your spending drops through better budgeting, your target shrinks. If life circumstances change and expenses climb, so does your reserve target, without requiring you to remember a separate formula.

The podcast answers these questions

How much money do you actually need to retire comfortably?

The amount needed to retire depends on your target annual spending, expected investment returns, and retirement duration. A common framework uses a 4% withdrawal rate, meaning a $1 million portfolio supports roughly $40,000 per year — but listeners are urged to model their own numbers rather than rely on generic benchmarks.

Are 401(k) balances a reliable measure of retirement readiness?

401(k) balances are often misleading indicators because they reflect gross savings before taxes, fees, and real purchasing-power erosion. A balance of $500,000 may deliver significantly less in retirement income than its face value suggests, making net-of-tax projections essential for accurate planning.

Can a household earning $100,000 per year realistically build significant wealth?

A $100,000 household income is sufficient to build substantial wealth if savings are automated early and lifestyle inflation is controlled. The critical variable is the savings rate, not the income level — households saving 20–25% of gross income at this bracket can reach financial independence within two to three decades.

What distinguishes a trustworthy financial advisor from a conflicted one?

The most reliable signal is fiduciary status: a fiduciary advisor is legally obligated to act in the client's interest, while a suitability-standard advisor only needs to recommend products that are generally appropriate. Fee structure matters equally — fee-only advisors eliminate the commission-based conflicts that drive many problematic recommendations.
