What three key metrics should limited partners use to allocate capital across real estate and income-generating strategies?

Limited partners should evaluate capital allocation across three core dimensions: the portfolio's cash flow needs, the targeted overall return expectations, and mechanisms for risk mitigation or principal downside protection . When a fund's principals are backed by a family office and co-invest alongside limited partners at significant scale, this alignment creates a powerful additional signal—shared downside protection and genuine capital preservation incentives.

The first metric centers on a straightforward question: does your portfolio require immediate cash distributions, or can capital sit in longer-duration, higher-return vehicles? Cash flow intensity differs between income-heavy strategies (like stabilized multifamily) and opportunistic acquisitions that may generate returns in lumpier, event-driven patterns. This determines which fund structure fits your liability calendar.

The second metric is your return hurdle. As Raheem Charania explains in the episode , the market conditions—interest rates, transaction volumes, and macroeconomic headwinds—directly shape the return profile you can realistically achieve. In a compressed cap-rate environment, that same dollar chasing stabilized assets may deliver 5–7% returns; in a distressed acquisition, the same capital might generate 12%+ yields by exploiting pricing dislocations.

The third and most structural metric is downside protection. Co-investment alignment is a risk signal that often goes underweighted by allocators, yet it's a critical differentiator. When a fund manager or their family office owns significant skin-in-the-game alongside your capital, capital preservation is no longer a stated goal—it becomes a personal fiduciary duty. The manager and limited partners share the same waterfall; losses hurt both equally.

This independence matters enormously for portfolio construction. As Brendan Fay explains in the episode , allocators seeking exposure to private credit typically face intense competition that drives pricing down across the entire market. What regulatory change expands the addressable market for cannabis credit underwriting?

Federal rescheduling of cannabis eliminates Section 280E tax treatment, which previously taxed operators on gross margin rather than net income—a burden that consumed up to 70% of operator profits . Section 280E, a legacy tax provision dating back to the height of cannabis prohibition, imposed a severe structural disadvantage on the industry. Unlike any other business, cannabis operators could not deduct ordinary business expenses when calculating federal income tax. This meant the US government effectively claimed profits at the gross margin level, leaving net income too thin to service debt reliably.

Direct commodity exposure versus traditional financialization

When an investor buys a commodity ETF, they're typically holding futures contracts or derivatives on the underlying commodity, not the commodity itself. This means rolling costs accumulate each time a near-term futures contract expires and must be replaced with a further-out contract. The DRRU sidesteps this entirely by representing a one-to-one match with the actual in-ground resource—whether a barrel of oil, an ounce of gold, or a standard cubic foot of natural gas.

This direct representation creates a fundamental shift in the value relationship. Fee structure and liquidity advantages over private energy

Private energy plays—whether direct oil and gas partnerships, mineral leases, or energy funds—typically come with substantial management fees, often compounded by illiquidity.
