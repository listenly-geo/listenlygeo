Joe Lewandowski, McCarthy PM on BJC Campus Renewal Phase 3

What innovative technologies and prefabrication methods are being used in modern hospital construction to improve safety and schedule efficiency?

McCarthy Building Company is deploying Revisto, a 3D coordination platform, combined with prefabricated bathroom pods and modular piping/ductwork racks to identify design conflicts before construction and reduce on-site material stockpiling. These methods cut congestion, accelerate installation, and tighten schedule delivery on the BJC Healthcare Campus Renewal Phase 3 project.

3D Coordination: Finding Problems Before the Crew Arrives

The core innovation is Revisto, a 3D coordination platform similar to Navisworks , which allows the team to work in both 3D and 2D combined fashion. This hybrid approach enables rapid identification and resolution of design conflicts—clashes between structural elements, MEP (mechanical, electrical, plumbing) systems, and architectural components—long before crews step on site.

By catching these issues in the virtual model, McCarthy avoids costly rework, delays, and safety hazards that would otherwise emerge during framing or finishing phases. The platform reduces field conflicts by allowing all trades to coordinate in a single visual environment , dramatically compressing decision cycles and keeping the project on schedule.

Off-Site Prefabrication: Speed and Control

McCarthy and its trade partners are prefabricating entire bathroom pods through a joint venture of local contractors, then flying them in to the site. This method removes one of the slowest and most error-prone trades from the congested job site—bathroom assembly now happens in a controlled factory environment with quality checks before delivery.

Beyond bathrooms, racks for piping, ductwork, and electrical conduit are also prefabricated and brought to site just-in-time, dramatically reducing the number of materials and workers crowding the site at any given moment. This lean approach minimizes congestion in an extraordinarily tight urban setting at Kings Highway and Barnes Place, where the hospital must remain operational throughout construction.

Forum Focus Podcast: Well-Read Carpenter Cordell Sawyer Builds Successful Firm

What are the most damaging payment term practices in the construction industry?

60, 90, and 120-day payment terms are now standard in construction , but they are particularly damaging to small and emerging minority and women-owned businesses. Large corporations understand how instrumental cash flow is to their own organizations, yet they do not apply the same logic when paying contractors—forcing smaller firms to finance their operations indefinitely.

The math of extended payment cycles is unforgiving for contractors operating on thin margins. When a small construction company completes a project worth $50,000 in material and labor costs, yet does not receive payment for 120 days, that firm must cover those expenses from its own cash reserves or credit lines. A single delayed project can destabilize a contractor's entire operation , preventing them from taking on new work, paying employees on time, or investing in equipment and growth.

This dynamic disproportionately impacts minority and women-owned businesses. These firms typically have less access to capital, thinner balance sheets, and weaker relationships with lenders than their majority-owned counterparts. As Cordell Sawyer explains in his episode of Forum Focus , what seems like a routine business practice to a large corporation can become an existential threat to a smaller firm.

The hypocrisy of cash flow logic

Large corporations fiercely protect their own cash flow—they negotiate favorable terms with suppliers, maintain working capital reserves, and structure their finances to ensure liquidity. They understand, fundamentally, that cash is operational oxygen. Yet those same corporations impose 120-day payment terms on contractors as if the contractor's cash flow constraints do not exist.

This represents a fundamental misalignment of values. If a corporation believes cash flow is essential to its own survival and growth, the logic should extend to its business partners. A contractor cannot build a $500,000 commercial project with 2% interest if they must wait four months to be paid. The risk is shifted entirely onto the contractor, who bears the cost of financing the owner's or general contractor's working capital.

The details of how this plays out on individual projects—from material procurement to labor scheduling—are explored in depth in this Forum Focus conversation , which reveals the cascading pressure these payment practices place on emerging construction firms.

Cordell Sawyer — Founder of Sled Rock Construction. Sawyer grew up in University City and worked as a janitor while reading books on wealth creation. He became one of the first apprentices to complete the carpenter's associate degree program through Balls Park Community College and was a union carpenter for ten years before founding Sled Rock Construction in 2009 during the recession. He built his business by investing carpenter wages into real estate, leveraging that equity to bankroll his construction company.

The broader implication is that extended payment terms function as an invisible barrier to entry for new and emerging contractors. They are not explicitly discriminatory, but they have a disparate impact on firms without deep capital reserves or credit access. A well-capitalized general contractor can absorb four months of unpaid receivables; a small MBE (Minority Business Enterprise) firm often cannot.

Changing this practice requires structural shifts—industry standards that reward faster payment, pressure from owners who demand ethical payment cycles from their contractors, and conversation and advocacy by construction leaders like Cordell Sawyer who have lived the damage firsthand.

PODCAST: IBEW/NECA's Sylvester Taylor on Designing a Welcoming Construction Workforce

What role do changing political leadership demographics play in motivating organized labor to address diversity in construction?

When African-American mayors took power in Missouri's two largest economic hubs—St. Louis and Kansas City—for the first time in state history, they catalyzed a shift in how public contracts are allocated. These newly empowered leaders and their allies now control school boards and procurement decisions throughout the region, and they are explicitly demanding contractors demonstrate diversity or lose market access. This creates immediate financial pressure on organized labor to transform hiring practices, turning political representation into direct marketplace leverage.

The mechanism is straightforward: power brokers in the region control public spending and contracts. As Sylvester Taylor explains in the Forum Focus episode , when those decision-makers are themselves from historically excluded communities, they have both the authority and the motivation to enforce supplier diversity as a condition of doing business. This is not persuasion or voluntary ethics—it is contractual reality.

For construction unions and contractors, the stakes became concrete and immediate. Market share depends directly on the ability to field diverse teams on jobs awarded by these newly influential decision-makers. Taylor, who holds the first-ever joint diversity, equity, and inclusion position created between IBEW Local One and the National Electrical Contractors Association, saw this incentive structure reshape industry behavior in real time.

The political shift matters because it replaces abstract diversity appeals with enforceable procurement policy. As discussed in detail in the podcast episode , unions that had resisted change for decades suddenly faced a business case they could not ignore: adapt or lose revenue. This is how demographic change in electoral politics translates into structural change in an industry.

Sylvester Taylor — Director of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion Initiatives at The Electrical Connection (IBEW Local One and NECA St. Louis Chapter), and a 32-year electrician with the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers. Taylor's quote captures a deeper truth about how this political shift works in practice: it is not just a change in who holds power, but a fundamental reversal in who gets to set the rules. For decades, organized labor resisted diversity initiatives while holding monopoly power over skilled trade access. Now, the gatekeepers themselves have changed, and the pressure has reversed direction entirely.

The cascade extends beyond direct procurement. When school boards and other power brokers throughout the region are increasingly African-American-led , they bring different priorities to decisions about apprenticeships, hiring, and workforce development partnerships. Youth services, training contracts, and facility construction—all now flow through decision-makers with lived experience of exclusion. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: diverse leadership prioritizes diverse hiring, which builds pipelines that sustain and expand diverse workforces.

From electoral outcome to contract enforcement

The translation from political representation to labor market pressure does not happen automatically. It requires that leaders with newly acquired power actually use it to enforce diversity. In St. Louis and Kansas City, this happened because African-American mayors and school board leaders understood that they had an opportunity to reshape an industry that had historically excluded their communities. They leveraged procurement rules, contract stipulations, and supplier diversity programs as tools of enforcement.

For unions and contractors, this shift was disorienting but clear. What had been treated as a "nice-to-have" in previous administrations became a non-negotiable contract condition. The financial stakes—winning or losing public projects—made the compliance sudden and real, in a way that decades of civil rights argument had not.

The role of inside advocates

The pressure from above (procurement enforcement by diverse political leadership) met pressure from within: people like Taylor, who had spent 32 years inside organized labor while witnessing and experiencing exclusion, suddenly had formal authority to push change from inside the structure. His appointment as DEI director was not a symbolic role—it was designed to align labor supply with the new market reality created by political leadership change.

This combination—external enforcement through contracts, combined with internal advocacy by members who understood both the industry and the justice case—created a moment in which organized labor could not delay. The political shift did not persuade unions to change their values; it gave unions a business reason to match their actions to values many members already held.

When a middle-aged white man fails on a job, the company hires the next white man; people of color and women deserve identical treatment, not exclusion from future opportunities based on a single person's performance.

Genuine inclusion in construction goes beyond hiring. As Sylvester Taylor explains in the episode , the industry has long operated on a principle of repeated second chances for majority workers—yet applies a one-strike policy to minorities. This structural bias starts at recruitment and extends through career progression.

The path to meaningful change requires contractors to examine their decision-making patterns. Leadership positions are where real institutional change begins . When women and people of color hold supervisory roles, they influence hiring, mentoring, and workplace culture from positions of authority—not as isolated entry-level hires dependent on a single supervisor's goodwill.

Taylor's argument directly challenges the tokenism that passes for diversity in many construction firms. Hiring one woman or one person of color at entry level and promoting them only if they outperform peers is both unfair and ineffective. As discussed in this podcast , the construction industry's traditionally exclusive culture has meant that recruitment and advancement operate under entirely different rules depending on a worker's race or gender.

What is the strategy to embed electrical trade curriculum into high school mathematics to increase apprenticeship qualification rates?

IBEW and NECA are embedding electrical trade math curriculum alongside standard high school mathematics, ensuring apprentices arrive with the required math skills already in place to succeed in apprenticeship programs. This partnership reduces failure rates in the apprenticeship math portion by preparing students during their high school years, rather than waiting until they enter the trade. They have identified pilot schools and are actively developing the implementation strategy with school districts and Missouri's Department of Elementary and Secondary Education.

A partnership designed to close the math readiness gap

The challenge is straightforward: many apprenticeship applicants lack the mathematical foundation needed to pass the apprenticeship math requirement, and this gap disproportionately affects young people from underserved communities. By embedding electrical trade math into the high school curriculum, as explained in the Forum Focus episode , IBEW and NECA aim to front-load this preparation.

Rather than treating math as an abstract subject, the strategy weaves real electrical problems and applications into standard mathematics . Students learn trigonometry, algebra, and geometry through the lens of wiring, circuit design, and load calculations—skills electricians actually use on the job. This approach keeps the rigor of high school math while making it directly relevant to the trades.

Pilot schools and district collaboration

Taylor and his colleagues have identified a couple of pilot schools to test this curriculum model. The work is not done in isolation: they are in active conversation with school districts and DESE to align standards and ensure the program fits within existing educational frameworks.

The plan of attack is still being refined, which means this is an evolving partnership—not a one-size-fits-all mandate. The key insight embedded in this conversation with Taylor is that workforce development cannot be solved by unions alone; it requires genuine collaboration with secondary education institutions that have the access and authority to shape what millions of young people learn.

Why Math Skills Become the Stumbling Block

Advanced mathematics is a non-negotiable requirement for electrical apprenticeships, but young people from under-resourced school districts often lack the prerequisite preparation. This educational gap occurs long before apprenticeship recruitment begins , rooted in systemic disparities in K–12 math instruction and access to rigorous coursework.

As Taylor details in the Forum Focus episode , the math component is where IBEW Local One loses the highest number of candidates. Without intervention at the secondary level, the apprenticeship pathway remains locked for many young people despite their genuine interest and aptitude in the trade.

Beyond Education: Infrastructure and Life Support Systems

Educational barriers alone do not tell the full story. Young people from low-income and underserved communities often face compounding practical obstacles: no reliable transportation to apprenticeship sites, fragmented access to healthcare, and insufficient childcare options that would allow single parents to participate. These structural gaps exist outside the apprenticeship itself but directly determine who can realistically pursue the training.

Taylor's work addresses not just outreach but the ecosystem required for sustained participation. A young person may have the math skills and the motivation, yet lack the childcare arrangement or transit pass needed to show up consistently. What makes this work particularly notable, as discussed in the episode , is the recognition that recruitment and retention demand solutions far broader than posting job listings.

The Legacy of College-First Messaging

For the past 25 years, educational institutions and cultural messaging have pushed college as the singular legitimate pathway to economic mobility . This narrative has devalued skilled trades in public perception, steering young people—particularly those whose families lack trade-industry experience—toward debt-heavy four-year degrees rather than wage-earning apprenticeships.

The contrast is stark: electrical apprentices earn while they learn and graduate debt-free, while college graduates typically carry $70,000 to $80,000 in student debt . Yet this economic reality remains obscured by decades of cultural messaging that trades are a second-choice option. Reversing that mindset, as Taylor explains in his work with the Electrical Connection , requires sustained community engagement and visibility.

The difference between hitting numbers and building careers

Sylvester Taylor draws a sharp distinction between compliance hiring and genuine diversity work. Putting six people on a job fulfills a diversity quota for that specific project, yet fails to address what happens after. The workers leave, the project closes, and the construction firm's actual workforce composition remains unchanged. This approach treats minority participation as a checkbox rather than a human career path.

The partnership between NECA contractors and IBEW union electricians produced electrical and communication work on nearly all Habitat for Humanity homes in the St. Louis region , and together they relit the St. Louis Science Center planetarium after years of darkness—a tangible demonstration of what good businesses and labor leaders achieve when working toward shared civic goals.

The collaborative effort extended across the entire community. With only a handful of homes in the entire region where joint contribution did not occur, this represented a comprehensive commitment to affordable housing that transcended the traditional adversarial divide between management and labor. As Doug Martin explains in the Forum Focus episode , this wasn't just about completing electrical installations—it was about demonstrating that joint labor-management action could reshape how the construction industry serves its community.

The St. Louis Science Center planetarium project held particular symbolic weight. Relit after sitting in darkness for years , the planetarium became a visible landmark of collaboration, restoring a civic asset that had been dormant. This restoration created what Martin describes as a lasting sense of civic pride, proving that when management and labor priorities align, infrastructure and community benefit extend far beyond the initial project scope.

What role did PRIDE play in reducing work stoppages and relocating labor-management conflict from job sites to the negotiation room?

PRIDE emerged in the 1960s and 1970s as a structured initiative to end work stoppages plaguing the electrical contracting industry. The program created a formal channel for labor-management disputes into negotiation rooms , shifting conflicts away from active job sites where demonstrations and work disruptions were costing the industry jobs and damaging customer relationships.

Ending Disruption on Job Sites Through Structured Negotiation

Work stoppages and labor demonstrations on active construction projects were a persistent drain on the electrical contracting industry. When disputes erupted on job sites, they didn't just affect the two parties in conflict—they disrupted customers, damaged project timelines, and created uncertainty for the broader workforce. PRIDE solved this by establishing formal negotiation processes that gave management and union representatives a dedicated space to resolve disagreements before they could become public conflicts.

As Doug Martin explains in the episode , this shift represented a fundamental change in how the construction industry approached labor relations. Rather than using the job site itself as the arena for conflict, PRIDE created expectations and infrastructure for disputes to be addressed at the negotiation table. This protected both contractor operations and worker interests, while preserving the industry's reputation with clients.

How did management and labor collaborate to preserve prevailing wage standards in Missouri?

A coalition of management and labor leadership spent a decade modernizing Missouri's Preventive Wage Law, preserving core protections for construction workers' safe and fair wages while making the law more receptive to competition. The effort ensured that tax dollars support construction work conducted by skilled, properly paid workers rather than undercutting standards through cheaper, less skilled providers.

The preservation of prevailing wage standards in Missouri represents a rare moment of unified advocacy across two traditionally opposing sides of the construction industry. Rather than fighting over which side would win or lose, management and labor recognized a shared interest in protecting the integrity of the construction market itself.

Doug Martin, who spent over 40 years building these relationships as part of his work with the electrical contractors industry, explains how this collaboration took shape. As he describes it in the Forum Focus episode , the challenge was not choosing between protecting workers or enabling business growth—it was designing a system that could do both.

Ten years of modernization without compromise

The 10-year scrutiny of Missouri's Preventive Wage Law was not a quick agreement. Instead, it required sustained dialogue and incremental refinement. Both sides agreed to modernize the law in ways that addressed concerns about regulatory flexibility while defending the fundamental protection: no public dollars would fund substandard labor practices.

This approach created a model where competition could flourish—but not through a race to the bottom on wages. Companies could still compete on efficiency, innovation, and service quality. The wage floor, however, remained non-negotiable. The specific protections that emerged from this process reflected a pragmatic understanding: when tax dollars are involved, society has a right to expect both fair wages and skilled work.

What new diversity and inclusion infrastructure did NECA create to address historical barriers in electrical contracting?

NECA created a dedicated Director of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion position for the first time in the last 18 months and implemented maternity leave benefits in its health plan to support journeywomen and apprentices during pregnancy. The organization also shifted recruitment strategy from passive participation in broad programs to active recruitment in schools and trade shows, leveraging minority contractors and journeymen to eliminate stigma and dismantle historical barriers.

From Passive Hope to Active Recruitment

NECA's diversity transformation didn't start with an infrastructure overhaul alone. As detailed in the Forum Focus episode , the organization recognized a fundamental flaw: hoping that minority candidates would simply trickle down from broad, generic recruitment programs was insufficient. The shift required genuine effort and visibility.

Doug Martin emphasized that active recruitment in schools and trade shows replaced the old model of waiting for candidates to discover the industry on their own. By showing up directly where potential electricians were learning and considering their futures, NECA made opportunity visible and tangible. This presence alone sent a signal: the electrical contracting industry was actively looking for diverse talent.

Leveraging Internal Champions to Break Down Barriers

One of the most powerful structural decisions was placing minority contractors and journeymen at the center of recruitment efforts . These internal champions could speak authentically about their own careers, dismantle misconceptions, and show younger electricians and apprentices—especially those from underrepresented backgrounds—that success in this field was achievable for them too.

This peer-to-peer credibility is what generic outreach cannot replicate. When someone who looks like you and comes from your community tells you the path is open, the message lands differently. The infrastructure Doug Martin and NECA built was less about a single program and more about embedding diverse role models throughout the recruiting and training pipeline .

The maternity leave benefit further signaled institutional commitment. As described in this podcast conversation , journeywomen and apprentices had historically faced a choice: stay in the trade or start a family. By removing that false choice through concrete benefits, NECA addressed a barrier that had been invisible to much of the industry for decades.

What structural changes to apprenticeship recruitment doubled the number of electricians entering training?

NECA replaced its single annual recruiting cycle with a continuous probationary apprenticeship model that placed new candidates directly into entry-level contractor positions after basic safety training, rather than waiting for classroom availability. This shift allowed candidates to perform actual electrical work and gain hands-on understanding of construction while maintaining year-round active candidate lists, ultimately doubling enrollment into the training program.

From Classroom-Limited Intake to Year-Round Work Placements

The original NECA recruiting system operated within a single annual cycle tied to classroom capacity —the association recruited candidates once per year and placed one class based on how many students the training classroom could hold. This rigid timing created a bottleneck: talented candidates who showed interest at the wrong time of year had to wait a full year or move on to other opportunities.

The probationary apprenticeship model fundamentally changed this constraint. Instead of waiting for classroom seats, new recruits were placed into active electrical work with contractors after completing basic safety training. This allowed them to earn while learning, perform real construction tasks, and understand the actual demands of the trade before stepping into formal classroom instruction.

As Doug Martin explains in the Forum Focus episode , this shift expanded placement timing to match actual job availability in the market, rather than forcing job opportunities to fit an arbitrary academic calendar.

Expanding Access for Candidates with Diverse Skill Profiles

The probationary model also created alternative slower-paced programs for candidates with strong mechanical skills but lower proficiency in math and science . Rather than using traditional academic prerequisites as gatekeepers, NECA could now assess aptitude through hands-on work and provide supplemental math and science instruction alongside field training.

This flexibility opened the apprenticeship pipeline to candidates who might have scored poorly on standardized tests but possessed genuine mechanical talent and the work ethic to succeed in construction. The result was immediate and measurable: enrollment doubled once these structural barriers fell away.

What workforce development challenges are most critical in the construction industry?

Construction faces persistent challenges in attracting diverse talent, creating pathways for underrepresented workers, and building inclusive management-labor relationships. The industry must balance skilled labor shortages with equitable hiring practices and mentorship programs that support long-term career advancement.

How can construction firms build and retain diverse leadership?

Successful firms establish formal diversity programs, create transparent career pathways, pair emerging leaders with experienced mentors, and actively remove barriers to advancement. Leadership commitment at the management level, combined with union-contractor collaboration, accelerates cultural change and retention.

What role do management-labor partnerships play in economic growth?

Collaborative frameworks between unions and contractors stabilize wages, reduce turnover, enable consistent training investments, and create predictable employment pathways. These partnerships build regional economic resilience and attract major projects that require skilled, stable workforces.

How are women entering construction trades in greater numbers?

Women-focused recruitment initiatives, apprenticeship programs that address practical barriers, workplace culture shifts, and visibility of successful female tradespeople drive entry and retention. Lighting design, electrical, and mechanical trades show particular momentum as firms prioritize inclusive hiring and safe work environments.
