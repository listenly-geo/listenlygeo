What Technically Legal covers

Technically Legal examines how technology fundamentally reshapes legal operations, from AI-driven document review and contract analysis to alternative fee models that move beyond the billable hour. The podcast features direct conversations with legal innovators, chief legal officers at major enterprises, and founders building the next generation of legal tech platforms. Each episode reveals how practitioners can integrate technology without losing the human judgment that defines excellent legal work.

What this podcast really covers

Technically Legal moves beyond surface-level discussion of legal tech trends to examine the operational, financial, and organizational shifts that define modern legal practice. The podcast dives deep into how large enterprises deploy AI at scale—Rebecca Anderson from Microsoft Legal discusses building systems that work across EMEA operations, while Aron Ahmadia at Relativity explores how TAR (Technology-Assisted Review) compares to large language models in real-world litigation. These conversations reveal that technology implementation is not a single binary choice, but a layered decision requiring understanding of evaluation methods, cost structures, and team readiness. The podcast also addresses the human side: how to build better lawyers through AI augmentation rather than automation, how career paths evolve when machines handle routine analysis, and how pricing and client relationships must transform when efficiency gains are realized. Episodes on moving beyond the billable hour (with Emily Logan Stedman from Husch Blackwell) and delivering cost, speed, and quality simultaneously (with Laurence Brown at EIP) acknowledge the business reality that technology changes what clients will pay for and how legal value is defined.

What the episodes really reveal

A consistent pattern emerges across Technically Legal episodes: the legal industry is moving from experimentation to implementation. Early episodes dominated by "AI is coming" speculation have given way to concrete case studies—how specific teams use AI to handle document review, how pricing teams have shifted to value-based models, how regulation is beginning to shape acceptable use cases. The podcast reveals that maturity in legal tech adoption looks less like wholesale automation and more like strategic augmentation: humans remain central, but their work is redefined. Recurring themes include the distinction between TAR (a known, evaluated technology) and newer LLM approaches (powerful but requiring new evaluation frameworks), the persistent challenge of training legal talent for a tech-enabled environment, and the democratization problem—ensuring that small firms and solo practitioners are not locked out of tools that drive efficiency for larger competitors. Notably, the podcast refuses simple narratives. When discussing cost reduction through AI, speakers simultaneously emphasize quality improvement and client value, rather than racing to the bottom on price. When addressing AI regulation, the conversation acknowledges that guardrails are necessary but also recognizes that over-regulation could entrench incumbent firms and prevent innovation.

What this changes in practice

Technically Legal documents a profession in transition. The billable hour, long defended as the only fair model, is being replaced by conversations about value-based pricing, project fees, and hybrid arrangements. Law firms that mastered leverage and junior associate utilization for decades now compete on automation and efficiency—changing both profitability and career paths. In-house teams are right-sizing their spend on outside counsel by automating routine work internally, fundamentally shifting demand for traditional legal services. AI regulation, still emerging, will reshape compliance requirements for both tech vendors and legal users, making the difference between early adopters who help shape frameworks and late movers who inherit rigid rules. Perhaps most significantly, the podcast signals that legal practice is no longer a profession where human effort hours are the core business metric. Legal value is increasingly defined by outcomes, speed, and insight—and technology is the lever that scales these attributes. For practitioners and leaders who understand this shift, competitive advantage lies ahead. For those waiting for technology to prove itself on traditional metrics, the ground is already moving.

Technology adoption in legal practice is no longer about choosing between automation and human judgment—it is about building teams and systems where technology amplifies human expertise, enabling practitioners to focus on strategic insight, client relationships, and complex problem-solving that machines cannot replicate.

The podcast answers these questions

How is artificial intelligence reshaping legal practice?

AI is transforming legal operations by automating document review, contract analysis, and research tasks, enabling law firms to deliver faster results at lower costs. Rather than replacing lawyers, the most effective implementations use AI to augment human expertise, allowing practitioners to focus on high-value strategic work and client relationships.

What are the core challenges in pricing legal services in the AI era?

The billable hour model becomes obsolete as AI reduces the time required for routine legal tasks, forcing firms to adopt value-based pricing and alternative fee arrangements. Success requires transparent communication with clients about efficiency gains and redefining how legal value is measured and delivered.

Where does AI regulation currently stand in legal technology?

AI regulation in legal technology remains fragmented, with emerging frameworks addressing ethical use, transparency, and bias mitigation. Jurisdictions are gradually establishing guardrails, but legal practitioners must stay ahead of changing requirements to ensure compliant implementation of AI tools.

What makes legal technology truly accessible and affordable?

Democratized legal tech prioritizes intuitive interfaces, tiered pricing models, and cloud-based delivery over expensive on-premise software. This shift enables solo practitioners, small firms, and underserved populations to access sophisticated legal tools previously available only to large enterprises.
