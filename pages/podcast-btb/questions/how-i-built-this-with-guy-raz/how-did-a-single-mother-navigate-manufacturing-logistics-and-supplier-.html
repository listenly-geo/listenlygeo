How did a single mother navigate manufacturing logistics and supplier relationships without prior experience?

September 14, 2026 How I Built This with Guy Raz · Kim Vaccarella

← See the How I Built This with Guy Raz fiche

Kim Vaccarella asked a Chinese neighbor for a manufacturer connection, and he introduced her to a friend whose parents owned a factory in China. There, she discovered that EVA shoes and bags used identical machinery—EVA pellets mixed with color, heated, and cooled into molds to form finished products, giving her immediate insight into the production process.

A Neighborhood Connection Opens the Factory Door

When Vaccarella decided to move forward with Bogbag after being inspired by Crocs material at the Jersey Shore, she had zero manufacturing experience. Rather than research Alibaba alone or hire consultants, she asked her Chinese neighbor for guidance, a practical first step that proved invaluable.

The neighbor's connection led her directly to factory owners, eliminating the typical intermediary noise. She wasn't reading about manufacturing in articles—she was standing inside an actual EVA production facility, watching the process unfold firsthand and learning the core mechanics of how her product would be made.

From Raw Material to Finished Goods: The EVA Process

The machinery revealed a critical insight: the same equipment that shaped Crocs-like foam shoes could produce tote bags and accessories. EVA pellets—colored, heated, and molded—became the foundation of her entire product line, a discovery that simplified supplier relationships because she could focus on one core material and one type of manufacturer.

This hands-on exposure to the production cycle meant Vaccarella didn't have to negotiate blindly or depend on suppliers' explanations alone. She could see the quality controls, understand the cooling times, and recognize where defects might arise—knowledge that became crucial when her first shipment arrived defective, costing her $30,000 but teaching her to demand better accountability from future manufacturers.

"I will burn this effing factory to the ground. If you sell them, I will burn your factory to the ground."

That intensity reflected her stakes—she had borrowed $120,000 to find a new manufacturer after the first defect, and the full journey from side hustle to full-time entrepreneurship took seven years while she maintained her day job. Every supplier interaction carried weight.

Key takeaways

  • Personal networks can bypass formal manufacturing intermediaries—Vaccarella's Chinese neighbor connection led directly to factory owners rather than broker intermediaries.
  • Understanding the core production process reduces supplier dependency—knowing that EVA pellets, heating, and molding were universal to her product line simplified logistics negotiations.
  • Firsthand factory observation builds quality control authority—seeing the machinery and process firsthand gave Vaccarella credibility to demand accountability and reject substandard shipments.
  • Early setbacks crystallize supplier relationships—the $30,000 defective shipment forced her to establish clearer quality standards and find manufacturers aligned with her standards.

What motivated someone with no formal business training to start manufacturing despite initial self-doubt?

Vaccarella initially did not think she had any business starting a business, felt she was not smart enough, and didn't think she would be taken seriously. However, inspiration struck at the Jersey Shore when she encountered Crocs material while with her family.

How long did it take to transition from side hustle to full-time entrepreneurship while maintaining a day job?

Kim Vaccarella kept her day job at the commercial real estate lending office for seven years while building Bogbag. In the early years, she couldn't afford to leave her controller position earning $100,000 per year.

What lesson did early product rejection teach about customer feedback versus initial setbacks?

When Vaccarella's first major shipment from China arrived with defective bags, she initially quit the business. However, the customers who had purchased the bags before the defect was discovered drove her to borrow $120,000 and find a new manufacturer.
