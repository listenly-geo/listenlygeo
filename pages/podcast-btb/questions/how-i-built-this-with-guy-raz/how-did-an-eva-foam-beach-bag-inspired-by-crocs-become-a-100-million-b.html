How did an EVA foam beach bag inspired by Crocs become a $100 million business?

September 10, 2026 How I Built This with Guy Raz · Kim Vaccarella

← See the How I Built This with Guy Raz fiche

Kim Vaccarella designed a washable, structured beach bag made from EVA material after playing with Crocs shoes at the Jersey Shore. She pitched it to Crocs, but when rejected, bootstrapped the business herself—and after a devastating $30,000 loss on defective inventory from China, borrowed $120,000 to restart with a new manufacturer. Today, Bogbag has sold close to five million bags and generates more than $100 million a year in sales.

From a Jersey Shore idea to rejection—then ruthless resilience

Vaccarella grew up in Ridgewood, New Jersey with a police officer stepfather and a waitress mother. She dropped out of high school at 17, worked her way through North Jersey clubs as a waitress and bartender, and eventually built a 26-year career in commercial real estate lending, reaching a controller role at $100,000 annually. By her own measure, she had stability. Then came the beach day that changed everything.

Playing with Crocs material at the Jersey Shore with her family, Vaccarella envisioned a structured, durable beach bag that could withstand sand, water, and repeated washing. She approached Crocs directly—and was turned down flat. Rather than accept defeat, she became determined to prove the concept worked. The rejection lit a fire instead of closing a door.

The road to manufacturing proved brutal. Her first shipment from a Chinese supplier came back with defective inventory, costing her $30,000 out of pocket—a hit that could have ended the dream. But Vaccarella borrowed $120,000 to find a new manufacturer and try again, a bet she placed on conviction alone. She kept her day job for seven years while building Bogbag on nights and weekends, a testament to the dual life many founders live before scale arrives.

Five million bags and a retail foothold

Today, Bogbag occupies shelf space at Target, Nordstrom, and Dick's Sporting Goods, a retail spread most startups never achieve. The company has sold close to five million bags and now generates over $100 million in annual sales. What began as a personal frustration—the lack of a truly functional beach bag—became a category-defining product built on refusal to quit after the first setback.
