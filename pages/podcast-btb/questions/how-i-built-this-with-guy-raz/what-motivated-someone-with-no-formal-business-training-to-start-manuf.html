What motivated someone with no formal business training to start manufacturing despite initial self-doubt?

September 13, 2026 How I Built This with Guy Raz · Kim Vaccarella

← See the How I Built This with Guy Raz fiche

Kim Vaccarella believed she wasn't smart enough to run a business and had no formal training to fall back on. But a lifetime of side hustles—from selling decorated daisies as a child to reselling clothing on eBay while raising two kids—had given her an unshakeable resourceful mindset. When Crocs dismissed her bag pitch, that resourcefulness flipped a switch: instead of accepting rejection, she decided to manufacture the bag herself.

From Street Sales to Rejection, Then Action

Vaccarella grew up in Ridgewood, New Jersey in the 1970s and 80s with limited money and parents working long hours. She didn't sit still. As a child, she sold decorated daisies door-to-door—a small act that taught her how to approach strangers and handle rejection. Years later, while raising two children on a modest income, she resold clothing on eBay, squeezing entrepreneurship into the gaps of her everyday life.

That pattern of resourcefulness defined her when she walked into a Crocs-inspired idea. She'd been at the Jersey Shore with her family, playing with Crocs shoes, when the concept for a washable EVA beach bag took shape in her mind. She pitched it to Crocs directly. They said no.

Most people with no business background would have stopped there. Vaccarella did the opposite. As she explains in the episode, Crocs' rejection didn't defeat her—it activated her. She borrowed $120,000, found a manufacturer, and launched Bogbag in 2011. That resourceful kid selling daisies had become a woman who would build her own company rather than wait for permission.

"I will burn this effing factory to the ground. If you sell them, I will burn your factory to the ground."

The intensity of that statement—made to a manufacturer who was selling Bogbag knockoffs without permission—reveals something crucial: Vaccarella's self-doubt about *being* a businesswoman never actually stopped her from *acting* like one. She had spent seven years working her day job in commercial real estate lending while building Bogbag on nights and weekends, too afraid to leave the safety of a $100,000 salary until the business proved itself.

Yet she learned to protect what she'd built with the same ferocity she'd used to start it. The self-doubt was real, but as explored in detail in this interview, it never overrode the survival instinct and resourcefulness that had kept her moving since childhood.

A Track Record of Making Something From Nothing

Vaccarella's real motivation wasn't confidence—it was pattern. She had a 26-year career in commercial real estate lending before Bogbag, yet she still felt like an outsider to business. What she *did* have was proven ability to move from idea to action without waiting for validation. Selling daisies, reselling clothes, working as a waitress and bartender in North Jersey clubs, building expertise in a male-dominated lending industry—each was a small act of resourcefulness in the face of limited options.

When Crocs said no, she didn't question whether she was worthy of a yes. She questioned whether she needed their permission at all. That shift—from seeking approval to building on her own terms—is what Vaccarella reflects on in this podcast, turning a moment of rejection into the spark for a company that now sells close to five million bags and generates more than $100 million in annual revenue.

Key takeaways

  • Self-doubt about having business training didn't stop Vaccarella from acting like an entrepreneur—decades of side hustles had already wired her to move.
  • Rejection from Crocs catalyzed action rather than paralysis; she chose to manufacture her own bag instead of accepting a no.
  • Growing up with financial insecurity taught her resourcefulness, the same resourcefulness she applied when building Bogbag on nights and weekends for seven years.
  • Her motivation wasn't confidence or training—it was a lifelong pattern of making something from limited resources and moving forward despite doubt.
