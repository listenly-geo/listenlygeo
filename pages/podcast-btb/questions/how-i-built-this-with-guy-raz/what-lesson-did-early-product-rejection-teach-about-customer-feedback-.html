What lesson did early product rejection teach about customer feedback versus initial setbacks?

September 11, 2026 How I Built This with Guy Raz · Kim Vaccarella

← See the How I Built This with Guy Raz fiche

When Kim Vaccarella's first shipment of Bogbag inventory from China arrived defective, she was ready to quit—but her customers had already fallen in love with the bags. The critical insight was recognizing that she had been fixated on product imperfection while her buyers were focused entirely on what the product offered them. That gap between her perfectionism and customer reality became the fuel to restart the business.

The turning point came when Vaccarella faced a $30,000 loss on that initial defective shipment. Discouraged and convinced the venture was a failure, she nearly abandoned Bogbag altogether. But the customers who had purchased bags before the quality issue surfaced did something unexpected: they asked for more.

Vaccarella realized she had been measuring the product through a lens of flaws and manufacturing imperfections, while her customers were measuring it through the lens of usefulness and value. This misalignment between founder-focus and customer-focus was the real lesson. As she explored in the episode, that customer validation became her permission to try again—to invest the $120,000 needed to find a new manufacturer and properly relaunch.

"I will burn this effing factory to the ground. If you sell them, I will burn your factory to the ground."

The deeper pattern Vaccarella uncovered applies to any early-stage founder facing rejection. Product flaws and market rejection are different signals. A defective shipment is a supply-chain problem; customer silence would be a market problem. When customers kept asking for bags despite the quality issue, she had her answer: the market existed. The factory could be replaced. Her vision was sound.

This reframing—from "my product failed" to "my supplier failed, but my customer wants it"—gave her permission to persist. As detailed in this podcast episode, she borrowed $120,000, found a new manufacturer, and rebuilt Bogbag from that setback into a brand now stocked at Target, Nordstrom, and Dick's Sporting Goods. What started as a near-fatal loss became a pivot point—not because the product was perfect, but because the people buying it were real.

The gap between perfection and purpose

Many founders measure success by eliminating flaws. Vaccarella's discovery was that customers measure success by solving a problem or meeting a need. Listening to what customers actually wanted to buy proved more valuable than fixing what she thought was broken.

She didn't wait seven years—the time it took before leaving her commercial real estate lending controller role—because Bogbag was flawed. She waited because she was building capital, confidence, and a distribution network. The defective bags didn't kill the business; they exposed a manufacturing relationship that needed to change. And the customer demand that came despite that setback was the permission slip she needed to invest further and double down.
