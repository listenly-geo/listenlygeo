What are the two distinct economic models for career paths, and how do they apply to commercial real estate roles?

September 2, 2026 Commercially Speaking Podcast · Daymond Kirby

← See the Commercially Speaking Podcast fiche

Commercial real estate careers split between two economic models: the time-and-effort economy, where income depends on showing up and working your hours, and the results economy, where income is entirely tied to what you produce, regardless of hours spent. The industry has traditionally been results-focused, but it is shifting to include more stable time-and-effort roles like property managers and salary-based positions blending base pay with commission—and choosing the right model for your temperament is essential to long-term career satisfaction.

The two models and how they shape your career

In the time-and-effort economy, you are paid for your presence and labor—salary, hourly wages, or benefits tied to consistent work. This model appeals to people who value predictability, security, and a stable paycheck. You know exactly what you will earn each month, and there is no risk of income collapse if you hit a temporary market downturn or a slow sales cycle.

The results economy operates on the opposite principle: your income depends entirely on outcomes you produce—commissions on deals closed, profits from investments, or revenue from your client base. There is no safety net; if you don't sell, you don't earn. But there is also no ceiling: your income is limited only by your ability to generate results.

Historically, commercial real estate has been built on the results economy. Brokers earn commissions, investors profit from deal performance, developers reap returns from projects. But as discussed in the Commercially Speaking Podcast episode, the industry is evolving. Property managers, asset managers, and increasingly, brokerage firms are offering salary-based and hybrid compensation packages that blend base pay with performance incentives, creating a middle ground for professionals who don't want pure commission risk.

Matching the model to your temperament and life stage

Not everyone thrives under the same system. Results-based roles demand tolerance for uncertainty and the ability to self-motivate without a guaranteed paycheck. They suit people who are competitive, driven by control, and willing to bet on their own productivity. They also require either capital reserves or the freedom to survive lean periods—a significant barrier for early-career professionals or anyone with dependents.

Time-and-effort roles attract people who prioritize security and prefer a predictable income stream. They work well for those managing family obligations, building an emergency fund, or simply wanting to separate their livelihood from the stress of variable earnings. As explained in this podcast, some professionals need this stability to function at their best; others feel constrained by it.

The critical insight is that one model is not superior to the other. Career satisfaction hinges on alignment between your economic model and your personal values. Placing a high-energy, self-directed person in a salaried role can lead to frustration and underperformance. Similarly, forcing a security-minded professional into pure commission work creates constant anxiety. Understanding which economy you belong in is a prerequisite for long-term success in commercial real estate.

Key takeaways

  • The time-and-effort economy rewards consistent work with stable income; the results economy ties all earnings to measurable outcomes and offers unlimited upside but no floor.
  • Commercial real estate has traditionally operated on results-based compensation, but is evolving to include hybrid and salary-based roles for property managers, asset managers, and other positions.
  • Career satisfaction requires honest alignment between your personal temperament—whether you thrive on security or control—and the economic model of your role.
  • Understanding which economy suits you is foundational to making the right career choice and avoiding burnout or frustration early in your commercial real estate journey.
