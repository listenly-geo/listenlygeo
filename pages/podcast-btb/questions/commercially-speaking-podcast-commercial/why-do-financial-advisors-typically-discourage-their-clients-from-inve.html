Why do financial advisors typically discourage their clients from investing in alternative investments?

September 25, 2026 Commercially Speaking Podcast · Bob Frazier

← See the Commercially Speaking Podcast fiche

Financial advisors actively discourage alternatives because they lack foundational knowledge of this asset class and aren't required to understand it. This knowledge gap feeds fear and misconceptions, leading advisors to portray alts as risky and fringe—a position conveniently protected by their existing conflicts of interest and their commission-based models tied to traditional public markets.

Knowledge gaps and structural incentives

Alternative investments fall entirely outside the standard training and licensing framework for most financial advisors. As Bob Frazier explains in the episode, alternatives are not part of an advisor's database or required curriculum. Without this foundation, advisors rely on stereotypes rather than understanding—they position alts as "wing nutty, edgy, fringy, and super risky" despite the fact that you can equally make poor investments in public stocks.

The deeper issue is structural: most financial advisors earn commissions or assets under management (AUM) fees from traditional investments. Directing a client toward private credit, distressed debt, or other alternatives means losing that recurring revenue stream. The incentive system itself is misaligned with what might genuinely serve the client's portfolio.

A valuable profession hampered by blind spots

Frazier acknowledges that financial advisors do serve a legitimate purpose—managing relationships, enforcing discipline, and keeping clients from making purely emotional decisions. However, very few advisors actually understand alternatives well enough to evaluate them properly. That gap doesn't just limit choice; it actively biases the conversation away from an entire category of investments.

This matters because, as discussed further in this conversation on alternative investment characteristics, professionally managed private credit and distressed debt can offer stability that public markets don't—structured terms, predictable cash flows, and downside protection mechanisms that traditional advisors rarely explain to their clients.

"The first thing you want to focus on is not losing money. Warren Buffett said the number one rule of investing is don't lose money. The number two rule is don't forget rule number one."

The philosophy Frazier articulates—protecting capital first, capturing upside second—is fundamentally at odds with the "buy and hold public equities" narrative that dominates traditional advisory. Yet this risk-first mindset is precisely what separates ultra-wealthy investors from everyday ones, and it's embedded in how alternative investments are actually structured and managed.

Key takeaways

  • Alternatives aren't part of standard financial advisor training, licensing, or knowledge requirements—this gap fuels misconception and fear.
  • Commission-based and AUM-fee models create a direct financial incentive for advisors to steer clients toward traditional public markets instead.
  • Financial advisors do serve a valuable function in discipline and relationship management, but very few understand alternatives well enough to evaluate them fairly.
  • The contrast matters: you can make poor investments in both public stocks and alternatives, yet advisors consistently frame only the latter as "risky" or "fringe."

How does the SEC regulation Reg D 506C enable alternative investment offerings?

Reg D 506C deals allow you to advertise or market the investment, and if somebody is an accredited investor recognized by the SEC, they are able to invest directly in these opportunities.

What are the key characteristics that define alternative investments like private credit?

Alternative investments are professionally managed passive investments where you put money into the deal with no other contribution. Private credit features structured terms, defined maturity windows, and multiple liquidity opportunities per year.
