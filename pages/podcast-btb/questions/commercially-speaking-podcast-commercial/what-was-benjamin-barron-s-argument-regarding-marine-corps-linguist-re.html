Why does the Marine Corps struggle to retain Arabic linguists?

September 18, 2026 Commercially Speaking Podcast · Benjamin Barron

← See the Commercially Speaking Podcast fiche

The Marine Corps invests nearly a quarter million dollars and over two years training Arabic linguists, only to watch them walk out the door after their five-year contract expires. Linguists can immediately turn around and earn double their military salary working as contractors or NSA employees in civilian clothes, doing the identical job they just left—making retention a massive cost-benefit problem the military cannot ignore.

The economics of military language training

When the Marine Corps commits to training an Arabic linguist, the financial and temporal investment is substantial. The language school program alone requires more than two years of intensive study and specialized instruction. Combined with this time, the total investment approaches a quarter million dollars per linguist. As Benjamin Barron explains in his Sergeant Major of the Marine Corps writing competition paper, this investment is built on the assumption that linguists will serve their full five-year obligation—a requirement justified by the length of language training.

Yet that assumption breaks the moment the contract ends. A linguist who completes five years of service has two immediate career paths: stay in the military at a Marine's salary, or step into the private sector as a contractor working for defense firms or the National Security Agency. The civilian salary for the identical work often doubles what the military was paying them, and there is no barrier to switching—the linguist can resign on the last day and return to the same job in civilian clothes the next morning.

Why the retention crisis matters

Barron's argument centered on the fact that the Marine Corps loses not only the trained personnel but also the return on a massive investment. Every linguist who departs is effectively a quarter-million-dollar loss in sunk training costs, plus the operational disruption of losing someone with years of specialized expertise and security clearances already in place.

The issue goes deeper than simple economics. In his writing competition submission, Benjamin advocated that the Marine Corps needed to do more—better incentives, retention bonuses, or career pathways—to compete with the civilian market. Without structural change, the military invests two years and a quarter million dollars to train linguists who can immediately leave for double-pay civilian roles. That math does not work.

The problem is systemic: retention cannot improve without addressing the salary and opportunity gap. Barron's paper made the case that ignoring this disparity wastes not only money but also the institutional knowledge and security clearances that trained linguists represent—a resource the military cannot easily replace.

Key takeaways

  • The Marine Corps invests more than two years and approximately $250,000 training each Arabic linguist.
  • Linguists are required to serve five-year contracts justified by the length of their training program.
  • Upon contract completion, linguists can immediately earn double their military salary in civilian contractor or NSA roles doing identical work.
  • The military loses both trained personnel and return on investment when linguists depart for the private sector.
