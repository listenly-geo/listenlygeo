When does word-of-mouth referral start driving hobby revenue?

September 8, 2026 Commercially Speaking Podcast · Joe Daly

← See the Commercially Speaking Podcast fiche

The first three years of Daily Goods were driven almost entirely by organic word-of-mouth and referral, with coworkers who saw leather goods in person asking to buy them, then gifting them to friends who became customers in turn. Each recipient became an unpaid promoter, creating a self-sustaining referral loop that generated consistent early revenue without any active marketing effort.

How the referral loop built itself

Joe Daly's leather business didn't require him to chase customers or spend money on marketing during those opening years. The momentum came naturally: coworkers at church would see him carrying handcrafted items, ask if they could buy one, then give the product as a gift to someone else in their life. That recipient would then reach out directly to Joe for their own purchase, creating a chain reaction of discovery.

This pattern repeated itself consistently across his network. The gifts functioned as high-touch product demonstrations—people received something handmade, felt the quality, and wanted to acquire more for themselves or pass along to others. As detailed in this episode of Commercially Speaking, Joe credits this humbling, organic approach as the primary driver of early traction, a contrast to the aggressive growth tactics that many new makers adopt immediately.

Why this isn't every business's story

Joe is candid about one crucial point: this referral-first model depends on product quality and personal proximity. His customers were initially people in his everyday circles—coworkers, church community, friends of friends—all close enough to see the work firsthand and trust the maker. Not every business has that built-in audience or that immediate access to word-of-mouth channels.

The deeper pattern is timing. Referral revenue typically begins flowing as soon as the hobby reaches a stage where someone outside the maker's own hands has experienced the product firsthand. For Daily Goods, that happened in year one; the real expansion came later when the business had matured enough to expand beyond those initial networks. Joe discusses the turning point moment when a specific event in 2020–2021 catapulted growth approximately 10x year-over-year, marking the shift from side-hustle referral to serious scaling.

"If you work with your mind, rest or Sabbath with your hands; if you work with your hands, Sabbath with your mind."

Key takeaways

  • Word-of-mouth revenue begins when a hobby reaches quality and visibility such that people in the maker's immediate circle experience the product firsthand and want to buy or gift it.
  • Daily Goods' first three years relied entirely on organic referral—coworkers seeing items in person, purchasing them, then gifting to others who became customers in turn.
  • This referral loop is not universal; it depends on product quality, personal proximity to customers, and access to a built-in audience within the maker's network.
  • Referral-driven growth, while slow, requires zero marketing spend and builds deep trust before transitioning to broader scaling strategies.
