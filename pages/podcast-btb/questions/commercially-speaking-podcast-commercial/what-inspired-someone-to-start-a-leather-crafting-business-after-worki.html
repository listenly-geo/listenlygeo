What inspired someone to start a leather crafting business after working in church operations for a decade?

September 6, 2026 Commercially Speaking Podcast · Joe Daly

← See the Commercially Speaking Podcast fiche

A colleague's journal became the spark: after seeing a friend receive one as a gift at church, Joe Daly joked for months that he could make one himself. In May 2018, he drove to a Tandy Leather store in Dallas with that journal in hand and walked out with $299 worth of bison leather and tools—enough to begin what would become an eight-year journey before turning the hobby into his full-time business.

The moment was deceptively simple. Daly had spent a decade managing operations, finance, HR, and facilities for local churches in Dallas and Waco. He wasn't looking for a new career—he was looking for something to do with his hands. That journal, a tangible object made by another person, cracked open something in him: the possibility that he too could create.

When Daly brought home the cow hide that first day, his wife was surprised. But she didn't stop him. What started as a late-night hobby in the garage became a rhythm of incremental craftsmanship. He wasn't chasing scale. He wasn't building a brand strategy. He was simply making leather goods—journals, wallets, bags—one at a time, learning by doing, selling mostly through word-of-mouth and later Facebook Marketplace.

Eight years of patient growth before the pivot

For the first three years, Daily Goods remained almost invisible. Organic growth. No pressure. Then something shifted around 2020–2021, and the business began accelerating roughly 10x year-over-year. By the time Daly decided to leave church operations and commit fully to his craft in 2024, after 18 months of operating Daily Goods as a full-time venture, the decision had already been made for him: the company had simply grown too large to manage alongside his church responsibilities and family life.

As Daly shares in the episode, one of his handmade leather bags sold at auction for $2,000—a price point that would have been unimaginable during those first nights in the garage. Yet the real value of the transition wasn't monetary. It was the realization that a hobby can evolve into authentic work without losing its soul—if you let it develop on its own terms.

"If you work with your mind, rest or Sabbath with your hands; if you work with your hands, Sabbath with your mind."

What makes Daly's story distinct from the typical "passion economy" narrative is how he addresses the dangerous collision between hobbies and monetization—a tension explored deeply in the full conversation. Unlike influencers who are told to monetize their passion immediately, Daly let the business breathe. He didn't race to Shopify on day one. He didn't build a personal brand. He simply made better leather goods, week after week, year after year, until the market found him.

Key takeaways

  • A single observed object—a colleague's leather journal—planted the seed that grew into a decade-long creative journey.
  • Daly's initial $299 investment in bison leather and tools from Tandy Leather was the only capital barrier; everything else came from consistent hands-on practice.
  • The first three years of Daily Goods operated almost invisibly, with organic growth through word-of-mouth before accelerating 10x annually around 2020–2021.
  • The decision to go full-time wasn't driven by ambition but by necessity—the business had simply outgrown its place as a side hustle.
