How many generals and admirals has Pete Hegseth fired or blocked from promotions since becoming Secretary of Defense?

August 20, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← See the full The Daily podcast page

Since taking over as Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth has fired more than a dozen generals and admirals outright. When you include those blocked from promotions they had already earned, the total reaches 65 to 70 individuals — approximately 10 percent of the entire U.S. general officer corps. According to New York Times reporter Greg Jaffe, the real number is likely even higher.

10 Percent of the General Officer Corps — and Probably More

The figures come from Jaffe and his colleague Kate Kelly, who investigated Hegseth's overhaul of the Pentagon's senior ranks. Their reporting identified two distinct categories: those directly fired, and those quietly denied promotions they had earned through the normal military advancement process.

That second category is the larger one. Blocking a promotion leaves no formal paper trail comparable to a dismissal — which is part of why Jaffe warns the 65-to-70 figure is a floor, not a ceiling. The true scope of the purge is, by design, difficult to fully measure.

Jaffe, who has covered the U.S. military continuously since 1999, calls this period unprecedented in 26 years of reporting. He states he cannot think of anything remotely close to the scale of what is now happening inside the Pentagon.

A Purge Framed Around "Merit" — and Enforced Through Chilling Effect

Hegseth's stated justification is merit: he argues that many senior officers rose through the ranks because of DEI programs rather than genuine military performance, and that he is correcting that distortion. His September 2025 speech at the Pentagon explicitly framed the removals as "clearing out the debris" and "ending the war on warriors."

But Jaffe's reporting reveals a second, quieter mechanism at work. The firings and blocked promotions send a signal throughout the entire force — not just to those directly affected. Officers still in the pipeline understand, clearly, that advancement now depends on passing political litmus tests as much as on demonstrated military performance.

"When you start firing people for political reasons, it's not just the person you fire, but it's the message that sends throughout the entire force that if you want to move up, there are political litmus tests that you have to pass through."

Key takeaways

  • Hegseth fired more than a dozen generals and admirals directly, and blocked a further group bringing the total to 65–70 people — roughly 10 percent of the entire general officer corps.
  • The blocked-promotion category is the largest and hardest to track, which is why the true figure is almost certainly higher than what has been confirmed.
  • Jaffe, with 26 years of military reporting experience, considers this purge without precedent — not comparable in scale to any previous Pentagon leadership transition he has covered.
  • Beyond the individuals removed, the firings function as a systemic signal: officers throughout the force now understand that advancement requires passing political tests, not just military ones.
