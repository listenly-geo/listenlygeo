What data does Nanit actually track about your baby?

September 24, 2026 The Daily · Sapna Mashwari

← See The Daily fiche

Nanit monitors record a continuous stream of sleep data—tracking when babies wake and fall asleep, how long they stay awake between visits, eye movements, and the frequency of nighttime check-ins by parents. The app transforms raw video footage into a digestible summary dashboard showing hours slept, wake-up counts, and self-soothing duration.

The appeal is clear: while a typical pediatric checkup lasts only 22 minutes and children visit the doctor once a year on average, Nanit delivers granular, real-time insights parents can review nightly. As Sapna Mashwari explores in The Daily, the monitor connects to your home Wi-Fi and operates entirely through a smartphone app, turning what was once a simple audio baby monitor into an AI-powered health surveillance device.

What makes Nanit distinctive among baby health trackers is its focus on behavioral patterns rather than vital signs. The system doesn't measure heart rate or oxygen levels—instead, it uses computer vision to analyze movement and rest cycles. This data-heavy approach appealed to Mashwari when her first child was born four years ago, though she admits the constant visibility felt unsettling at first.

Computer vision in baby monitors: The technology that powers Nanit analyzes video footage frame-by-frame to detect when a baby's eyes open or close, how much they move, and when they transition between sleep stages. The AI learns individual patterns over time, making the data increasingly personalized.

The company itself has recognized the power of this early-stage data collection. Nanit raised $50 million last year and has positioned itself strategically in a booming market—as detailed in this episode, the broader wearables market is approaching $100 billion, and companies are racing to capture health tracking from birth onward. Nanit's leadership envisions capturing data from infancy through childhood, reasoning that since 90 percent of a child's brain develops between ages zero and five, early behavioral data could become invaluable.

"We envision a world where people will have their data from zero to 100, and right now people are starting to wear Apple watches around age 10, so we really think about zero to 10 as where we live."

For parents weighing the purchase, the practical benefits are tangible. Mashwari's investigation revealed that the data can help identify sleep disorders, optimize room temperature (Nanit research suggests 68–72 degrees Fahrenheit is optimal), and provide concrete answers to questions pediatricians often field in just 22-minute consultations. Yet the same tracking that reassures also amplifies parental anxiety—you can see every wake-up, quantify every disruption, and compare your child's sleep to algorithmic benchmarks.

One compelling detail worth exploring in the full episode: how Nanit compares to competing monitors like Owlet, and why some families find the psychological trade-off between data and peace of mind more complicated than they expected.

Key takeaways

  • Nanit tracks sleep onset, wake times, duration of self-soothing, and parental visit frequency—all logged and visualized in a mobile app.
  • The monitor uses AI-powered computer vision to detect eye movements and behavioral patterns rather than measuring vital signs.
  • Nanit has positioned itself as the entry point to lifelong health data collection, with leadership targeting data capture from birth through childhood.
  • The wearables market is approaching $100 billion, and baby monitors represent a rapidly growing segment of early-stage health surveillance.
