Why did President Biden's selection of Franchetti differ from Secretary of Defense Lloyd Austin's recommendation?

September 17, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← See The Daily podcast fiche

Secretary of Defense Lloyd Austin recommended Sam Paparo, a senior Navy officer with deep Pacific experience, for Chief of Naval Operations. But Biden overruled Austin and selected Lisa Franchetti instead, making her the first female Chief of Naval Operations. Paparo was reassigned to U.S. Indo-Pacific Command to leverage his expertise on China-related challenges.

This decision was unusual but not unprecedented. The president ultimately holds the constitutional authority to appoint the Chief of Naval Operations, regardless of the Secretary of Defense's recommendation. While Austin's choice of Paparo was grounded in operational expertise—particularly his background handling Indo-Pacific command responsibilities—Biden had both strategic and symbolic reasons for his selection.

Biden's choice reflected his interest in Franchetti's qualifications and his desire to make history. As Greg Jaffe explains in The Daily, this represented a significant departure from traditional succession planning within the Pentagon's military hierarchy. Rather than simply deferring to Austin's recommendation based on operational continuity, Biden balanced competing priorities: maintaining the Indo-Pacific focus that Paparo provided elsewhere while elevating Franchetti to the Navy's top operational post.

The distinction between Austin's operational logic and Biden's decision highlights a broader tension in military leadership appointments. Austin's focus on continuity and Pacific expertise made strategic sense for maintaining U.S. military readiness in the region. However, Franchetti's confirmation by the Senate—95 to 1 with overwhelming bipartisan support—suggests her qualifications transcended the political calculus that often surrounds senior military appointments.

This case reveals how presidential authority in military appointments operates in practice. While cabinet secretaries like Austin provide critical input on operational grounds, the final decision rests with the commander-in-chief. The Daily episode explores how Franchetti's path to this role—breaking through barriers that once prohibited women from combat roles entirely—intersected with Biden's strategic vision for military leadership.

Interestingly, the realities of military appointments under Hegseth reveal a starkly different approach, with shifting priorities now dominating officer selection, where political considerations have begun to override traditional merit-based advancement.

Key takeaways

  • Biden had constitutional authority to overrule Austin's recommendation, though Austin had suggested Sam Paparo based on his Indo-Pacific experience.
  • Franchetti was confirmed by the Senate 95 to 1, demonstrating broad bipartisan confidence in her qualifications despite the presidential override of the Secretary's choice.
  • Paparo was reassigned to U.S. Indo-Pacific Command to maintain critical China-related expertise in a key strategic region.
  • Biden's decision balanced operational expertise with symbolic representation, making history by appointing the first female Chief of Naval Operations.

What was the combat exclusion ban and how did it affect Lisa Franchetti's early Navy career?

The combat exclusion ban was a policy in place when Franchetti joined the Navy in the 1980s that prevented women from serving on combat ships or in combat roles, significantly limiting their advancement opportunities.

What is the current status of women in senior Navy leadership under Pete Hegseth?

For the first time in at least a decade, there will be no women nominated to admiral rank under Hegseth, even though women now make up approximately 21 percent of the U.S. Navy.

Why is the politicization of military promotions considered dangerous for national security?

When people are fired for political reasons, it sends a message throughout the entire force that advancement requires passing political litmus tests rather than earning promotions based on merit.
