What is the current status of women in senior Navy leadership under Pete Hegseth?

August 30, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← See The Daily fiche

For the first time in at least a decade, no women will be nominated to admiral rank under Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. Women currently represent approximately 21 percent of the Navy, meaning the leadership increasingly looks less like the force it oversees—a gap that removes crucial role models for junior female officers and represents a setback for senior female officers like Lisa Franchetti who fought hard to reach their positions.

The scale of this shift is stark. When Lisa Franchetti was one of only 184 female surface warfare officers in the entire Navy in 1989, the path forward seemed limited. Yet she persisted, and when she was nominated for Chief of Naval Operations—the top Navy job—the Senate confirmed her 95 to 1, with overwhelming bipartisan support. That nomination and confirmation represented a milestone for women in the Navy.

Under Hegseth's tenure as Secretary of Defense, however, the trajectory has reversed. As Greg Jaffe reports in The Daily episode, no female officers are being elevated to admiral rank, breaking a pattern that had held for at least ten years. This is not a gap in one year—it is a systematic exclusion at a moment when the Navy is expanding its female officer corps.

A leadership gap that mirrors a broader purge

Hegseth arrived at the Pentagon with a stated mission to remove what he characterized as officers compromised by DEI initiatives. The Daily's investigation reveals that between 65 and 70 people have been fired or blocked from earned promotions—roughly 10 percent of the entire general officer corps of admirals and generals combined. More than a dozen generals and admirals have been dismissed since he took office.

The exclusion of women from admiral nominations fits into this larger restructuring, but it carries distinct consequences. Unlike a standard promotion freeze, the absence of women in the highest ranks sends a particular message: that advancement into Navy's most senior circles is reserved for officers who align with Hegseth's vision of what the military should look like.

"When you start firing people for political reasons, it's not just the person you fire, but it's the message that sends throughout the entire force that if you want to move up, there are political litmus tests that you have to pass through."

For junior female officers coming up through the ranks, the removal of senior female role models has immediate professional and psychological impact. The absence of women in admiral ranks signals that leadership roles at the top are not equally accessible—a message that reverberates through recruitment, retention, and career planning at every level below.

The episode documents how officers like Franchetti—who overcame long odds to reach the highest ranks—now watch as the door closes behind them for the next generation of women pursuing the same path.

Key takeaways

  • For the first time in at least a decade, no women are being nominated to admiral rank under Pete Hegseth's leadership of the Department of Defense.
  • Women comprise approximately 21 percent of the Navy's active-duty force, creating a widening gap between the officer corps and senior leadership.
  • The exclusion of women from admiral nominations is part of a broader purge affecting 65 to 70 officers—roughly 10 percent of the general officer corps—fired or blocked from promotions.
  • The absence of female role models in top ranks sends a powerful signal to junior female officers about the political criteria for advancement in Hegseth's military.
