What was the Inspector General complaint against Admiral Daryl Caudill and how was it resolved?

August 24, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← Voir la fiche The Daily

An Inspector General complaint was filed against Admiral Daryl Caudill roughly a year before his nomination was being considered. It stemmed from remarks he made to sailors at a Navy awards banquet, where he used analogies that suggested victims of sexual assault bear partial responsibility for attacks. The IG ultimately cleared him — finding he had done nothing wrong — which removed the last formal obstacle on the path to his White House nomination.

Remarks at an Awards Banquet That Prompted Formal Complaints From Sailors

The incident occurred during a discussion of the Navy's sexual assault awareness program. Caudill, speaking to sailors at the banquet, used analogies widely understood to imply that victims share some blame for assaults committed against them. Sailors who heard the remarks filed complaints, which triggered the Inspector General process, as Greg Jaffe details in this episode of The Daily.

The nature of the analogies was serious enough that the complaint had been open for approximately a year by the time Caudill's name entered the conversation for a top Navy leadership post. That timeline matters: the investigation was already well underway when Pete Hegseth's Pentagon was weighing his candidacy — it was not a new allegation surfacing at the last minute.

The IG Cleared Caudill — and the Nomination Moved Forward

The Inspector General concluded that Caudill had done nothing wrong. With the complaint formally dismissed, there was no longer any open disciplinary process blocking his path to a senior nomination. The clearance effectively handed the White House an unencumbered candidate at a moment when Hegseth was actively reshaping the top ranks of the military.

This episode sits at the center of a broader pattern that The Daily examines in depth: who gets pushed out under Hegseth, and who gets elevated in their place. Caudill's case is one of the most pointed examples — a nomination that proceeded despite a recent IG investigation, in the same period when Hegseth fired or blocked 65 to 70 officers, roughly 10 percent of the entire general and admiral corps.

"When you start firing people for political reasons, it's not just the person you fire, but it's the message that sends throughout the entire force that if you want to move up, there are political litmus tests that you have to pass through."

The Caudill nomination is directly connected to the removal of Admiral Lisa Franchetti, the former Chief of Naval Operations. Franchetti had been confirmed by the Senate 95 to 1 — overwhelming bipartisan support — before Hegseth dismissed her. Caudill emerged as her potential replacement in a process that, as Jaffe reports in this episode, involved active outreach to Hegseth's circle even while Franchetti was still serving.

What did Admiral Daryl Caudill do to position himself for the Chief of Naval Operations job while Franchetti was still in post?

While Franchetti was still in her role, Caudill directed a senior officer on his staff to reach out to Hegseth multiple times during December to offer help — an unusually aggressive move that signaled his ambitions long before any vacancy was officially open.

Why did Pete Hegseth fire Admiral Lisa Franchetti as Chief of Naval Operations?

Hegseth had criticized Franchetti in his 2024 book, arguing she was not qualified for the job and had only gotten it because she was a woman — identifying her as a symbol of the DEI-driven promotions he was determined to reverse upon taking office.

Who is Lisa Franchetti and how did she rise to become Chief of Naval Operations?

Lisa Franchetti grew up in Rochester, New York, attended Northwestern University, and joined the Navy after being recruited on campus. She rose through the ranks over decades, beginning in 1989 when she was one of only 184 female surface warfare officers in the entire United States Navy.

Key takeaways

  • The IG complaint against Caudill centered on remarks at a Navy awards banquet implying sexual assault victims share responsibility for attacks against them.
  • The complaint was approximately one year old when his nomination was being actively weighed — not a minor or quickly resolved matter.
  • The Inspector General found Caudill had done nothing wrong, formally closing the investigation and clearing the path for his White House nomination.
  • His case is part of a sweeping reshaping of the officer corps under Hegseth, who fired or blocked roughly 10 percent of all U.S. generals and admirals.
