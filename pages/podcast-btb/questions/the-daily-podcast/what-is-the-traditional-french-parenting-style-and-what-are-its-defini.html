What is the traditional French parenting style, and what makes it so distinctive?

Traditional French parenting is highly rule-bound, built around one central idea: children belong in adult society, and they must be shaped to fit it — not the other way around. From toddlerhood, children are expected to eat complex, multi-course meals including strong cheeses, follow strict bedtime schedules, and defer to parental authority without negotiation. The result is a household where adults retain their own lives, identities, and rhythms alongside their role as parents.

This model rests on firm boundaries rather than emotional negotiation. The assumption is that structure itself is a form of care: by setting clear rules early, parents equip children with the self-discipline and social graces they need to function in adult company. France's internationally recognized reputation for well-behaved children at the table — and, more broadly, in public life — is largely rooted in this philosophy. Pamela Druckerman's 2012 bestseller Bringing Up Bébé made this approach famous in the United States, introducing American readers to a model that had always felt self-evident on the French side of the Atlantic. What gives this tradition its staying power — and now its flashpoint status — is that it is not merely a set of parenting tips. It is a coherent vision of childhood itself: children as future adults in training, not as individuals whose feelings must guide family life. That vision is now being challenged by a generation of French parents drawn to gentler, more child-centered methods, igniting one of the fiercest cultural debates in contemporary France.

"Parents were essentially abandoned by adults with exclusively empathic attitude — and for Goldman and her allies, that is exactly why it is time to go back to a more disciplinary form of education."

— Madeline Schwartz, Reporter, The New York Times

About Madeline Schwartz

MS
Madeline Schwartz
Reporter · The New York Times

Madeline Schwartz is a reporter for The New York Times, based in Paris. Her authority on this subject is not merely professional — it is biographical. Born to an American mother and a French father who separated when she was a baby, Schwartz grew up moving between New York and Paris, attending school in both cities and absorbing both cultures from the inside. She did not study French parenting from a distance; she lived it in two versions simultaneously, navigating the strict meal rules and bedtime expectations of French households alongside the more emotionally expressive atmosphere of American ones. That dual upbringing gives her a lens few reporters can claim: she understands, from personal experience, exactly what is at stake in this debate and why it resonates so deeply with parents on both sides. In this episode of The Daily, she draws on that background to explain not just what the two styles look like in practice, but why the clash between them has become so charged in France today.
