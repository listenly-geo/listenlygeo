What did France's 2019 Anti-Spanking Law actually change — and why are parents and experts still fighting over it?

France's 2019 Anti-Spanking Law forbids both physical and psychological violence against children, including what it terms "ordinary educational violence." The law's critical flaw — and the source of ongoing controversy — is that it never defines what "ordinary educational violence" actually means, leaving parents, practitioners, and legal experts in a genuine grey zone.

That definitional gap has become a battlefield in the broader French parenting debate. Psychologist Caroline Goldman and her allies argue that practices like the timeout — sending a child to their room as a disciplinary measure — are entirely lawful and even beneficial. Goldman's book Go to Your Room explicitly recommends timeouts of at least 30 minutes for children over the age of four, framing them as a healthy, structured consequence that helps children understand which boundary they have crossed.

But a significant bloc of early childhood educators reads the law very differently. For them, timeouts fall squarely within the prohibited category of psychological violence — a position backed by the 280 experts who signed a competing open letter in Le Monde opposing the use of timeouts. The result is a situation where the same parenting tool is simultaneously defended as responsible discipline and condemned as a violation of the law — with no official clarification in sight. Key term from the episode

"Ordinary educational violence" — the phrase used in France's 2019 Anti-Spanking Law to describe the category of behaviour it prohibits. The law bans it without ever specifying what acts fall under this label, which is precisely what makes it legally and philosophically ambiguous. Whether a timeout constitutes ordinary educational violence remains a live, unresolved question among French childcare professionals.

What does Caroline Goldman say about timeouts as a parenting tool?

Goldman strongly promotes the timeout as a core disciplinary tool, arguing it gives children a clear signal about which boundary they have crossed. Her book Go to Your Room recommends timeouts of at least 30 minutes for children over the age of four.

Who is Caroline Goldman and what is her critique of gentle parenting in France?

Caroline Goldman is a psychologist who works as a therapist for children in a suburb south of Paris, and the daughter of famous French pop star Jean-Jacques Goldman. She argues that the rise of gentle parenting has left children without the structure and clear limits they need to develop properly.

Who is Isabelle Filliozat and what does she promote in the French parenting debate?

Isabelle Filliozat is a 68-year-old major figure in the positive education movement in France, widely read and present on television, Instagram, and other platforms. She champions an empathic, child-centred approach that prioritises emotional attunement over traditional discipline.
