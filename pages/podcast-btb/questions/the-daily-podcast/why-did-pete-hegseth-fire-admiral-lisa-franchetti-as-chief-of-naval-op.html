Why did Pete Hegseth fire Admiral Lisa Franchetti as Chief of Naval Operations?

August 22, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← Voir la fiche The Daily

Pete Hegseth fired Admiral Lisa Franchetti not because of any failure in performance or military competence — she had done nothing wrong on either count. He fired her because he had already condemned her in his 2024 book, arguing she was unqualified and had only been given the job because she was a woman. It was a political and ideological decision, dressed up in the language of merit.

A verdict written before the firing — Hegseth's 2024 book as a hit list

Before Pete Hegseth ever set foot in the Pentagon as Secretary of Defense, he had publicly identified Admiral Lisa Franchetti as a problem. In his 2024 book, he singled her out as emblematic of the Biden administration's prioritization of diversity over competence — arguing that her appointment as Chief of Naval Operations was driven by gender, not qualifications.

That pre-existing verdict made her firing all but inevitable once Hegseth took office. As Greg Jaffe reports in The Daily, the official spokesmen for Hegseth offered public justifications for the decision — but none of those justifications held up to scrutiny when measured against Franchetti's actual record.

Franchetti had been confirmed by the Senate 95 to 1, with overwhelming bipartisan support. That near-unanimous vote reflected a broad, cross-party assessment of her fitness for the role — making the post-hoc framing of her removal as a merit-based decision particularly difficult to sustain.

A 26-year military reporter says this has no precedent

The scale of what happened to Franchetti is not an isolated case. According to Jaffe's reporting — detailed in full in this episode of The Daily — Hegseth has fired more than a dozen generals and admirals since taking over, with between 65 and 70 people total either fired or blocked from promotions they had already earned. That represents approximately 10 percent of the entire general officer corps.

Jaffe, who has covered the U.S. military since 1999, says he cannot think of anything remotely close to this in his 26 years on the beat. The purge is, by his account, genuinely unprecedented in modern American military history.

One data point captures the ideological dimension of what is happening: as of 2025–2026, women make up approximately 21 percent of the U.S. Navy, yet no women are being nominated to admiral rank — for the first time in at least a decade. Franchetti's firing sits at the center of that pattern, explored further in the full conversation with Greg Jaffe.

"When you start firing people for political reasons, it's not just the person you fire, but it's the message that sends throughout the entire force that if you want to move up, there are political litmus tests that you have to pass through."

Who is Lisa Franchetti and how did she rise to become Chief of Naval Operations?

Lisa Franchetti grew up in Rochester, New York, attended Northwestern University, and joined the Navy after being recruited on campus. She rose through the ranks over decades — including commanding the USS Ross and leading U.S. Fleet Forces Command — to become one of the most senior women in the history of the U.S. Navy.
