Why is the politicization of military promotions considered dangerous for national security?

August 29, 2026 The Daily · Greg Jaffe

← See the The Daily fiche

When military promotions are decided on political grounds rather than merit, it sends a message throughout the entire force that advancement requires passing political litmus tests instead of demonstrating military capability. Officers then make critical wartime decisions based on what will please their superiors rather than what sound military judgment demands, directly undermining combat effectiveness and national security.

The danger runs deeper than individual cases. When officers know that loyalty to a political agenda matters more than honest military advice, they stop offering unvarnished assessments—even when those assessments are unpopular or contradict their leadership's preferred course of action. In warfare, commanders need subordinates who will tell them hard truths, not those who tell them what they want to hear.

As Greg Jaffe explains in his reporting for The Daily, the scale of this problem has become unprecedented in recent military history. Since Pete Hegseth took office as Secretary of Defense, approximately 65 to 70 senior officers have been fired or blocked from promotions—roughly 10 percent of the entire general officer corps of admirals and generals combined.

"When you start firing people for political reasons, it's not just the person you fire, but it's the message that sends throughout the entire force that if you want to move up, there are political litmus tests that you have to pass through."

The human cost compounds the institutional damage. Officers who have spent decades building expertise and earning promotion through performance now face removal for failing to align with political ideology rather than military standards. This creates a chilling effect: rising officers learn that technical skill and strategic thinking matter less than ideological conformity.

Detailed in this episode of The Daily, the case studies show how this plays out in practice. Experienced commanders are sidelined or fired, while younger officers who demonstrate the "right" political views move up faster. The military's institutional memory and expertise erode as a result, weakening its capacity to conduct complex operations and adapt to genuine threats.

Sound warfighting depends on promoting the best officers, not the most politically aligned ones. When that principle breaks down, the force's ability to protect national interests deteriorates from within.
