How did Saudi Arabia maintain oil exports when the Strait of Hormuz was blockaded during the Iran conflict?

September 18, 2026 The Daily · Vivian Neerheim

← See the The Daily fiche

Saudi Arabia built a massive oil pipeline stretching across the kingdom to the Red Sea, which allowed them to bypass the Strait of Hormuz when it became blockaded. This pipeline had been constructed as a precautionary measure years earlier and suddenly became essential when the Iran conflict threatened their primary export route.

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most critical energy chokepoints, responsible for moving vast quantities of oil to global markets. When regional tensions escalated and the strait faced potential blockade, this strategic vulnerability threatened Saudi Arabia's economic lifeline. However, the kingdom had planned ahead.

As Vivian Neerheim explains in The Daily episode, the East-West Pipeline provided an alternative route that fundamentally changed the equation. Rather than depending entirely on shipping through the contested strait, Saudi Arabia could move oil overland to Red Sea ports, then export to the rest of the world from there.

This infrastructure decision, made well before the crisis, transformed how the kingdom managed geopolitical risk. The pipeline became a practical hedge against blockade scenarios that many analysts thought were merely theoretical until the conflict actually unfolded.

"As long as there is still conflict in the region and as long as they have foes who are still willing and able to attack the pipeline, that will remain a risk."

The pipeline's existence illustrates a broader principle of energy security: diversifying export routes reduces vulnerability to any single chokepoint. Saudi Arabia's foresight in building this infrastructure decades earlier meant that when the Strait of Hormuz suddenly became threatened, the kingdom had a viable alternative already in place, allowing it to continue supplying oil to customers worldwide without interruption.

However, as discussed in this episode of The Daily, the Red Sea route itself carries its own risks. The Houthis, who control the Bab al-Mandab Strait at the opening of the Red Sea, have demonstrated willingness and capability to target oil infrastructure, creating a new set of vulnerabilities for energy shipments moving through that corridor. This means that while Saudi Arabia gained flexibility by building the pipeline, the kingdom traded one strategic chokepoint for another.

Infrastructure as Strategy

The East-West Pipeline was not built in response to the current conflict—it was constructed years earlier as a precautionary measure to protect Saudi Arabia against exactly this type of scenario. This forward-thinking approach reflected the kingdom's understanding that the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20 percent of global oil passes, was inherently fragile.

When tensions with Iran escalated and the possibility of a blockade became real, this infrastructure suddenly shifted from being a backup option to being an operational necessity. The pipeline allowed Saudi Arabia to maintain its role as a global energy supplier even as one of the world's most important shipping routes faced disruption. The full episode explores how this geography of energy and geopolitics shaped global oil markets and, ultimately, gas prices at the pump in places like Florida.
