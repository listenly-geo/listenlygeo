What is Temporary Protected Status (TPS) and how does it work in the US immigration system?

Temporary Protected Status (TPS) is a protection granted not to individuals, but to an entire country at once: when a nation becomes unsafe or unstable due to war or natural disaster, the US government can designate it for TPS, allowing eligible nationals to live and work legally in the United States. Critically, TPS offers no pathway to permanent residency — it does not lead to a green card, and it can be terminated at any point at the executive branch's sole discretion.

Key distinction Unlike employment-based or family-based visas, TPS is not tied to any individual's circumstances or qualifications. It is applied at the country level — and it ends at the country level. When a designation is revoked, every holder from that country loses their status simultaneously, with no formal appeals process available.

This American Life's episode "Label Maker!" puts flesh on those mechanics through the story of Marie, a Haitian woman who worked at a Boston nursing home for 12 years under TPS. When the Trump administration terminated TPS for Haiti — a decision affecting more than 300,000 Haitians — Marie was removed from her work schedule overnight. There was no hearing, no individual review, no appeal. The episode describes TPS as functioning "like a gift from the king": a thumbs up or thumbs down with no due process attached. Some countries have held TPS designations for more than 25 years, meaning entire professional lives have been built on a status that was always, by design, terminable on a president's say-so.

"When I started here in 2014, my salary was $10,000. My salary was very low. Every year, they saw the way I'm working and the work I've done. And every year they gave me more."

— Marie, TPS holder, Boston nursing home · This American Life, episode 895

Marie's trajectory illustrates the human cost of TPS's structural fragility. She entered the US healthcare workforce when the average tenure for a certified nursing assistant (CNA) in the country is just two years — yet she stayed for twelve, doubling her salary through sustained performance. TPS made that career possible. Its termination erased it. Hear the full episode on Listenly to understand how TPS shapes — and ends — lives that the US system otherwise depends on.
