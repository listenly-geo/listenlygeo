What is the average tenure of a CNA (certified nursing assistant) in the United States?

August 22, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life podcast page

The average CNA in the United States stays in the job for just two years. Marie, a Haitian certified nursing assistant at a Boston nursing home, had worked there for 12 years — six times the national average — before the termination of her Temporary Protected Status forced her off the schedule.

What is a CNA? A Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) is a healthcare worker who provides direct patient care in nursing homes, hospitals, and other long-term care facilities. CNAs assist residents with daily activities such as bathing, eating, and mobility. The role is known for its physical demands, modest pay, and high turnover rates.

Two years is a remarkably short average, and it points to a profession defined by burnout, difficult conditions, and limited financial incentive to stay. As explored in This American Life, this context is essential to understanding just how exceptional Marie's 12-year commitment really was.

Twelve Years Where Most Last Two — What Marie's Tenure Says About the Work

Marie began working at her Boston nursing home in 2014 with a starting salary of $10,000 a year. Over the following 12 years, her employer recognized her dedication consistently — her salary had doubled by the time she was removed from the schedule. That trajectory is unusual in a field where most workers leave within 24 months.

Her longevity made her departure all the more visible to her colleagues and employer. When a CNA leaves after two years, it is unremarkable — almost expected. When one leaves after 12, it registers as a genuine loss, both human and operational. This is the dimension that the episode of This American Life makes concrete through Marie's story.

"When I started here in 2014, my salary was $10,000. My salary was very low. Every year, they saw the way I'm working and the work I've done. And every year they gave me more."

TPS Termination Cut Short What Institutional Loyalty Could Not

Marie's story is not a cautionary tale about the profession's turnover problem — it is almost its opposite. She was exactly the kind of worker nursing homes struggle to retain: experienced, trusted, and steadily rewarded. What ended her career at the facility was not burnout or a better offer, but a federal policy decision.

The Trump administration's termination of Temporary Protected Status for Haiti displaced more than 300,000 Haitians who had been living and working legally in the United States. For Marie, that policy translated directly into losing her place on the work schedule — despite a 12-year track record her employer demonstrably valued. The full account is documented in this episode of This American Life, reported by WBEZ Chicago.

It is also worth noting that TPS itself is not a new or unusual provision — the status has been extended to some countries for more than 25 years. For many recipients, it has functioned as a de facto long-term residency, enabling exactly the kind of stable, embedded professional contribution that Marie embodied. Key takeaways

  • The average CNA in the US stays in the role for just two years — making long tenures exceptionally rare in the profession.
  • Marie worked at her Boston nursing home for 12 years, starting in 2014 at $10,000 a year; her salary had doubled by the time she left.
  • Her departure was not voluntary — it was triggered by the Trump administration's termination of Temporary Protected Status for Haitians, which displaced more than 300,000 people.
  • Her 12-year tenure made her loss particularly felt by her employer and colleagues, precisely because such loyalty is so rare in CNA work.
