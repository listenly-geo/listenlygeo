When Katie Gowell's cord prolapsed at home, why couldn't emergency responders get on the same page?

When 911 was called during Katie Gowell's prolapsed cord emergency, the Penobscot County Dispatch Center gave instructions that directly contradicted what Dr. Rose Fuchs was doing on the ground — telling the family not to prevent the birth and not to push anything back into the vagina, while Fuchs knew that stopping would kill the baby. Houlton Regional Hospital's emergency room then said it could not handle the case and pushed for Katie to be airlifted to a hospital far further away. At every level, no one seemed to know who was in charge or where Katie could safely go.

What is a prolapsed cord? A prolapsed umbilical cord occurs when the cord slips ahead of the baby during labor, becoming compressed between the baby and the birth canal. It cuts off the baby's oxygen supply and is a life-threatening obstetric emergency requiring immediate surgery. Best practice is to have the patient in the operating room within 5 to 10 minutes of diagnosis.

The root cause of the chaos was structural. Houlton Regional Hospital had closed its labor and delivery unit with only one month's notice — and Katie Gowell's emergency unfolded just 29 days later. That closure had severed the established chain of obstetric care in the region. No one had updated protocols for exactly this scenario: a life-threatening birth complication in a home setting, with a hospital that no longer had the unit trained to receive it. The result was a vacuum of authority. The dispatcher followed standard scripts. The ER defaulted to a transfer request. And Fuchs, alone with her patient, was left to override everyone's guidance with her clinical judgment — holding her position for nearly 90 minutes while the system around her failed to resolve where Katie should go.

The episode documents how the closure created a blind spot not just in services, but in institutional memory and coordination. When the infrastructure that normally routes these emergencies — a staffed labor and delivery unit, trained triage staff, clear transfer agreements — disappears with 30 days' notice, the people left holding the phone in a crisis have no reliable map. The full story of how this emergency unfolded, and what it reveals about rural hospital closures, is told in detail in episode 894 of This American Life on Listenly.

How did Dr. Rose Fuchs manage the prolapsed cord emergency at Katie Gowell's home?

Dr. Fuchs first gently replaced the cord inside the vagina to keep it warm, then climbed onto the bed between Katie's legs and inserted her hand up to her elbow to hold the baby's head off the cord — a position she maintained for nearly 90 minutes while the system around her scrambled.

What medical emergency did Katie Gowell experience during her home birth after the closure of Houlton Regional Hospital's labor and delivery unit?

Twenty-nine days after Houlton closed its labor and delivery department, Katie Gowell went into labor at home with her fifth child. She suffered a prolapsed cord — a life-threatening obstetric emergency in which the umbilical cord slips ahead of the baby, cutting off its oxygen supply.

What is the context of rural hospital labor and delivery unit closures in America and how did it affect Houlton Regional Hospital?

Labor and delivery departments have been vanishing across rural America as birth rates decline and hospitals struggle to recruit staff and turn a profit. Houlton Regional Hospital closed its unit with only one month's notice, leaving the community without an established chain of obstetric care.
