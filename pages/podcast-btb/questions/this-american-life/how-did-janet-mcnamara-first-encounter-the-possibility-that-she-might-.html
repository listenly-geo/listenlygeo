How did Janet McNamara first encounter the possibility that she might be autistic?

August 23, 2026 This American Life

← Voir la fiche This American Life

Janet McNamara, a comedian performing in Boston, was approached after a show by Dick Dougherty, the owner of Dick Dougherty's Comedy Vault. He noticed she looked up and darted her eyes when thinking, and asked whether she had ever been tested for autism. No one had ever raised the possibility with her before that moment.

An unexpected question from an unlikely source — a comedy club owner, not a clinician

The observation came entirely unsolicited. Dougherty was not a doctor or a psychologist — he was a comedy club owner. Yet he noticed something specific in McNamara's behavior: the way her eyes moved when she was thinking. That single physical detail was enough to prompt the question that would reframe her understanding of herself.

This kind of late recognition is a pattern that surfaces repeatedly in stories about autism, particularly among women. The suggestion didn't come from a school counselor, a family member, or a therapist — it came backstage at a comedy venue in Boston, from someone who simply paid close attention. As explored in this episode of This American Life, that moment of being truly seen by a stranger carried real weight.

McNamara had no prior frame for it — the question landed in a vacuum

What makes the encounter striking is not just the source, but the absence of anything that came before it. McNamara explicitly said that no one had ever suggested autism to her prior to Dougherty's question. No teacher, no doctor, no family member — nobody.

The moment Dougherty asked is recounted in the "Label Maker!" episode of This American Life as a pivot point — the kind of offhand observation that reorganizes everything that came before it. A comedian performing on stage, a club owner watching from the back of the room, and a question that had apparently never been asked before.

The reference to Asperger's is part of the broader context around this conversation, connecting McNamara's story to a larger cultural reckoning with how autism — particularly what was once labeled Asperger's — goes undiagnosed in adults for decades. The story is part of why This American Life consistently chooses subjects that reframe ordinary experience through unexpected details.
