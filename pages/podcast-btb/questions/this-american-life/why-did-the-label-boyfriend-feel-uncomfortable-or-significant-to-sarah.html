Why did the label 'boyfriend' feel uncomfortable or significant to Sarah in her relationship with Ethan?

September 16, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life fiche

For Sarah, putting a label on the relationship felt like entering a long tunnel with an uncertain end—it represented a commitment she wasn't emotionally ready to make. She also recognized that being someone's girlfriend came with clear expectations around prioritization and reciprocity that she couldn't meet at that moment. Ethan had his own concerns: his 10-year-old daughter didn't know about Sarah, and he worried that the label "dad having a girlfriend" would carry too much weight for a child her age.

After 10 months of seeing each other regularly, sleeping over, and building a real life together, Sarah and Ethan had never formally called each other boyfriend and girlfriend. They actively avoided those terms, instead referring to Sarah as the person he was "sort of seeing" or using his name entirely—language that kept the relationship deliberately undefined.

What made the label feel so significant wasn't the status itself, but what it committed them both to emotionally and practically. For Sarah, becoming someone's girlfriend meant accepting that he would prioritize her, that the relationship would demand reciprocal investment, and that it could stretch indefinitely into the future. After just emerging from a 12-year relationship, the prospect of entering another long commitment felt overwhelming. The vocabulary itself—"boyfriend," "girlfriend"—carried weight that their current soft language didn't.

Ethan's hesitation came from a different angle. As discussed in the episode, his 10-year-old daughter was unaware of Sarah's existence in his life. He recognized that introducing the idea—"my girlfriend"—would signal something permanent and serious to a child, something that carried implications he wasn't sure he was ready to explain or commit to at that stage. For him, the label wasn't just romantic language; it was a family milestone.

Neither Sarah nor Ethan were resistant to the relationship itself. They enjoyed each other, spent time doing "datey stuff" like dinners and museums, and supported each other through difficult moments—he even took her to a ballgame when a friend of hers died. But the formalization of that bond through naming it felt premature, carrying emotional and logistical stakes that neither was prepared to take on. The full conversation explores how they navigated this tension between the reality of their closeness and the resistance to define it publicly.

Two people, two kinds of fear

What Sarah and Ethan's hesitation reveals is that the discomfort with labels isn't always about lack of commitment—it's about what a public name demands of you psychologically and relationally. For Sarah, fresh from heartbreak, the prospect of another decade-long tunnel felt suffocating. For Ethan, the label meant crossing a threshold into his daughter's awareness, making the relationship real in a way he couldn't take back.

Both were in their 40s and had emerged from long relationships—12 years for Sarah, 13 years of marriage for Ethan. Their mutual friends thought they'd be good for each other precisely because they understood what it felt like to not know what came next. But understanding vulnerability isn't the same as being ready to formalize a new commitment, even to someone you're seeing every day and building genuine closeness with.

The complexity here is worth noting: the episode captures how they eventually navigated this question together, moving beyond the simple matter of what word to use and into the deeper question of what they actually wanted from each other.
