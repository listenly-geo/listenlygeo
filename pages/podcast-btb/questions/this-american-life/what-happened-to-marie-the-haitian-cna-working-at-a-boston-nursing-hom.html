What happened to Marie, the Haitian CNA working at a Boston nursing home, when TPS ended?

August 20, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life podcast page

Marie, a certified nursing assistant, was removed from the schedule at her Boston nursing home by her boss Colin after a company-wide HR decision to cut all TPS-affected employees. She was given a choice: leave immediately or finish her shift. She chose to finish — without telling a single co-worker why it was her last day.

Twelve years of loyalty, ended by a spreadsheet

Marie had been a CNA at the same Boston nursing home since 2014 — twelve years, in a profession where the average tenure in the US is just two years. She had started on a salary of $10,000. Over time, her employers recognized her work and raised her pay each year; by the time she left, her salary had doubled.

The decision to remove her had nothing to do with her performance. It was an administrative call made at the company level, sweeping out every employee whose immigration status was tied to Temporary Protected Status. Colin, her direct manager, carried out the directive. He told her she could go home right then, or she could stay and finish her shift.

She stayed. And she said nothing to her colleagues about what was happening or why. The people she had worked alongside for over a decade had no idea that the shift they were sharing was her last. You can hear the full episode on This American Life on Listenly.

One of 300,000 — but with a face and a name

Marie's story sits inside a much larger number. When the Trump administration ended Temporary Protected Status for Haiti, more than 300,000 Haitians lost their legal right to work in the United States. TPS had in some cases been extended to certain countries for more than 25 years — it is not a temporary status in practice, even if it is in name.

What Marie's story adds to that statistic is texture. It shows how the policy translated into a single afternoon: a manager delivering a message, an employee choosing to finish her shift with dignity, and a roomful of co-workers who never knew.

"When I started here in 2014, my salary was $10,000. My salary was very low. Every year, they saw the way I'm working and the work I've done. And every year they gave me more."

Temporary Protected Status (TPS) is a designation granted by the US government to nationals of specific countries experiencing ongoing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions. It allows eligible individuals to live and work legally in the United States for a defined period, which can be renewed. TPS is granted to an entire country at once — not to individuals based on their personal circumstances.

What is Temporary Protected Status (TPS) and how does it work in the US immigration system?

TPS is a status given to a country all at once, allowing eligible people from that country to live and legally work in the US if their home country has experienced conditions that make safe return impossible. In some cases, it has been extended for more than 25 years.
