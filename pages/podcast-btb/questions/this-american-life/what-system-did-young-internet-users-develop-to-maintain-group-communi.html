How did young internet users build a secret communication system after their podcast was removed?

September 25, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life fiche

When a girl's podcast that played TikTok videos for kids was banned from Spotify, a group of around 20 kids—some as young as 11 years old—developed an ingenious workaround: they created public playlists with names like "Let's Chat Here" or "New Chat," placed a single podcast episode inside, and used the comment section as their hidden group chat. Other members would find the playlist, locate the episode, and post comments to communicate with each other.

The system worked because podcast comment threads weren't yet monitored as closely as traditional social media. A 14-year-old member of the group named Ella explained how it unfolded: people would simply make playlists specifically designed to host their conversations, creating a de facto chat room disguised as harmless podcast content.

How the discovery revealed a generational blind spot

The group's communication method went unnoticed for some time until Dave Blanchard, who runs the public radio show Wild Card, spotted unusual activity in the comment section on Spotify. What he initially saw looked like pure gibberish—clipped, incoherent postings from different accounts that he assumed were bot activity. Comments like "ah, OK, MH" and "Gilgamesh quit and I'm crying" made no sense to him.

Blanchard reported the suspected bot activity to Spotify and escalated it within NPR. That's when a younger colleague entered the conversation. Hannah Chin, a Gen Z employee at NPR who was 10 years younger than Blanchard, immediately recognized what he had missed: this wasn't bot behavior at all—it was middle schoolers using comment threads as a covert group chat. The seemingly nonsensical posts were actually shorthand and coded language that made perfect sense to the group using it.

As Blanchard reflected on the moment: "I literally needed Hannah to be my generational translator. It was such a clarifying realization of my age and obsolescence." What appeared as suspicious technical glitches to a millennial turned out to be deliberate, creative communication by kids who simply needed a way to stay connected after their original platform closed.

Why podcast comments became the perfect hiding place

The group didn't randomly choose NPR podcasts specifically for their chat system. Ella revealed that they selected shows that already had very few comments, making it easier for their activity to blend in unnoticed. Podcast platforms, unlike TikTok or Instagram, weren't designed with heavy moderation or algorithmic ranking of comments, giving the group an ideal space to operate under the radar.

Key takeaways

  • A group of roughly 20 young internet users, including children as young as 11, created playlists with single podcast episodes to use comment sections as private group chats.
  • The system worked because podcast comment sections were less monitored and had slower algorithmic visibility than mainstream social platforms.
  • Adults initially mistook the coded comments for bot activity, unable to decipher the shorthand language the group used naturally.
  • A generational gap made the difference: a Gen Z colleague recognized the pattern instantly, while millennial staff saw only noise.
