What did a sister discover about her relationship with her sibling through examining text messages after death?

September 19, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life fiche

After her sister Sasha died, Nadia discovered a text message her sister had sent to a friend the night of a fight about drug use that revealed a devastating truth: "How do I not hate my sister? My family truly hates me." This hidden message shattered Nadia's understanding of what her sister had been feeling, and she realized she had failed to respond to what turned out to be Sasha's last text asking about a childhood memory.

The discovery came through a painful examination of her sister's final days. Text messages became the primary window into Sasha's inner life, exposing a struggle that had been invisible to those around her. Nadia had not known the depth of Sasha's pain or desperation, and this gap between what she thought she knew and what the messages revealed forced a reckoning with their relationship.

What made the discovery even more harrowing was the timing—Sasha's last text to Nadia was a simple question about a shared childhood memory, something that now felt impossibly distant. Nadia never responded to that final message, a silence that would echo through the years following her sister's death on July 31, 2015. The text about hating her family and struggling with her identity had been sent just hours before Sasha died from a heroin and fentanyl overdose.

As the episode reveals, Nadia spent years after her sister's death reconstructing these moments through digital traces—the words Sasha had typed but never fully shared, the conversations that existed in a space between them. The messages became evidence of a deeper connection and a deeper distance, all at once.

"You don't want that to be the last text she ever sends you. She may need you. And you need to let her know that you love her."

The weight of unanswered messages carries a particular kind of grief. The final text Sasha sent was a request for connection rooted in memory, something as ordinary as it was crucial. It was a moment when presence and response might have mattered, when Nadia might have said: I'm here, I care, I want to understand what you're going through. That she didn't know she had the chance, that she didn't know Sasha was in such a dark place, compounds the loss.
