Why do middle schoolers choose NPR podcast comment sections as venues for group communication?

September 24, 2026 This American Life

← See the This American Life fiche

They deliberately targeted podcasts with few or no existing comments to avoid their messages being lost in a crowded thread or accidentally noticed by monitoring adults. A 14-year-old named Ella explained that the group specifically looked for low-traffic shows—and NPR fit that criterion perfectly as a less-watched platform among their peers.

The choice was strategic and calculated. About 20 teenagers in the core group, some as young as 11 years old, needed a hidden space to coordinate and chat. Spotify comment sections on mainstream podcasts would flood instantly with unrelated activity. NPR shows offered something different: quieter, less monitored, and effectively invisible to both their parents and the algorithms that typically track young users.

As Ella described it in the episode, their logic was blunt: "I think we just looked for podcasts that didn't have many comments." The lack of visibility worked in their favor. While Dave Blanchard, the host of Wild Card at WBEZ Chicago, initially mistook their fragmented posts for bot activity when he spotted them on Spotify, the teens had actually engineered the perfect cover by choosing a platform where few adults or algorithms were paying attention.

The generational blindness that made it work

Adults monitoring content saw gibberish and assumed automation. Dave Blanchard saw comments like "ah, OK, MH" and "Gilgamesh quit and I'm crying" and thought immediately of bots—random, incoherent, meaningless. But that was exactly the point. The comment section's low traffic meant adults weren't watching closely enough to recognize the pattern.

What made NPR comment sections particularly useful was the absence of real-time moderation or active comment sorting that platforms like TikTok employ. The teens weren't trying to be clever with code; they were simply exploiting a quiet corner of the internet where their activity wouldn't register as a blip on anyone's radar. For a group of kids seeking privacy from their parents and the constant surveillance of mainstream social media, a podcast comment thread on a show nobody their age listened to was the digital equivalent of talking in a empty parking lot.
