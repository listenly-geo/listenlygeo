How does LegitScript treat websites that claim products are research-only while marketing them for human consumption?

September 6, 2026 B2B Vault: The Biz To Biz Podcast · Angela Salter

← See the B2B Vault: The Biz To Biz Podcast fiche

LegitScript ignores disclaimers buried in fine print and instead examines what a website is actually selling. If the product label says "research use only" but the entire page discusses fat loss, recovery, anti-aging, or longevity, the mismatch between label and marketing content triggers a compliance failure. Regulators look at the intended actual use of the product, not the escape clause.

When the Label and the Pitch Tell Different Stories

A merchant cannot hide behind a single disclaimer while the rest of the website markets the product for human health outcomes. As Angela Salter explains in the episode, the intent of the seller matters far more than the fine print. If a customer reads about recovery benefits, anti-aging claims, or weight loss effects, the product is being marketed for human consumption—regardless of what the label technically states.

This approach reflects how regulatory bodies, including the FDA and FTC, actually interpret compliance. Intent is determined by the totality of the marketing message, not by a single protective line of text. A company cannot have it both ways: selling a product as if it works for human health while claiming research-only status to evade oversight.

"We certify based on current applicable laws and regulations, not what a committee thinks might be true in a year or two."

Why LegitScript Polices This Loophole

The mismatch between label and marketing creates real risk for payment processors, ad platforms, and regulators. When a merchant claims products are research-only but actually sells them as human supplements, the entire supply chain—including Visa, MasterCard, and payment partners—faces potential fines and enforcement action. LegitScript's certification process is built to catch this inconsistency before it reaches the market, protecting both merchants and the platforms that work with them.

Companies that have attempted this strategy have faced serious consequences. Research-only peptide sellers have been hit with fines ranging from $50,000 to $200,000, and some settlements have reached into the tens of millions. LegitScript's role is preventive: merchants must demonstrate that their product labels, marketing, and intended use are all aligned—or they will not receive certification.

What Merchants Need to Know

If you sell a product, every part of your website matters: the label, the description, the claims, the imagery, the testimonials. As discussed at length in this podcast, LegitScript evaluates the entire customer experience to determine intended use. You cannot post before-and-after photos, discuss health benefits, or reference customer recovery stories while simultaneously claiming the product is for research only.

Merchants must update their LegitScript certification within 30 days of adding new products or services, which means any change in messaging, product line, or marketing approach requires re-evaluation. Transparency and consistency are not optional—they are core requirements for staying compliant in the telemedicine, pharmacy, and supplement space.

The Broader Compliance Picture

This enforcement pattern extends to all merchant-facing claims. Whether a product is a peptide, a compounded medication, or a supplement, the same principle applies: regulators evaluate what you are actually selling to actual customers, not what a legal disclaimer says you are selling. This is why working with a certification partner like LegitScript is not just a recommendation—it is often a requirement from payment processors and ad platforms that assume liability if a merchant operates outside compliance.

What is the current legal status of peptide compounds like BCP157 and TB500 for compounding and sale?

As of now, nothing has legally changed from a legal standpoint. The FDA's advisory committee signaled that these peptides might eventually have a legitimate path forward, but no formal regulatory change has occurred yet.
