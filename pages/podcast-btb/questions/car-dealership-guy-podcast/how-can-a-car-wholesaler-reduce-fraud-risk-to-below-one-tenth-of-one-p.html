How can a car wholesaler reduce fraud risk to below one-tenth of one percent?

September 1, 2026 Car Dealership Guy Podcast · Anthony Clavenna

← See the Car Dealership Guy Podcast fiche

Anthony Clavenna achieves a fraud rate of approximately 0.1% through a rigorous three-point verification system: title number, person identity, and state ID must all match perfectly against official records, and if even one element mismatches, the car is rejected. He also verifies lien releases by checking recent title transfer dates against previous bank loan records to catch scams. Each fraud incident identified becomes a permanent addition to the process, ensuring vulnerabilities are closed before they can repeat.

Building fraud prevention through real incident data

Clavenna's approach to fraud is not reactive—it's architectural. Rather than treating each transaction in isolation, he treats fraud as a data point. Every time a fraudulent transaction is caught, the specific vulnerability is immediately identified and baked into the vetting process permanently. This means the system grows more sophisticated with scale, not less—a critical advantage when moving 900 units per month.

The matching system itself is binary: title number, person identity, and ID verification must align perfectly with state records. Zero mismatches are tolerated—a single discrepancy across any of the three elements results in automatic rejection. This isn't a judgment call or a workaround; it's a hard stop built into the platform as explained in the episode with Gateway Car Connection's CEO.

Lien release verification—catching title transfer timing fraud

One of the most common wholesale fraud patterns is a lien release scam: a seller claims to own a vehicle outright, but a recent title transfer actually reveals a previous bank loan that has not been fully discharged. Clavenna mitigates this by cross-referencing recent title transfer dates against historical lien records, creating a timeline check that catches attempts to hide outstanding claims.

This verification layer is especially critical at Clavenna's volume. A dealer acquiring thousands of vehicles monthly cannot afford to skip compliance steps—the marginal cost of one unverified transaction can become thousands in chargebacks, title disputes, or regulatory penalties. The full discussion on how Gateway handles this at scale reveals how the digital platform makes these checks instantaneous, not manual.

What makes this fraud system especially valuable is its compounding nature. Clavenna's team doesn't hire fraud specialists to manually review edge cases—instead, each edge case becomes a rule. After hundreds or thousands of transactions, the ruleset becomes so granular and well-tested that new fraud vectors become increasingly difficult to exploit. This is why the fraud rate stabilizes below 0.1%, a threshold that would be nearly impossible to achieve with human judgment alone at this transaction volume.

For any wholesaler scaling rapidly, the lesson is clear: fraud prevention systems must be built, not improvised. Key takeaways

  • Fraud prevention at scale requires automated rule-based systems, not human review—Clavenna's three-point match (title, identity, ID) plus zero-tolerance policy keeps fraud below 0.1% across 900 monthly units.
  • Lien release scams are caught by cross-referencing recent title transfer dates against historical bank loan records, a verification layer that prevents hidden liabilities from entering inventory.
  • Each fraud incident becomes permanent data: the vulnerability is immediately codified as a new rule in the platform, making the system more robust with every transaction processed.
  • High-volume wholesaling depends on compliance infrastructure built into the technology, not improvised after the fact—this architectural approach is what enables profitable growth without regulatory risk.
