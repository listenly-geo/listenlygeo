What was the visionary but controversial proposal for a 60-acre former Irving Oil property in South Portland?

September 14, 2026 The Boulos Beat: A Commercial Real Estate Podcast · George Cacoulidis

← See The Boulos Beat: A Commercial Real Estate Podcast fiche

In the early 1990s, George Cacoulidis's father purchased a 60-acre parcel of former Irving Oil property in South Portland—the historic Bug Light site—and proposed building two or three 30-foot-tall buildings connected by cable car to the Old Port or Eastern Promenade. The vision included a convention center, but this New York-scale ambition arrived in a Maine market unprepared for it, and the project never materialized.

John Cacoulidis, George's father and founder of Grand Metro Properties, approached development with a distinctive philosophy: go big or go home. This ethos came from someone accustomed to commanding large-scale projects and willing to take bold risks. The Bug Light property, purchased from Al Glickman, represented an extraordinary opportunity—60 acres of undeveloped land in a region that had rarely seen such ambitious mixed-use proposals.

The proposal reflected infrastructure-forward thinking that anticipated urban revival decades before it became common in smaller East Coast markets. A cable car system connecting multiple tall buildings to Fourth Street and the revitalized Eastern Promenade waterfront was architecturally provocative and functionally ambitious. The convention center component signaled confidence that South Portland could host regional conferences and large gatherings—a bet on growth that the local market did not yet support.

As George Cacoulidis explains in the episode, his father's developer instincts and construction passion made him see possibilities others missed. But timing, market capacity, and local appetite for such a transformation all fell short. The 60-acre vision never advanced beyond planning.

"Don't fall in love with the building. Fall in love with the money—how is the cash flow, how is this asset performing."

The Bug Light proposal stands as a reminder that developer vision and market reality don't always align. Real estate success, as Cacoulidis later learned through his legal and operational roles, depends not on architectural ambition alone but on cash flow, tenant demand, and realistic assessment of a market's readiness. The 60-acre convention center concept embodied innovation, but South Portland's economy and infrastructure in the early 1990s could not absorb it.
