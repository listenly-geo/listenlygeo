Why does historic preservation matter for a city's economy and quality of life?

September 6, 2026 The Boulos Beat: A Commercial Real Estate Podcast · Julie Gondzar

← See the The Boulos Beat: A Commercial Real Estate Podcast fiche

Preservation brings in people, keeps the economy strong, and attracts businesses and residents. Cities with distinctive character and historic architecture become destinations—people want to visit and live there, while the working waterfront and historic districts drive tourism and business activity. If a city loses its unique character, it loses people.

Portland exemplifies this principle. The city's historic neighborhoods and waterfront create an economic engine that draws both visitors and new residents. As Julie Gondzar, Director of Development at Greater Portland Landmarks, explains in the podcast, preservation is not simply about saving old buildings—it's about maintaining the identity that makes a place worth living in.

The relationship works in both directions. When neighborhoods retain their historic character, property values remain stable and often increase. Businesses thrive because they operate in walkable, distinctive areas where customers actually want to spend time. This creates a virtuous cycle: stronger local economies support the resources needed to maintain historic properties, while well-preserved neighborhoods attract further investment.

Urban renewal in the 1960s and 70s removed thousands of homes from neighborhoods like Bayside and Munjoy Hill, fragmenting communities and erasing the character that made those areas special. Greater Portland Landmarks was founded in 1964 following the devastating 1961 demolition of Union Station, a turning point that showed how quickly a city can lose its landmarks and the sense of place they embody.

"Success ultimately comes from collaboration. I believe that if you want a good result at the end of the day, collaboration is key."

The economic argument for preservation is straightforward: heritage buildings and districts generate sustained economic activity through tourism, local business, and residential appeal. But the quality-of-life dimension is equally important. People choose to live in Portland because of its maritime history, walkable streets, and buildings that tell stories—elements that would disappear if preservation efforts slackened. Without intentional stewardship, even thriving historic neighborhoods can erode, taking their economic benefits with them.

What is the role and public access situation of the Portland Observatory on Munjoy Hill?

Greater Portland Landmarks is the steward of the Portland Observatory, which is owned by the City of Portland. The observatory is open May through October, serving thousands of visitors every year and functioning as both a historic landmark and an active community asset.

How should preservation organizations balance advocacy with collaboration when engaging developers on historic buildings?

Greater Portland Landmarks has shifted from being confrontational to being proactive and collaborative. The organization now seeks to get a seat at the table early in development conversations to find solutions that honor both preservation and economic viability.

What was the historical catalyst for the founding of Greater Portland Landmarks as an organization?

Greater Portland Landmarks was founded in 1964 following the 1961 demolition of Union Station on St. John Street. The passionate group of people who fought to preserve Portland's historic character created the organization to prevent further loss of the city's architectural heritage.
