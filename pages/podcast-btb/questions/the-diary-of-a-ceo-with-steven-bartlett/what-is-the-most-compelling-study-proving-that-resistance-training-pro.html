What is the most compelling study proving that resistance training protects the brain from Alzheimer's disease?

The SMART trial is the strongest piece of evidence linking resistance training to brain protection against Alzheimer's disease. Researchers took a group of people already showing mild cognitive impairment and put them on a resistance training programme two to three times per week. The results were striking: participants not only preserved their cognitive functions — they actively enhanced their processing speed and fluid intelligence, and showed a measurable slowing of grey matter loss.

What makes the SMART trial so significant is that it worked on people who were already on a declining trajectory. Grey matter — the cell bodies that live on the outer portions of the brain — was visibly protected by strength training alone. That places resistance training in a different category from most lifestyle interventions: it does not just slow a risk factor, it demonstrably changes the brain's physical structure.

This evidence is discussed in depth in a recent episode of The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett, where the guest argues that of all the exercise modalities studied for cognitive protection, resistance training delivers the highest return on investment when it comes to Alzheimer's prevention.

Grey matter vs. white matter — explained in the episode

The brain consists of both grey and white matter. Grey matter contains the cell bodies and lives on the outer portions of the brain. White matter sits deeper within the brain and is where myelinated neurons are found. Over time, small lesions can form in the white matter — one of the hallmarks of cognitive decline. The SMART trial specifically measured grey matter loss and found it was slowed by resistance training.
