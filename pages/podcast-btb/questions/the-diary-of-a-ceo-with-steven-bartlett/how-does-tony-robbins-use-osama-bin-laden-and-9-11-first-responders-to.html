How does Tony Robbins use Osama bin Laden and 9/11 first responders to illustrate how the same human need can produce radically different behaviors?

2026-09-08 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Tony Robbins

← See the The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett fiche

Both Osama bin Laden and the 9/11 first responders were driven by the same dominant human need—significance—yet their choices were radically opposite. The difference lies not in the need itself, but in the beliefs they held about how to fulfill it. Bin Laden, one of approximately 27 children who felt like a nobody, sent others to die to achieve importance. The firefighters and police officers who rushed into the Twin Towers, by contrast, believed that dying as a hero made life meaningful and chose to sacrifice themselves for strangers.

This contrast reveals a fundamental principle: any human need can be met in either constructive or destructive ways, depending entirely on what someone believes is the right path. As Robbins explores in the episode, the same emotional driver—the hunger for significance—can lead one person toward evil and another toward heroism.

Belief systems determine the expression of need

Robbins emphasizes that Bin Laden and the first responders shared the psychological root: both needed to feel significant in the world. The 9/11 first responders knew entering those buildings likely meant death, yet they went anyway. Their belief system told them that dying to save others would make them heroes, making their sacrifice meaningful. For them, significance came through contribution and sacrifice.

Bin Laden, raised as one of many children in an enormous family, felt worthless. He had access to his father's vast wealth, but money alone could not give him the significance he craved. So he built a different belief: that he could become historically important by orchestrating a massive attack—not by going himself, but by commanding others to die. Both paths came from the same need, but were separated by what each person believed significance actually looked like.

This mechanism underlies much of what Robbins discusses about human behavior. The need for significance is not inherently destructive; it is the belief system attached to that need that determines whether it becomes a source of heroism or atrocity.

"Any time you meet a belief, an emotion or a behavior meets at least three of these six needs, you will become addicted to that thought, that feeling or that behavior."

Key takeaways

  • Osama bin Laden and 9/11 first responders both pursued significance, but via opposite beliefs about how to achieve it.
  • Bin Laden, one of approximately 27 children, felt insignificant and used his father's wealth to orchestrate attacks rather than risk himself.
  • First responders entered burning buildings knowing they might die, driven by the belief that heroic self-sacrifice creates meaning.
  • Any human need can be met constructively or destructively depending entirely on the belief system attached to it.

See also

Why does Tony Robbins argue that love and contribution are the highest-order needs and should be prioritized above significance and certainty?

Robbins explains that the first four needs — certainty, uncertainty, significance, and connection — can all be met in destructive ways and have no built-in moral structure, whereas love and contribution inherently align with human wellbeing.

What is Tony Robbins's explanation for why significance-seeking leads to unhappiness and social comparison?

Robbins states that social media has made significance disproportionate in modern culture and is why so many people are unhappy, because people now compare themselves directly to billionaires and celebrities rather than their immediate community.

How does Tony Robbins explain the rise of online violence, virtual signaling, and social media toxicity through the lens of human needs?

Robbins argues that social media enables people to meet their need for significance without effort or consequence, by tearing others down rather than building themselves up, creating a low-barrier path to feeling important.
