What lifestyle did Alex Honnold lead in his early 20s to 30s, and how did he describe his relationship with money and possessions?

August 24, 2026 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Alex Honnold

← See the full The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett podcast page

Alex Honnold lived in a van throughout his early 20s to 30s, spending his days reading books and climbing — no drinking, no partying. His lifestyle was so stripped back that teammates nicknamed him "the monk." A single North Face sponsorship, worth roughly $10,000 a year, was all he needed to sustain it.

"The Monk" — A Life Deliberately Reduced to Two Things

Honnold's asceticism was not circumstantial — it was a conscious choice. He had no interest in alcohol or the social rituals most people his age treated as default. On at least one climbing trip, fellow climbers started calling him "the monk," a nickname that stuck precisely because it fit so well.

His days had an almost monastic structure: climb, read, sleep in the van, repeat. As Honnold explains in The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett, this was not deprivation — it was clarity. When your needs are that small, you do not need much money, and you do not need much from the world beyond the next wall.

$10,000 a Year — and It Was Enough

His first major sponsorship through The North Face paid approximately $10,000 annually. For most people that figure would represent a severe constraint. For Honnold, living out of a van with no rent, no bar tabs, and no consumer habits to feed, it was sufficient — and then some.

This is a detail worth sitting with. The man who would go on to free solo El Capitan in Yosemite — a feat documented in the Oscar-winning Netflix film Free Solo — was building his entire foundation during years of radically low overhead. The sponsorship covered fuel, food, and gear. Nothing else was required. This context is explored further in this episode of the podcast.

It also points to something important about the economics of obsession. Honnold was not trading his time for money — he was engineering a life where money almost ceased to matter. The van was not a sacrifice; it was the vehicle (literally) that made five days a week of climbing, every week for decades, structurally possible.

"There's no hack. You just get really freaking scared over and over for so long. And eventually it's not that scary anymore. But that's a very enduring way to overcome your fear."

That quote — about fear, repetition, and the slow erosion of what once felt impossible — maps cleanly onto how Honnold approached his entire lifestyle. He did not avoid discomfort; he structured his life so that the only discomfort he faced was the one that made him better at climbing. Everything else was removed. Steven Bartlett draws this thread out clearly across the episode.

Key takeaways

  • Honnold lived in a van throughout his early 20s to 30s, by deliberate choice — not by necessity.
  • He neither drank nor partied, spending his time exclusively on reading and climbing, earning him the nickname "the monk."
  • His North Face sponsorship of roughly $10,000 per year was sufficient because his lifestyle required almost no money to sustain.
  • This radical reduction in needs enabled him to climb five days a week for decades — the foundation on which his free solos were built.
