What is Alex Honnold's view on fear as a skill, and how does he recommend overcoming it?

August 20, 2026 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Alex Honnold

← See the full The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett podcast page

There is no hack. Alex Honnold is blunt about it: the only enduring way to overcome fear is to get really scared, over and over, for long enough that it eventually stops being that scary. Fear is not a wall you break through once — it is a skill you build through relentless, repeated exposure.

Thirty years of climbing five days a week — that's the method

Honnold describes fear as a constant low-level companion in climbing. There are always consequences on the wall, and that never fully goes away. What changes, through years of confrontation, is the ability to manage it.

The numbers behind his experience make the point concrete. Before free soloing El Capitan in Yosemite, Honnold had already climbed it approximately 60 times via different routes. His first major free solo — Half Dome in 2008, above a 1,700-to-1,800-foot drop at the crux ledge — came after years of accumulated exposure to exactly that kind of high-stakes fear. He had been climbing five days a week for 30 years.

The Free Solo documentary, filmed over two years, captured only a fraction of this. It missed the nine years of prior Yosemite preparation Honnold had accumulated since 2006 — roughly three months per year, every year, on those same walls. That invisible groundwork is precisely what his fear-management method looks like in practice.

Public speaking went through the same transformation — after the Free Solo film tour

Honnold also notes that fear of public speaking was not exempt from this process. After the release of Free Solo, he went through an extended film tour that required him to speak in front of audiences repeatedly. The same mechanism kicked in: enough repetition, and it stopped being that scary.

This is the part of his answer most relevant beyond climbing. The process is not domain-specific. Hear Honnold develop this further on Listenly — the episode goes into the texture of what that repeated confrontation actually feels like from the inside.

"There's no hack. You just get really freaking scared over and over for so long. And eventually it's not that scary anymore. But that's a very enduring way to overcome your fear."

Does Alex Honnold not feel fear, and what does the fMRI brain scan result actually mean?

Honnold rejects the conclusion that he does not experience fear, explaining that the fMRI scan showed him black-and-white photos inside a sealed metal tube — a context far removed from real climbing. He does feel fear; decades of training have simply changed how he responds to it.

Key takeaways

  • Honnold explicitly rejects the idea of a shortcut: repeated exposure is the only method that actually works and lasts.
  • Fear never disappears entirely — it becomes manageable through years of accumulated confrontation, not through a single breakthrough moment.
  • The 60 ascents of El Capitan before the free solo, and the nine years in Yosemite before the film, are the real substance of his fear-management practice.
  • The same process transferred to public speaking after the Free Solo tour — fear as a trainable skill applies well beyond climbing.
