Why does social media make significance-seeking more unhappy and more destructive?

6 September 2026 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Tony Robbins

← See the The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett fiche

Social media has made significance disproportionate in modern culture because people now compare themselves not to neighbors or friends but to the world's 3,000 billionaires or doctored influencer images — creating impossible standards. The pattern Robbins observes among the biggest names in Hollywood and sports is that seeking significance paradoxically destroys it: the more you push your significance on others, the less significant they make you.

This disconnect between the pursuit of significance and actual happiness stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of how impact works. When someone believes that being recognized, admired, or elevated above others will create love and connection, they discover the opposite — they face attack and rejection. As Robbins explores in depth in the podcast, this dynamic plays out at the highest levels of entertainment and professional sports.

The problem intensifies because social media removes the natural limits that used to constrain comparison. Historically, you measured yourself against the people in your immediate environment — your neighborhood, your workplace, your friend group. Today, every ordinary person has access to curated highlight reels of billionaires and influencers, creating a psychological gap that cannot be closed by normal achievement. There are only approximately 3,000 billionaires in the world, yet millions of people are now in daily visual competition with them.

What makes this worse is that the images people see are often fabricated. Influencers stage gym visits for content they won't actually use to work out; they present doctored versions of their lives designed for maximum engagement. When real humans compare their authentic selves to these constructed personas, unhappiness is inevitable. The full episode dives deeper into how social media has rewired the human need for significance, exploring why this shift happened and what it costs us psychologically.

The real cost of significance-seeking on social media is that it inverts the mechanism for actual achievement. Instead of building something, creating value, or connecting authentically with others, people focus on appearing significant. Robbins also discusses how Osama bin Laden was reportedly one of approximately 27 children, which he cites as the origin of extreme significance-seeking behavior — a historical example of how the hunger for recognition can drive destructive patterns at scale.

The antidote, according to Robbins, is understanding that real significance comes from contribution, not consumption or status display. When you shift from trying to make yourself look significant to actually being significant through helping others and creating real value, the social comparison trap loses its power. This is why the most fulfilled people in his research were those driven by contribution and love, not by needing the world to recognize their importance.

Key takeaways

  • Social media has replaced natural peer comparison with comparison to billionaires and fabricated influencer images, creating impossible standards.
  • The more people push their significance on others, the less significant they become — a paradox that plays out visibly among Hollywood and sports figures.
  • Only 3,000 billionaires exist globally, yet millions now compete against them daily on social platforms.
  • Real significance comes from contribution and building value, not from status display or seeking recognition.
