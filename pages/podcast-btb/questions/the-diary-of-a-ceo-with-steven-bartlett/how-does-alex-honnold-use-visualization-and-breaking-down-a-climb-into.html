How does Alex Honnold use visualization and breaking down a climb into pieces as a preparation strategy, and can this apply to other challenges?

August 23, 2026 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Alex Honnold

← See the full The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett podcast page

Before any free solo attempt, Alex Honnold ropes up and scouts every individual section of the climb, checking each transition between distinct segments to understand exactly how each piece feels. He frames this explicitly as a universal principle: break any major challenge into pieces, work on those pieces separately, and the whole thing starts to feel manageable. For Taipei 101, he flew out in September for a dedicated scouting trip — four months before the January climb — inspecting each architecturally distinct section on its own.

Scouting Taipei 101 Section by Section — Four Months Out

Honnold's preparation for Taipei 101 is a precise illustration of the method. He did not simply show up and attempt the structure. He made a full preparatory trip in September, well before the January climb, to inspect each architecturally distinct section of the building separately.

Each part of the structure presented its own unique challenges — different surfaces, different transitions, different physical demands. By isolating each segment during scouting, Honnold could understand what each piece required before ever stringing them together. This is exactly the same logic he applies to big walls like El Capitan in Yosemite, which he has climbed approximately 60 times across different routes — a volume of experience that made the free solo feel earned rather than improvised. You can hear Honnold walk through this approach in detail on The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett.

The Piece-by-Piece Principle: From Rock Faces to Any Big Project

Honnold is explicit that this is not just a climbing technique. He frames it as a general mental framework for approaching anything that looks overwhelming at first glance. The logic is straightforward: the challenge feels impossible when viewed as a single monolithic object. Break it into discrete, workable pieces — and it stops being impossible.

He draws a direct parallel to how he approached El Capitan before the Free Solo documentary was ever made. The film covers two years of preparation, but it missed nine years of prior work in Yosemite — years of climbing El Capitan repeatedly, section by section, route by route, since 2006. The free solo was not a sudden leap. It was the accumulated result of thousands of individual pieces, each worked separately over time.

This is also why Honnold dismisses the idea of a single breakthrough or shortcut. As he explains in this episode, the process is deliberate and cumulative — not dramatic. You work the pieces until the whole stops feeling unreasonable.

"There's no hack. You just get really freaking scared over and over for so long. And eventually it's not that scary anymore. But that's a very enduring way to overcome your fear."

The quote underlines what makes the piece-by-piece approach work in practice: it is not about eliminating discomfort upfront. It is about repeated, deliberate exposure to each piece — until that piece no longer triggers the same level of fear. The method and the mindset are the same thing, applied together.
