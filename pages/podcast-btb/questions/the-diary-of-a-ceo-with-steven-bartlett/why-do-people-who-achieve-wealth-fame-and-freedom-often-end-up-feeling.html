Why do people who achieve wealth, fame, and freedom often end up feeling depressed or empty?

August 28, 2026 The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett · Tony Robbins

← See the full podcast page: The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett

According to Tony Robbins, achieving money, fame, and total freedom isn't enough — because none of those things provide lasting fulfillment on their own. The real cause of the emptiness is the absence of two specific needs: growth and contribution. Without them, every other achievement becomes hollow, no matter how impressive it looks from the outside.

Robbins makes this point with striking clarity in The Diary Of A CEO with Steven Bartlett. He isn't talking in abstractions — he draws directly from his own experience with people who had reached the very top.

He cites friends who sold their companies for over a billion dollars. They were happy for approximately one month. Then they wanted back in. Not because they needed the money — because without growth and contribution, the win meant nothing.

The slot machine that loses its thrill — what happens when life becomes too predictable

Robbins uses a precise analogy to explain the mechanism. Imagine pulling a slot machine lever a hundred times, and every single time it produces the same predictable outcome. No tension, no surprise, no possibility of loss. The result isn't satisfaction — it's boredom. Certainty, at its extreme, destroys the very experience it was supposed to protect.

This is what happens when someone accumulates enough wealth, fame, or freedom to eliminate all uncertainty from their life. The other needs — certainty, significance, connection — don't disappear. They just become mechanical. They stop being sources of meaning and start feeling like obligations, like jobs. As Robbins explains in this episode, without growth and contribution, even the greatest external achievements become empty rituals.

He lists the full range of what people typically chase: money, sex, notoriety, Academy Awards. His point is that none of it holds. Hollywood provides the clearest evidence — people who reach the absolute peak of cultural recognition and still describe their lives as empty. The pattern is consistent enough that Robbins treats it as a rule, not an exception.

Growth and contribution — the two needs that make the others worth having

Robbins is explicit about the hierarchy. Growth and contribution are different in kind from the other four needs. They are the only two that, when absent, cause the remaining needs to collapse in meaning. This isn't a philosophical opinion — it's a framework Robbins has built from decades of direct observation.

He spent most of his life driven by contribution first. He performed on stage for 13 hours at a time and stayed until 1:30 or 2 in the morning to sign autographs for every single attendee — before smartphones made the process faster through photos. That wasn't a marketing strategy. It was a response to what he identifies as the deepest human driver: the need to matter to someone else's life.

To build the frameworks behind this thinking, Robbins interviewed 50 of the world's greatest financial minds — including Ray Dalio, Carl Icahn, and Warren Buffett — all of whom started with nothing and became billionaires. What they shared wasn't a love of money. It was an obsession with growth and with contributing something larger than themselves. This conversation, explored at length in The Diary Of A CEO, makes the case that the pattern holds across radically different lives and contexts.

The six human needs (Robbins's framework): Certainty, uncertainty/variety, significance, connection and love, growth, and contribution. The first four are needs of the personality. Growth and contribution are needs of the spirit — and the only two that give the first four their meaning. A belief, emotion, or behavior that meets at least three of the six becomes, in Robbins's words, an addiction.

"Any time you meet a belief, an emotion or a behavior meets at least three of these six needs, you will become addicted to that thought, that feeling or that behavior."

Tony Robbins identifies six core human needs: certainty, uncertainty (variety), significance, connection and love, growth, and contribution. Key takeaways

  • Achieving wealth, fame, or freedom doesn't cause emptiness — stopping growth and contribution does.
  • Friends of Robbins who sold companies for over a billion dollars reported happiness lasting roughly one month before needing to re-engage.
  • When growth and contribution are absent, the remaining four human needs become mechanical — sources of boredom rather than meaning.
  • Robbins treats this not as a philosophical observation but as a consistent, observable pattern confirmed across his interviews with 50 billionaires including Ray Dalio, Carl Icahn, and Warren Buffett.
