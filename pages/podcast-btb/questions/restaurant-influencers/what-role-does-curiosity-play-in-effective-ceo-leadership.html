How Does Curiosity Shape Better CEO Decision-Making?

September 21, 2026 Restaurant Influencers · Alistair Darby

← See the Restaurant Influencers fiche

Curiosity is the foundation of sound leadership because it drives genuine inquiry beyond surface-level answers, uncovering what guests and stakeholders truly need rather than what they claim. Without curiosity, leaders default to assumptions that inevitably prove incorrect, leading to flawed decisions in operations and strategy.

Alistair Darby's mentor taught him that natural curiosity was the single most valuable trait to recruit for and develop in leadership roles. This isn't idle wondering—it's the deliberate practice of looking at a situation, narrowing your eyes, and asking: "Is that really the case?"

This approach matters especially in hospitality, where guest expectations shift constantly and operational complexity hides beneath surface-level metrics. As Darby explains in the episode, understanding what customers truly want requires going beyond surveys and direct feedback to observe actual behavior and patterns.

Curiosity Versus Assumption in Real Operations

Many leaders fall into the trap of accepting answers at face value because it's faster and feels certain. A guest might tell you they're happy, a manager might report strong metrics, a team might claim a process is working—but curiosity demands a second look.

Darby's mentor understood that the best leaders ask follow-up questions, dig into why something is happening, and resist the urge to move forward until the real picture emerges. This approach prevents costly mistakes, from product misalignment to operational inefficiencies that compound over months or years.

The hospitality industry amplifies this principle because guest satisfaction depends on unstated expectations. What a guest says they want during a survey often differs from what their behavior reveals about their actual preferences. A detailed exploration of this dynamic is discussed further in how customer data reveals what guests actually want versus what they say.

Beyond the boardroom, Darby has learned this lesson through real operations. For example, discovering that breakfast trade in German bars represented 30 percent of daily revenue came only because he was curious enough to investigate why those bars succeeded—a finding that transformed how he thinks about revenue opportunities. This curiosity-driven discovery is detailed in what business lesson emerged from studying breakfast buffet success in German bars.

Curiosity also shapes how a CEO relates to their role and team. Rather than taking a quarterback position barking orders from above, a curious CEO becomes a linebacker—asking why frontline staff do what they do, understanding the constraints they face, and learning directly from operations. This mindset shift is explored further in how a CEO's role should differ in hospitality organizations.

Key takeaways

  • Curious leaders ask "Is that really the case?" instead of accepting surface-level answers, which leads to better decisions and fewer costly mistakes.
  • In hospitality and service industries, true guest needs are often hidden and require active inquiry beyond what customers state directly.
  • Curiosity-driven CEOs position themselves as enablers and investigators within their organizations rather than distant decision-makers, learning directly from operations.
  • Observing actual behavior and patterns—not just listening to claims—reveals the insights that drive competitive advantage and operational improvement.

How does customer data reveal what guests actually want versus what they say?

Darby explains that when you ask a guest or customer a question directly, you will not get the proper answer—you get the instant answer. True customer wants are revealed through data patterns and behavior, not what people claim they want.

What business lesson emerged from studying breakfast buffet success in German bars?

Darby recounts meeting Bernd Rieger, an ex-hotelier running successful all-day bars in Germany where breakfast represented 30 percent of daily trade. This discovery revealed how a dedicated morning offering can become a major revenue driver and competitive advantage.

How should a CEO's role differ in hospitality organizations?

Darby rejects the quarterback analogy for a CEO, arguing instead that a CEO's job is to be the linebacker. The quarterback is the person serving a drink—the frontline staff—while the CEO supports and enables them from behind.
