How did Elizabeth I manage to reward Drake without openly provoking Spain?

Elizabeth I handled the situation through deliberate duplicity. She officially ordered Francis Drake to register his treasure and send it to the Tower of London, while secretly allowing him to keep £10,000 for himself and permitting his crew to retain £14,000. She publicly declared that Drake had no charge to answer and dismissed Spanish allegations as outright lies — all while quietly benefiting from the haul herself.

The stakes were enormous. Drake had returned from his circumnavigation of the globe aboard the Golden Hind with gold, silver, and goods valued at approximately £400,000 — a sum exceeding Elizabeth I's entire annual income. Acknowledging the seizure openly would have amounted to an act of war against Philip II of Spain, who in January 1580 had become king of Portugal as well, uniting the whole of Iberia and both empires into a single formidable power. Elizabeth could not afford that confrontation directly. Instead, she engineered a careful fiction of official distance.

Part of the performance included genuine concessions: Elizabeth licensed £30,000 to be returned to Spanish merchants who could prove legitimate claims against Drake in London. But the larger settlement was handled off the books. Drake secretly donated £100,000 directly to Elizabeth — recorded nowhere publicly — while the remainder of the treasure stayed unacknowledged. The culmination of this balancing act came on 1 April 1581, when Elizabeth staged Drake's knighting ceremony aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford. It was a calculated signal: honour without admission, reward without accountability. What role did Bernardino de Mendoza play in the dispute over Drake's treasure?

Bernardino de Mendoza, Philip II's ambassador to Elizabeth's court, was described as a menacing, zealously Catholic figure who actively worked to subvert Elizabeth's rule and pressed Spanish claims over the treasure Drake had seized.

Which members of Elizabeth I's Privy Council supported Francis Drake and why?

The hardline Protestant or Puritan wing of the Privy Council — including Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester), Francis Walsingham, and Sir Christopher Hatton — supported Drake, seeing him as a weapon against Catholic Spain and a champion of English Protestant interests.
