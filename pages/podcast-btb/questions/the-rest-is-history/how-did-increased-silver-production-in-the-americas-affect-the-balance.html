How did increased silver production in the Americas shift the balance of power between Spain and Protestant England?

Francis Drake discovered during his circumnavigation that technological advances in South American mines were producing a rapidly growing flow of silver back to Spain. Philip II of Spain was channelling this wealth to fund his armies in the Low Countries against Protestant rebels and to sustain his broader cold war against Protestant England. This gave Elizabeth I the reasoning — and the political cover — to treat captured Spanish silver not as plunder but as a legitimate instrument of strategic retaliation.

The strategic weight of that silver becomes clearer when you consider what Philip II had at his disposal. By January 1580, he had become king of Portugal as well as Spain, uniting the whole of Iberia and both empires under a single crown. Across the sea from England, he maintained Alexander Farnese, Prince of Parma, with a battle-hardened army of 30,000 soldiers stationed in the Low Countries. American silver was the engine keeping that military machine running — and Drake had seen exactly how that engine worked.

£400,000 Total value of treasure Drake returned with — more than Elizabeth I's entire annual income
30,000 Soldiers in Parma's Low Countries army, funded in part by Spanish-American silver

Elizabeth I's response was characteristically calculated. On 1 April 1581, she staged Drake's knighting ceremony aboard the Golden Hind at Deptford — a public signal that England was not merely tolerating the raids on Spanish silver but endorsing them. Rather than returning the full haul, Elizabeth quietly kept £100,000 for herself (recorded off the books), permitted Drake to keep £10,000 and distribute £14,000 among his crew, and returned only £30,000 to Spanish merchants with legitimate claims. The rest disappeared into the crown's finances, repurposed as a counter-weight to the very silver funding Spanish aggression. Why was the Duke of Guise a threat to both Elizabeth I and Mary Queen of Scots' enemies?

The Duke of Guise was cousin to Mary, Queen of Scots, which meant he could realistically aspire to the thrones of England and Scotland: if he deposed Elizabeth I, Mary's claim would flow directly through him.

Who were the 'Three Henries' (Trois Henri) in France during the late sixteenth century, and what did each represent?

The three factions in France were each led by a man named Henri: Henri of Navarre led the Huguenots and would eventually become Henri IV; Henri, Duke of Guise led the Catholic League; and Henri III was the reigning king caught between them.

What was the significance of Elizabeth I knighting Francis Drake on the Golden Hind at Deptford on 1 April 1581?

Knighting Drake was a bold and deliberately theatrical public statement, as Elizabeth was famously stingy with knighthoods and Drake was the grandson of a farmer — making the ceremony a pointed message directed at Philip II of Spain.
