What was the 1935 Belfast rioting and how serious was it?

17 September 2026 The Rest Is History

← See The Rest Is History fiche

The 1935 Belfast riots erupted into full-scale street violence with gun battles, snipers positioned on rooftops, and British troops deploying armored cars to restore order. Eleven people were killed, around 100 injured, and approximately 2,000 residents were driven from their homes.

Armed violence and military intervention

The rioting in 1935 was not a spontaneous outbreak of mob disorder—it was organized, sustained conflict that required heavy military intervention. British troops moved through Belfast's streets in armored vehicles, indicating the scale of weaponry involved. Snipers took position on buildings, creating a battlefield environment in the middle of a major city. This level of armed engagement reflected the depth of sectarian tension that had festered in Belfast since partition in 1921.

The casualty figures underscore the gravity of the violence. With eleven deaths and a hundred injuries, the 1935 riots represented a significant loss of life concentrated within a brief period of unrest. Yet the human toll extended far beyond those numbers: the displacement of 2,000 people from their homes meant families fled neighborhoods, businesses were abandoned, and entire streets became contested territory.

Sectarian reprisals and religious targets

The rioting in Belfast triggered a wave of retaliatory violence that crossed the Irish border. Protestant homes were attacked, and Church of Ireland churches were burned in the Irish Republic as retaliation for the violence in the north. This pattern of counter-attack showed how deeply the 1935 riots were rooted in sectarian identity rather than a localized dispute. Violence in one city sparked organized reprisals hundreds of miles away, revealing the interconnected nature of Irish and Northern Irish religious conflict.

As the episode details, the 1935 riots were part of a longer pattern of sectarian violence in Belfast that would intensify dramatically in the coming decades, ultimately leading to the systematic bloodshed of the Troubles.

What was Sir James Craig's position on Northern Ireland as a Protestant state?

Sir James Craig, the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland, stated explicitly in the 1930s that he envisioned Northern Ireland as a Protestant state, drawing a parallel to the Irish Free State's Catholic identity.

What was the IRA's S-Plan bombing campaign in Britain in 1939?

In January 1939, the IRA launched a sabotage campaign called the S-Plan against Britain, planting bombs in major cities including London, Birmingham, Bristol, and Liverpool as part of a broader strategy to destabilize British rule.

What violence accompanied the early years of Northern Ireland between 1920 and 1922?

Between the summer of 1920 and the summer of 1922, intense street fighting between the IRA and security forces, especially in Belfast, resulted in more than 500 deaths, 10,000 Catholics driven from jobs, and 25,000 Catholics driven from their homes.
