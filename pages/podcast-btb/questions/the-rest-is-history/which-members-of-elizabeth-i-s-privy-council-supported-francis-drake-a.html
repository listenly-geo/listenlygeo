Which members of Elizabeth I's Privy Council supported Francis Drake, and what drove them to back him?

The hardline Protestant — or Puritan — wing of the Privy Council backed Francis Drake without hesitation. Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester), Francis Walsingham, and Sir Christopher Hatton all sided with Drake, driven by a potent combination of ideological conviction and direct financial self-interest: all three had invested in his expedition and stood to profit enormously if the treasure was kept.

Their support was inseparable from the broader religious fault lines running through Elizabeth I's court. Leicester, Walsingham, and Hatton occupied the Protestant hard-line of the Privy Council — men who viewed Drake's raids on Spanish possessions as strikes against Catholic power rather than mere piracy. The £400,000 Drake returned with aboard the Golden Hind — a haul that exceeded Elizabeth's entire annual income — was not abstract politics for these men: it was money they had personally put at risk. Supporting Drake meant protecting their own returns.

The episode, part of The Rest Is History's extended series on Elizabeth I and the Catholic threat, makes clear that these two motivations were genuinely inseparable. Backing Drake was at once a Protestant statement against Philip II of Spain — whose empire had grown to encompass Portugal and its territories after January 1580 — and a straightforward act of financial self-preservation. What was the Privy Council?

Elizabeth I's Privy Council was her inner circle of senior advisers, responsible for the day-to-day governance of England. Its members held enormous influence over royal decisions — including how to handle politically explosive situations like Drake's return laden with Spanish treasure.

"A war is easily begun, but not so soon ended."

— Quoted in Episode 694, The Rest Is History

That tension between starting something and controlling its consequences defined the entire Drake affair. Elizabeth herself faced pressure from Spain's ambassador Bernardino de Mendoza to return the treasure and punish Drake, while Alexander Farnese, Prince of Parma, commanded a battle-trained army of 30,000 across the sea in the Low Countries. The Protestant councillors who backed Drake understood the stakes — and calculated that solidarity with him outweighed the diplomatic cost.

How much treasure did Francis Drake bring back from his circumnavigation, and how did it compare to Elizabeth I's income?

Drake returned with gold, silver, and goods totalling approximately £400,000 — exceeding Elizabeth I's entire annual income. Elizabeth secretly allowed Drake to keep £10,000 for himself and distributed £14,000 among his crew, while £100,000 was donated directly to her, off the books.

Why was Francis Drake anxious when he returned to England in September 1580 after circumnavigating the globe?

Drake was deeply worried about Elizabeth I's reaction to his return because he was persona non grata in Spain following his raids on Spanish territories, and there was real uncertainty about whether the Queen would receive him as a hero or hand him over to appease Philip II.
