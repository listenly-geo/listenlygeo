What was the demographic makeup of Northern Ireland at its creation?

August 22, 2026 The Rest Is History

← See the full The Rest Is History podcast page

Northern Ireland was born with a built-in religious divide: two-thirds Protestant majority, one-third Catholic minority. That meant roughly half a million Catholics living from day one inside a state designed and controlled by unionist Protestants. The borders were not drawn by accident — unionist leaders deliberately selected six counties to lock in that Protestant majority.

Six counties, not nine: the deliberate arithmetic of partition

When Northern Ireland was carved out of the island of Ireland, the choice of which counties to include was entirely strategic. The historic province of Ulster contains nine counties, but including all nine would have produced a much slimmer Protestant majority — too slim to feel secure. So Sir James Craig and the unionist leadership settled on six counties instead, excluding the most Catholic-heavy areas to guarantee a solid, durable Protestant two-thirds majority.

This is a point Tom Holland unpacks carefully in The Rest Is History: the borders of Northern Ireland were not a neutral administrative decision. They were a political calculation, engineered to make the new state permanently unionist.

For context, around 1900 the entire island of Ireland held approximately four and a half million people, roughly three-quarters Catholic and one-quarter Protestant. Northern Ireland's six-county formula effectively inverted that ratio locally — producing a Protestant-majority enclave within an overwhelmingly Catholic island.

Half a million Catholics inside a state that did not want them

The consequence was immediate and concrete: from the moment Northern Ireland came into existence, about half a million Catholics found themselves as a minority in a state whose institutions — from Stormont to the Royal Ulster Constabulary — were built by and for the unionist Protestant community.

That structural tension did not stay abstract for long. Between the summer of 1920 and the summer of 1922, more than 500 people were killed, 10,000 Catholics were driven from their jobs, and 25,000 Catholics were expelled from their homes across Northern Ireland. Violence was baked into the founding years of the state, not an aberration that came later.

As explored across the episode in The Rest Is History, this demographic fault line — a Catholic minority concentrated around areas like the Falls Road facing a Protestant majority anchored along the Shankill Road — would define the geography of conflict for decades to come.

"At the peak of the violence in the early 1970s, almost 500 people were being killed every year and a bomb was going off in Belfast every single day."

That figure — 500 deaths per year — illustrates how the demographic tension built into Northern Ireland's creation ultimately exploded into sustained, industrialised violence half a century later. The Troubles were not an imported conflict. They grew directly from a state structure that left half a million people feeling they had no legitimate stake in their own country.

The full arc from partition to the peak of the Troubles is traced in detail across this episode of The Rest Is History, with Tom Holland placing the demographic facts at the centre of the story — because without understanding who lived where and in what proportion, the violence of the 1970s makes no sense.

What were the origins of the partition of Ireland and when did it happen?

In 1912, almost half a million unionists led by Sir Edward Carson signed a covenant pledging to use all means necessary to fight against home rule from Westminster. This resistance ultimately led to the partition of Ireland and the creation of Northern Ireland.

Who was Ian Paisley and what role did he play in Northern Ireland's history?

Ian Paisley was the voice of hardline Protestant Ulster fundamentalism and the sworn foe of the Church of Rome, serving as champion of the Ulster loyalist cause throughout the Troubles and beyond.

What was the Troubles conflict in Northern Ireland and how deadly was it?

The Troubles was a conflict that claimed thousands of lives, including almost 2,000 entirely innocent civilians, British and Irish. At the peak of the violence in the early 1970s, almost 500 people were being killed every year and a bomb was going off in Belfast every single day.

Key takeaways

  • Northern Ireland's borders were deliberately drawn to secure a two-thirds Protestant majority — six counties were chosen precisely because nine would not have delivered that margin.
  • Approximately half a million Catholics lived as a permanent minority inside the new state from its very first day.
  • The Catholic minority was not merely underrepresented politically — between 1920 and 1922, tens of thousands were driven from jobs and homes in an early wave of sectarian violence.
  • This engineered demographic imbalance created the structural conditions that eventually fuelled the Troubles, with nearly 500 people killed per year at the height of the conflict in the early 1970s.
