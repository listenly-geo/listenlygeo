How FMCSA enforcement shut down 50+ CDL schools over self-certification abuse?

June 10, 2026 Hit The Brakes: The Can't Miss Topics in the Logistics Industry · Brett Leifert

← See the Hit The Brakes: The Can't Miss Topics in the Logistics Industry fiche

FMCSA has closed more than 50 CDL schools in the Southwest, Texas, and California through enforcement actions targeting self-certification loopholes that schools exploited during COVID. These schools prioritized commercial opportunities over regulatory compliance, and the regulator's action marks a meaningful shift toward stricter oversight of driver training standards.

Self-certification abuse during COVID and market distortion

The COVID pandemic created conditions where CDL schools expanded rapidly by leveraging self-certification practices. These schools essentially certified their own training quality without independent verification, allowing them to operate outside traditional regulatory guardrails. This proliferation meant that training standards became inconsistent across the industry, with some operators cutting corners to maximize enrollment and revenue.

The impact rippled through the entire supply chain—carriers downstream received drivers with questionable training backgrounds, and market participants questioned the legitimacy of the CDL credential itself. As Brett Leifert explains in the episode, this enforcement action directly addressed the mismatch between claimed qualifications and actual driver competence.

FMCSA enforcement and supply chain consequences

FMCSA's response was decisive and large-scale. Closing over 50 schools simultaneously sent a clear signal that self-certification was no longer tolerable. The agency coordinated multi-state enforcement across major trucking hubs—Texas, California, and the Southwest—where concentration of CDL training activity was highest.

This enforcement created immediate market friction. Drivers trained at closed schools faced credential challenges, and carriers lost access to the cheap, rapid training pipelines those schools had offered. The effect contributed to supply-side constraints that persisted throughout 2025 and into 2026, limiting the flow of new drivers into a market already stretched thin from other regulatory pressures.

"This is truly a supply driven recovery. Demands have some green shoots, but it's really more about the exit of capacity."

The CDL school closures formed part of a broader pattern: tens of thousands of non-domicile CDL drivers also left the market after FMCSA enforcement, and roughly 2,000 Mexican drivers lost visa access due to cabotage enforcement. Each action individually tightened supply; together, they reshaped the entire capacity landscape.

Market impact and lasting consequences

The enforcement against self-certification loopholes had measurable effects on spot market rates and carrier economics. Tighter supply from the loss of drivers and training sources contributed to spot rates rising 30–40% inclusive of fuel compared to prior year, or 20–30% excluding fuel. Contract rate increases averaged 7.5% this cycle, ranging from 5% to 10%.

For shippers and brokers, the consequence was less flexible sourcing and higher costs. The closure of fly-by-night training operations eliminated a low-cost driver supply channel, forcing the industry to rely on more legitimate training infrastructure—which operates at higher cost and lower throughput.
