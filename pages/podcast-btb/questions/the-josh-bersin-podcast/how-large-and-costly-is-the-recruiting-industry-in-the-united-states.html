How large and costly is the recruiting industry in the United States?

August 17, 2026 The Josh Bersin Company

← See the full The Josh Bersin Company podcast page

Between 20% and 30% of Americans change jobs every year — even during the worst recessions. Hiring a single skilled employee costs anywhere from $5,000 to $10,000 or more, and bringing on a senior software engineer or executive means paying an outside recruiter nearly one-third to one-half of that person's first-year salary. Recruiting is not a niche HR function: it is one of the largest, most continuous, and most expensive operations in American business.

A market that never stops — because job movement never stops

The sheer volume of job transitions in the U.S. is what makes talent acquisition structurally unavoidable. Even in economic downturns, tens of millions of people are actively moving between roles every single year. This constant churn means companies are never done hiring — and never done spending.

As Josh Bersin breaks down in this episode of The Josh Bersin Company podcast, the scale of the market is visible everywhere: in the volume of job board advertising on platforms like Indeed, in the sheer size of HR departments, and in the fact that a very high percentage of HR roles — well more than half of LinkedIn's revenue — are tied directly to recruiting functions. The industry is not cyclical. It is permanent.

For younger workers, job-seeking is especially frequent in the early career years. For everyone else, it happens periodically — whether triggered by layoffs, career changes, company closures, or personal ambition. The demand side of recruiting never fully quiets down.

From hundreds of dollars to half a salary — the true cost of a hire

The cost of hiring is not uniform. For high-volume, low-skilled positions, it can be measured in the hundreds of dollars per hire. But for specialized technical roles or leadership positions, the numbers climb steeply.

Filling a highly skilled role typically runs $5,000 to $10,000 or more in direct hiring costs. And for senior software engineers or executives, companies relying on external executive recruiters should expect to pay close to one-third to one-half of the candidate's first-year salary as a placement fee. A $200,000 hire can carry a $70,000–$100,000 recruiting cost before day one.

This is a point that Josh Bersin — whose firm has conducted extensive benchmarking on talent acquisition — emphasizes clearly in The Josh Bersin Company podcast: the financial risk is not just the fee. A bad hire compounds the cost — wasted interview time, onboarding resources, and then the burden of managing or exiting someone who isn't working out.

Talent acquisition benchmarking — Josh Bersin's firm has tracked the per-hire cost across industries and job types for years. The range is wide by design: a $300 cost-per-hire at a fast-food chain and a $90,000 recruiter fee for a VP of Engineering are both real figures, from the same industry category.

Large companies have responded to this cost pressure by building substantial internal recruiting infrastructure — teams of hundreds of recruiters, many on contract, dedicated to sourcing, screening, scheduling interviews, conducting background checks, and managing offers. The apparatus is large because the stakes are large, as detailed throughout this episode.

"We have AI creating resumes and producing and sending them out and AI consuming them — slop talking to slop — and the recruiters constantly tell us they're getting so flooded with fraudulent resumes that they really can't sort through it all."

This observation — made in the context of AI's growing role in recruiting — underscores exactly why the cost of a bad hiring process is rising, not falling. The volume of noise is growing faster than the tools designed to filter it. The full analysis is available in The Josh Bersin Company podcast episode on AI-powered recruiting.

Key takeaways

  • 20–30% of Americans change jobs every year, making talent acquisition a permanent, not seasonal, industry.
  • Per-hire costs span from a few hundred dollars for high-volume roles to $10,000+ for skilled positions — and up to half a first-year salary for executive placements.
  • A wrong hire multiplies the cost: wasted process expenses stack on top of the downstream cost of managing underperformance.
  • Big companies run recruiting as a major internal operation, with hundreds of recruiters — many on contract — handling everything from sourcing to background checks.
