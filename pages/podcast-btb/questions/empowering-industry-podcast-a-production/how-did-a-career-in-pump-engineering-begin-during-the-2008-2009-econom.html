How did a career in pump engineering begin during the 2008-2009 economic crisis?

September 18, 2026 Empowering Industry Podcast - A Production of Empowering Pumps & Equipment · Glenn Powell

← See the Empowering Industry Podcast - A Production of Empowering Pumps & Equipment fiche

Glenn Powell entered pump engineering during the 2008-2009 financial collapse with a master's degree but no industry foothold, landing first as a CAD drafter at a manufacturing company. A colleague's referral led him to Hydro, which hired him despite his lack of pump expertise, providing the training and mentorship that launched a 13-year career.

The timing was brutal. Powell held advanced credentials but faced a saturated job market during one of the worst recessions in modern history. Rather than wait for a perfect fit, he took entry-level technical work—drafting assemblies, learning manufacturing processes from the ground up—a practical reset that kept him employed and building skills when many others were freezing their careers.

The turning point came through a personal connection. One of his colleagues at the manufacturing firm moved to Hydro and encouraged Powell to apply, vouching for his work ethic and potential. Hydro's hiring decision was unconventional—they brought in someone with no pump-specific background, betting instead on aptitude, coachability, and integrity.

"Your integrity and your honesty is kind of a limited resource, and you only ever have the chance to introduce yourself once in your career."

Once inside Hydro, Powell absorbed extensive training and mentorship, skills unavailable to him in that first manufacturing role. Over 13 years, he progressed from junior engineer to senior engineer, contributing to everything from pump reliability services to the company's global engineering initiatives. The company's willingness to take a chance on raw talent—rather than only hiring experienced pump engineers—gave him a trajectory that would have been impossible had he stayed unemployed or underemployed during the recession.

What makes this path remarkable is that it was neither inevitable nor easy. Powell didn't have connections in the pump industry, didn't land a prestigious internship, and didn't start with insider knowledge. What he had was a master's degree, adaptability, and the humility to start over in a junior role. The recession, while destructive for millions, forced him into a position where his early experience at the manufacturing company became genuine apprenticeship—practical knowledge that companies like Hydro value more than pedigree alone.

From CAD Drafting to Global Engineering: Building Expertise Through Opportunity

Hydro's decision to hire Powell as an unproven quantity paid off. The company invested in his development, exposing him to the reliability services group and pump test lab operations, where he learned industrial systems at scale—up to 5,000 horsepower equipment and 40,000 gallons per minute of discharge flow. These weren't shortcuts; they were earned credentials through real work.

Today, Powell works with Hydro's global engineering group on major customer projects. His career arc—from recession-era CAD drafter to senior engineer designing smart pump systems—reflects both personal resilience and an employer willing to develop talent rather than only buy it. In many ways, his story is a blueprint for anyone entering engineering during economic uncertainty: take the work available, prove your reliability, and let merit and relationships build from there.

  • A colleague's personal referral to Hydro was the catalyst; the company hired Powell on potential and character rather than pump industry experience.
