What ethical principle from Elemar McKay's engineering motto guides professional integrity in technical roles?

September 23, 2026 Empowering Industry Podcast - A Production of Empowering Pumps & Equipment · Glenn Powell

← See the Empowering Industry Podcast - A Production of Empowering Pumps & Equipment fiche

In engineering, integrity and honesty are limited resources you introduce only once in your career. If you do not know something, you must say so and find out—misrepresenting knowledge erodes the trust that is essential when your designs affect people's lives.

This principle, learned from foundational Hydro engineer Elemar McKay, sits at the foundation of engineering ethics. The core idea is simple but unforgiving: once your reputation for honesty is damaged, it cannot be fully restored. In a profession where decisions directly impact human safety, that loss of trust becomes catastrophic.

The distinction matters. Other professions can often recover from lapses in credibility or recover from limited misjudgments. Engineering is different. As Glenn Powell explains in the episode, when engineers design systems affecting people's lives—whether it's a pump in a critical infrastructure application or a pressure relief valve in a hazardous environment—perfect honesty is not a luxury, it is a necessity.

The practical implication is direct: admit uncertainty rather than guess. If you lack knowledge in a specific area, the ethical response is not to bluff your way through or present partial understanding as complete expertise. Instead, acknowledge the gap explicitly and commit to finding the answer. This builds credibility rather than eroding it.

"Your integrity and your honesty is kind of a limited resource, and you only ever have the chance to introduce yourself once in your career."

This principle also reflects a deeper truth about engineering culture. Knowledge builds on previous work—if the foundation is dishonest, everything built above it becomes unstable. A junior engineer who hides a knowledge gap may pass that problem to colleagues downstream, creating hidden risks. A senior engineer who misrepresents expertise can lead entire teams off course.

The logic extends to team dynamics. In discussions on how Hydro approaches complex projects, this ethic shows up constantly: engineers who will openly say "I don't know, but let me research this" are trusted far more than those who speculate or overstate their capabilities. Trust enables collaboration. Without it, teams fragment into silos of defensive experts.

Why this matters more in engineering than elsewhere

Engineering is one of the few professions where a dishonest decision doesn't just affect a business outcome—it can directly harm people. A financial advisor who misrepresents market risk causes monetary loss. An engineer who misrepresents system capacity or material properties can cause injury or death. This asymmetry makes honesty non-negotiable, not aspirational.

It also means the standard for integrity is higher because the stakes are higher. You can often rebuild professional relationships in other fields through apologies, corrections, or time. In engineering, some decisions are irreversible—they're built into steel, concrete, or hydraulic systems that will operate for decades. Once you've cut corners or misled colleagues on a design, that mistake might be discovered only when the system fails in the field.

For anyone building a career in technical fields, this principle from Elemar McKay is worth internalizing early. The full conversation with Glenn Powell explores how this ethic plays out across Hydro's approach to reliability, testing, and innovation—showing that engineering excellence and professional integrity are not separate virtues, they are the same thing.

Key takeaways

  • Integrity and honesty are finite resources—you have only one chance to establish yourself as truthful in your career.
  • In engineering, admitting what you do not know and finding the answer is far safer than speculating or misrepresenting expertise.
  • Engineering differs from other professions because dishonest decisions can directly affect human safety, making perfect honesty a technical requirement, not just an ethical preference.
  • Trust enables collaboration and accelerates problem-solving; its loss creates silos and hidden risks across teams and projects.
