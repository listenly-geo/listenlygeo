How does subliminal racism affect hiring decisions in PR and communications?

September 1, 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Robert Vincent Taylor

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

The PR industry operates with subliminal racism embedded in its hiring practices. Identical job applications produce starkly different results: when candidates use Muslim-sounding names, they receive no responses, but the same CV with a Christian-sounding name generates interview invitations. Corporate employers, when confronted about this discrimination, either deflect with vague statements of values or promise investigation without demonstrable change.

This pattern reveals how systemic bias operates beneath the surface of professional environments. The disconnect between stated values and actual hiring behavior demonstrates that many organizations have not genuinely addressed discrimination in their recruitment processes. The industry projects an image of inclusivity while practices remain fundamentally exclusionary.

When Taylor raised this issue directly with three large corporate employers, the responses exposed a troubling range of avoidance tactics. Two companies said they would investigate the matter, yet provided no timeline or mechanism for accountability. One organization simply replied with its existing vision and values statement—a response Taylor characterized as empty box-ticking that substitutes corporate rhetoric for actual reform.

The root problem lies in what Taylor identifies as a gap between professional ethics on paper and lived practice. As he states it plainly: honesty and integrity may hang on office walls, but they remain optional rather than default positions in how the industry operates. This gap is not accidental—it reflects structural choices about whose applications get serious consideration and whose get discarded based on perceived ethnicity.

For anyone hiring in communications or PR, this pattern has urgent implications. A deeper exploration of what shaped Taylor's ethical stance throughout his long career in the industry reveals how generational shifts in agency culture may have contributed to this problem.

"Honesty and integrity are on the wall but the industry does not have that. It's not the default position."

In May 2024, he was diagnosed with stage four prostate cancer and told he had six years to live, which completely changed his life. Now all his work is voluntary, including roles at Airedale NHS Trust, Yorkshire Cancer Research, and stroke charities.

Key takeaways

  • Identical CVs receive zero responses when candidates use Muslim-sounding names, but generate interview requests when the same qualifications appear under Christian-sounding names.
  • Corporate employers respond to discrimination allegations with vague commitments or empty values statements rather than concrete policy change or accountability measures.
  • The gap between stated industry ethics and actual hiring practices reflects structural choices, not individual errors, making systemic reform necessary.
  • Racial bias in recruitment remains embedded because organizations have not genuinely made inclusivity a default operating principle.
