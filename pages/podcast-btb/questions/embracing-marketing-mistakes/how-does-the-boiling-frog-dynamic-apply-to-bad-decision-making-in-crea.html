When do creative teams get trapped in a flawed concept they know is failing?

Published 22 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Once a creative team becomes deeply committed to a concept, moving backward feels harder than pushing forward—even when the work is fundamentally flawed. The agency gradually accepts small compromises and adjusts expectations until they convince both themselves and the client that it could work, when it shouldn't.

The incremental trap: how commitment locks in bad decisions

The boiling frog dynamic plays out in creative work the same way it does elsewhere. There are critical moments—single telling gestures—where a team could have stopped the process cold. But once the agency became steeped in the TV concept, they had already sunk time, energy, and professional identity into the execution. Carrying on felt less irksome than going back.

As Shakespeare wrote in Macbeth, and as Norton echoes in the episode discussion of creative failure, when you're "steeped so far in blood that to carry on is less irksome than to go back," the path forward becomes inevitable—regardless of its direction.

The team never sat down and consciously chose a doomed path. Instead, they accepted a series of reasonable-sounding compromises. A brief detail here, a creative adjustment there, a client revision they could work with. None of these moments felt like the breaking point. Together, they created one.

Why creative teams convince themselves—and their clients

What makes this trap particularly insidious is that creative professionals are skilled at rationalizing. Once committed, the team's job becomes defending what they've already sold, not questioning whether it was right to sell it in the first place. As explored in this conversation on marketing mistakes, the agency eventually persuades the client that a fundamentally broken concept could work.

By then, both sides are too invested. The client has briefed in stakeholders, the agency has allocated resources, and both have committed publicly to the direction. Walking away becomes the scariest option of all.

The boiling frog analogy: A frog placed in gradually heating water doesn't jump out because the temperature rises so slowly that the frog never perceives danger. In business decisions, teams fail to recognize risk when change happens incrementally rather than all at once. Each small compromise feels tolerable; the full picture only becomes clear when it's too late to reverse course.

The clearest signal to watch for is when a team stops asking "Is this right?" and starts asking "How do we make this work?" The question itself reveals the shift from evaluation to defense. And by that point, the frog is already warm.
