What are the warning signs that a creative brief concept will fail despite team enthusiasm?

21 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

The moment your team gets so invested in executing a specific format that they stop questioning whether the idea actually works in that format, you've already lost. Team enthusiasm can blind you to a fundamentally broken concept—even when everyone in the room is excited about it.

When the medium eclipses the idea itself

The Big Cheese Dipper case study crystallizes this perfectly. The brief mentioned TV as an option, and the agency interpreted it the way a dog hears its name—they only heard 'TV.' The creative team wrote a 60-second spot without stopping to verify that the product concept actually functioned in a television format.

The problem wasn't the creative execution or the team's commitment. The problem was that the idea's core logic depended on a specific one-upmanship escalation structure that doesn't translate to a linear 60-second ad. The team became so focused on crafting a polished broadcast spot they never questioned the fundamental premise: does this idea even work here?

As Chris Norton explains in the episode, the brief contained a phrase that triggered an autopilot response in everyone's mind. One word—TV—became the entire creative direction, regardless of whether it was actually the right one.

The trap of selective listening

This pattern repeats across many failed campaigns: the creative brief contains optionality, nuance, or multiple paths, but the team hears only what confirms their preferred approach. This selective interpretation happens unconsciously, driven by past successes, industry defaults, or the loudest voice in the room.

The real warning sign isn't lack of enthusiasm—it's the absence of defensive questioning. If nobody in the room is asking "But does this idea actually work in this format? What would disprove our assumption?" then you're already operating in an echo chamber. Discover more about what went wrong with the Big Cheese Dipper campaign in the full discussion.

Red flags that appear in hindsight

The Big Cheese Dipper was later compressed into 20-second spots to fit different media slots. This compression didn't save the core idea—it revealed that the original format was never the right vessel for the concept. The one-upmanship escalation that the product depended on simply couldn't breathe in 60 seconds, let alone 20.

If you're in the middle of creative development and someone says "We'll need to cut this down or adapt it for different formats," that's a signal to pause and ask: Does the idea work because it's good, or because we've invested so much in it that we can't see straight? The best protection is a mandatory review moment, as discussed in this episode of Embracing Marketing Mistakes, where someone outside the echo chamber gets to challenge the fundamental fit between idea and medium.

Key warning signs

  • The team defaults to one format without interrogating whether alternatives were considered in the brief.
  • Everyone is excited about the execution, but nobody questions whether the core idea depends on format-specific properties.
  • The brief mentions multiple options, but the team acts as if only one was ever possible.
  • Format constraints (duration, medium, context) aren't tested against the idea's actual logic until after development is locked.
