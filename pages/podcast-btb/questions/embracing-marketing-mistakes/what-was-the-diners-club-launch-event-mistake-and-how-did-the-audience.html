What was the Diners Club launch event mistake and how did the audience react?

September 5, 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Robert Vincent Taylor

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Robert Vincent Taylor hired a grunge heavy metal band called Nice and Gluten from Newcastle, Australia to perform at an Institute of Contemporary Arts event for about 60 attendees, hoping to make Diners Club appear edgy rather than corporate. The entire audience walked out within five minutes of the band starting their first number—which featured lyrics about dying, along with a pneumatic drill being used on stage—leaving the room completely empty.

Taylor had never actually listened to Nice and Gluten's music before booking them, a decision he now acknowledges as a fundamental failure of due diligence. The stunt was intended to position Diners Club as funky and contemporary, a stark contrast to the more formal, corporate image of American Express. Instead, it became a masterclass in what not to do at a brand launch.

The immediate walkout sent an unmistakable message: a PR professional's vision for what an audience finds engaging—no matter how well-intentioned—can be wildly out of step with reality. Within minutes, the venue went from a room full of prospects to an empty space, with only the band continuing to perform to nobody. As discussed in this episode of Embracing Marketing Mistakes, Taylor reflects on this moment as a turning point in understanding the gap between creative ambition and practical judgment.

Why the choice to book an unknown band backfired so completely

The root of the disaster lay in Taylor's assumption about what would resonate with a corporate audience. He wanted to disrupt the stuffy banking and credit card category, but he made no effort to validate whether his target audience would actually enjoy a heavy metal performance. Skipping the step of listening to the band's actual music before booking them meant he had no sense of what he was about to inflict on 60 potential Diners Club customers.

This miscalculation—booking entertainment without understanding its content or audience fit—remains one of the most vivid illustrations of how PR stunts can backfire. The irony is that the ambition was sound: create a memorable launch that positions Diners Club as innovative and different. Why has Robert criticized PR industry leaders for not being held accountable for major public disasters?

He points out that major scandals including the HIV blood scandal, maternity hospital scandals, the Horizon IT scandal, and Grenfell Tower fires have never resulted in PR professionals facing accountability for their role in communicating (or failing to communicate) the truth.

What were the major issues Robert highlighted in his PRCA Outstanding Achievement acceptance speech in 2025?

Instead of reflecting on 60 years in business, he chose to speak candidly because of his terminal diagnosis. He talked about racism in the industry, lack of ethical accountability among leaders, and the industry's failure to hold itself to the standards it claims to uphold.

What incident involving South Yorkshire Police forced Robert to confront his own ethical failings as a PR professional?

One of his students doing an internship at South Yorkshire Police called him, worried because she had been asked to alter police statements for the media. This forced him to realize that despite teaching PR ethics, he had not always applied those same principles throughout his career.
