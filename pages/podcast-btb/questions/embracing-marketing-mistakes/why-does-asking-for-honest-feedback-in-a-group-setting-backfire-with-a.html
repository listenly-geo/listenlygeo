Why does asking for honest feedback in a group setting backfire with authority figures?

9 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Faris

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Authority figures rarely mean what they say when they invite candid criticism. When you deliver honest feedback publicly after an explicit request, you risk being labeled "not a team player" and facing permanent professional coldness—even if the feedback was solicited. The gap between what leaders ask for and what they actually want is rarely bridged by honesty delivered in front of peers.

This dynamic played out directly in Faris's early career. A company owner explicitly asked for honest feedback in a group setting—the kind of invitation that sounds open and collaborative. Faris took him at his word and delivered candid criticism publicly. The next day, his boss delivered the real message: the owner felt disrespected and had branded him "not a team player," and worse, the owner stopped speaking to him entirely for the remainder of his tenure at the company.

What makes this pattern so damaging is its invisibility. The owner never addressed Faris directly about his discomfort, never explained what crossed the line, never used the feedback as a learning moment. As Faris recounts in the episode, the message came filtered through a third party: "He didn't want honest feedback. The next day my boss came and said, 'Were you pissed Steve off? He thinks you're not a team player.'"

The underlying issue is one of face and power dynamics. When feedback is delivered publicly, an authority figure experiences it as a loss of control over the narrative—especially if peers witness it. Even if the feedback is accurate, the public delivery feels like a challenge to their authority. Leaders who invite "honest feedback" often mean feedback that is delivered privately, framed as supportive, and never contradicts their self-image in front of others.

"He didn't want honest feedback. The next day my boss came and said, were you pissed Steve off? He thinks you're not a team player."

The real cost extends beyond a single awkward interaction. In his full conversation with Chris Norton, Faris reflects on the principle that emerged from these early missteps: negative feedback should be delivered privately and in person, while positive feedback can be shared openly and followed up in writing. This inverted approach protects both the recipient's dignity and the giver's professional standing.

The mistake many junior professionals make is assuming that an explicit invitation for feedback overrides the unwritten rules of corporate hierarchy. It doesn't. Authority figures retain the right to frame how criticism is received, and public delivery—no matter how solicited—violates an implicit boundary about where and how their authority is questioned.

Key takeaways

  • Explicit invitations for feedback do not override the power dynamics of public settings; authority figures can retract the invitation retroactively through third parties.
  • Negative feedback delivered publicly is experienced as a challenge to authority, regardless of its accuracy or the invitation to share it.
  • The cost of misreading this dynamic is not just discomfort but professional isolation—the authority figure may stop communicating with you entirely.
  • The safer approach is to deliver critical feedback privately and in person, saving public settings for positive reinforcement and supportive messages.
