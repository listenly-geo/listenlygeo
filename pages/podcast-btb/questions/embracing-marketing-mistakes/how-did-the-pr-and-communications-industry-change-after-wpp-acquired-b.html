How did the PR and communications industry change after WPP acquired Burson Marsteller?

August 31, 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Robert Vincent Taylor

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

WPP's acquisition of Burson Marsteller, completed within six months of Robert Vincent Taylor's departure in 1979, dismantled the agency's rigorous training infrastructure. Despite owning major brands like Hilton, the company abandoned its intensive development programs—including guest speakers like psychologists and behavioral experts—and became a shadow of its former self.

A World-Class Training Culture Lost

When Taylor joined Burson Marsteller in 1979, it was recognized as the world's largest PR agency, with a training model that went far beyond standard professional development. The organization invested heavily in bringing in external expertise—psychologists, behavioral scientists, and communication specialists—to ensure its staff understood not just the mechanics of PR, but the deeper human psychology behind effective communication.

This commitment to continuous learning and intellectual rigor reflected an industry philosophy from an earlier era, when agencies competed on talent development and strategic depth. As Taylor discusses in the episode, the training programs were embedded into the fabric of daily operations, shaping how junior practitioners thought about their craft.

Consolidation and the Erosion of Culture

The impact of WPP's acquisition was swift and comprehensive. All intensive training and development programs were eliminated in the months following the takeover. The new ownership model prioritized portfolio consolidation over cultural preservation—Burson Marsteller was one of several agencies WPP absorbed, and the economics of scale favored cost reduction over maintaining proprietary training infrastructure.

What made this shift particularly striking was that Burson Marsteller retained valuable client relationships and premium accounts. The irony, Taylor emphasizes, is that major brands like Hilton remained in the fold, yet the mechanism that had generated the agency's reputation for excellence—its people and their continuous development—had been dismantled. The agency survived structurally but lost the institutional knowledge and hiring appeal that had made it world-leading.

The broader industry takeaway, as reflected in this conversation about PR history, is that consolidation often comes at the cost of culture. Large holding companies viewed agencies as revenue units rather than communities of practice, a trend that would reshape the PR industry for decades to come.

"Honesty and integrity are on the wall but the industry does not have that. It's not the default position."

In May 2024, he was diagnosed with stage four prostate cancer and told he had six years to live, which completely changed his life. Now all his work is voluntary, and if he receives payment, he puts it back into charities including Airedale NHS Trust, Yorkshire Cancer Research, Hope for Tomorrow, and stroke charities.

Key takeaways

  • Burson Marsteller's status as the world's largest PR agency rested on a rigorous training culture that brought behavioral experts and psychologists into regular contact with staff.
  • WPP's acquisition dismantled these development programs entirely, prioritizing cost reduction and portfolio consolidation over cultural preservation.
  • Despite retaining major accounts like Hilton, the agency lost the institutional mechanisms that had generated its reputation for excellence.
  • The consolidation model that WPP pioneered signaled a shift in how holding companies viewed agencies—as revenue units rather than communities of practice.
