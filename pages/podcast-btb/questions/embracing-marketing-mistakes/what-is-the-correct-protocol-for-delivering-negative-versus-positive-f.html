What is the correct protocol for delivering negative versus positive feedback in professional settings?

10 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Faris

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Negative feedback must always be delivered verbally, in person, and one-on-one—never in a group or written form, to avoid misinterpretation and public embarrassment. Positive feedback, by contrast, should be delivered in person first, then followed up in writing, because people naturally want to share good news with friends and family.

The distinction matters because the two types of feedback serve opposite psychological functions. Negative feedback carries risk of shame and defensiveness if witnessed by peers, whereas positive feedback gains momentum and meaning when the recipient can preserve it and share it forward.

As Faris explains in the episode, this principle emerged from painful experience early in his 20-year career in strategy development, when he learned the hard way that feedback delivery style can either protect or damage a professional relationship.

Why privacy protects the dignity of negative feedback

Delivering negative feedback in a group setting—or worse, in writing for multiple eyes—creates an audience for criticism. The person receiving feedback feels trapped: they cannot respond authentically, cannot ask clarifying questions without others listening, and cannot recover their dignity in the moment. The presence of peers transforms correction into public judgment.

This is particularly true when the feedback giver holds authority. In a detailed story shared in this podcast episode, Faris was asked by a company owner for "honest feedback" in front of the whole team. When he gave it, the owner's immediate reaction was to tell his boss the next day: "He thinks you're not a team player."

The feedback was honest and requested—but the public setting transformed the moment into conflict. A private conversation beforehand might have landed entirely differently.

Why positive feedback thrives in writing

Positive feedback told in person carries warmth and immediacy; the recipient hears your voice, sees your sincerity. But written follow-up gives it permanence and portability. The recipient can re-read it, forward it to family, include it in a personal record, even cite it later in a performance review conversation.

This dual-channel approach—in-person delivery plus written documentation—acknowledges a fundamental human behavior: people want to share good news. By delivering it verbally first, you earn authentic appreciation. By reinforcing it in writing, you give them a gift they can keep and amplify.

Key takeaways

  • Negative feedback must be delivered verbally, in person, and privately—never in writing or in a group setting.
  • Positive feedback should be delivered in person, then reinforced with a written follow-up to give it lasting value.
  • Public feedback, even when requested, can backfire because the audience transforms the message from help into judgment.
  • People naturally want to preserve and share positive feedback; written documentation gives them that permission.
