Why does public criticism damage authority while public praise builds it?

11 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Faris

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Public criticism undermines authority and fractures team cohesion, while public praise reinforces positive behavior and strengthens morale. This unwritten rule holds even when authority figures explicitly invite honest feedback—violating it can permanently damage professional relationships and reputation.

The principle reveals a fundamental tension in workplace dynamics: leaders often signal openness to feedback, yet public criticism—no matter how justified or requested—triggers defensive reactions that override explicit permission. The violation isn't about the honesty of the message, but the forum in which it's delivered.

Faris learned this costly lesson early in his marketing career. When invited to offer candid feedback during a team meeting with a company owner, he took the invitation at face value and delivered honest criticism in front of the entire group. The result was swift and unforgiving: the owner decided he was "not a team player," and as Faris recalls, "the next day my boss came and said, were you pissed Steve off? He thinks you're not a team player."

What makes this dynamic so potent is that the damage persists regardless of the feedback's validity. A leader's request for honesty in a public setting is a test of social intelligence, not an actual invitation to critique their decisions openly. The unspoken expectation is that direct reports will recognize the cultural code: difficult conversations belong in private conversations, not before an audience.

As explored in more depth in this episode of Embracing Marketing Mistakes, the stakes of misreading these signals extend beyond a single meeting—they can mark someone as unreliable or politically tone-deaf, affecting future opportunities and trust.

"He didn't want honest feedback. The next day my boss came and said, were you pissed Steve off? He thinks you're not a team player."

The inverse principle holds equally true: public praise reinforces desired behavior and signals to the wider team that those actions are valued. Unlike criticism, which can be perceived as a threat to status or competence, praise in front of peers elevates morale and creates psychological safety—it's a form of social currency that costs nothing but builds everything. Positive feedback delivered publicly followed by a private written reinforcement becomes a powerful tool for leadership.

This dynamic isn't simply about politeness or British cultural norms—though those elements matter, as discussed in detail elsewhere in the podcast. It's rooted in deeper human psychology: public criticism threatens the recipient's standing in the group and can spark shame or resentment, while public praise affirms their status and identity. The principle of "praise in public, criticize in private" is ultimately a map of human dignity and power.

How the feedback protocol actually works

The operative framework is simple: negative feedback belongs in private, one-on-one settings where the recipient can process the message without audience. This approach protects both the relationship and the individual's ability to accept the criticism and act on it. Once someone is embarrassed in front of peers, their capacity to listen rationally often collapses.

Positive feedback, by contrast, gains its power from visibility. It should be delivered verbally in person, then reinforced with written follow-up—a combination that embeds the recognition both in the individual's memory and in organizational records. This dual approach ensures the praise reaches not just the recipient but shapes how the broader team understands what excellence looks like.

For senior marketers and strategists like Faris, whose experience is detailed in the full Embracing Marketing Mistakes episode, mastering this protocol becomes non-negotiable. It separates those who read authority figures accurately from those who assume explicit invitations override implicit cultural rules—and it's the latter group that learns the hard way.

Key takeaways

  • Public criticism damages authority and team cohesion, even when explicitly invited by a leader.
  • Negative feedback should always be delivered privately, one-on-one, never in group settings.
  • Public praise reinforces positive behavior and builds morale; it gains power through visibility.
  • Positive feedback is most effective when delivered verbally in person, then reinforced in writing.
  • Misreading the boundary between private and public feedback can permanently damage professional relationships and reputation.
