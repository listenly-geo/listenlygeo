How do career mistakes and personal crises make someone a better professional communicator?

16 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Chris Norton & Will Ockenden

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

Surviving a viral crisis and personal trauma equipped a senior PR leader to authentically guide clients through their own media disasters with real authority she couldn't have gained any other way. Having navigated the process herself meant she could speak from lived experience rather than theory alone.

The transformation happens because crisis survival changes a communicator's credibility fundamentally. When someone has been through genuine trauma—public humiliation, online harassment, institutional pressure—they develop an intuitive understanding of what their clients are experiencing that no amount of training can replicate.

As described in this episode of Embracing Marketing Mistakes, the guest's three-year stalking incident involving both herself and her partner Stephen—which escalated to the Metropolitan Police and the Crown Prosecution Service—forced her into a position where she had to confront media chaos, personal vulnerability, and institutional indifference simultaneously. That ordeal didn't break her ability to advise; it sharpened it.

From client advice to practitioner wisdom

When she moved into an advisory position at her agency, the experience of having survived that process meant she could authentically guide others through similar challenges. She wasn't offering theoretical frameworks or textbook responses—she was offering the hard-won knowledge of someone who had actually lived through the worst-case scenario.

This is particularly valuable in the PR and communications world, where many professionals can go their entire careers without facing a genuine crisis. Those who have tend to become the trusted anchors for their teams and their clients. They carry a quiet authority that comes not from credentials but from scars.

The paradox is that the very situation that could have ended her career—the viral tweet about a Sleeping Beauty illustration that triggered global harassment during the Me Too movement—became the foundation of her expertise. She didn't learn crisis management from a case study. She lived it.

"I'm a much better practitioner for it. And I get a lot of shit in this role. You know, like, there's a lot of trouble."

What makes this wisdom valuable is that it breaks the illusion many professionals hold: that crisis management is a detachable skill, something you can compartmentalize and apply clinically. In reality, the episode reveals that the emotional and psychological weight of a crisis is inseparable from how you navigate it. Someone who has felt that weight can help others recognize and manage their own.

Key takeaways

  • Surviving a public crisis creates authentic credibility that theoretical training cannot provide.
  • Lived experience of trauma and institutional pressure becomes a competitive advantage in advisory roles.
  • The practitioner who has navigated their own media disaster understands client psychology in ways pure specialists do not.
