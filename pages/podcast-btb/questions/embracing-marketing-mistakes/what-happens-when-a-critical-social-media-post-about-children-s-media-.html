What happens when a critical social media post about children's media gets misreported by tabloids and goes globally viral?

12 September 2026 Embracing Marketing Mistakes · Chris Norton

← See the Embracing Marketing Mistakes fiche

A single tweet critiquing a Sleeping Beauty illustration in Biff and Chip books during the Me Too movement was picked up by the Evening Chronicle, syndicated across tabloids, and went globally viral with major commentators mischaracterizing it as an attempt to ban fairy tales. The author faced death threats, rape threats, and threats of child removal, alongside media doorstepping at her children's school and the headmaster's home. Yet standing by her values ultimately led to professional opportunities and genuine community support.

How a single tweet became a global tabloid storm

What began as a thoughtful observation about gender representation in children's literature became a cascade of misreporting and harassment. The Evening Chronicle's initial coverage was picked up by other tabloids and amplified by commentators who had not read the original tweet. The narrative shifted from a critique of one illustration to claims that fairy tales themselves were under attack.

As documented in this episode of Embracing Marketing Mistakes, the speed and scale of misinterpretation revealed how little media outlets verify before republishing. The original context was abandoned entirely, replaced by a sensationalized version designed for clicks and outrage.

The personal cost of standing by your values

The immediate aftermath was brutal. Death threats, rape threats, and threats of child removal arrived alongside doorstepping journalists at her children's school and the headmaster's private residence. The harassment extended both online and into her physical community, creating an environment of genuine fear for her and her family.

Initial responses to her attempt to clarify the original intent were met with dismissal. Online shaming compounded the isolation. The experience tested not only her resilience but also her commitment to speaking honestly about issues she believed mattered, even when the cost was severe personal harm.

Yet as she reflects in the full conversation, refusing to recant or apologize for her original observation became an unexpected anchor. Communities that valued her ethics began to surface, and professional doors opened precisely because she had demonstrated integrity under extreme pressure.

"I'm a much better practitioner for it. And I get a lot of shit in this role. You know, like, there's a lot of trouble."

What makes this story particularly relevant to marketers and communicators is that it demonstrates how reputational crises rarely unfold as predicted. The person at the center of the storm did not attempt to "manage" her way out of it through PR tactics—she stood still. The community that eventually rallied around her did so precisely because she did not betray herself to appease the mob.

Interestingly, beyond the harassment and the vindication, there are other dimensions to this incident—including specific details about how she navigated the media landscape and what institutional support actually looked like—that reveal uncomfortable truths about how organizations respond when their employees face public attacks.

What does the principle 'praise in public, criticise in private' reveal about workplace dynamics?

This principle reflects the reality that public criticism damages authority and team cohesion, while public praise reinforces positive behavior and morale. How you deliver feedback shapes organizational culture far more than the content of the feedback itself.

What is the correct protocol for delivering negative versus positive feedback in professional settings?

Negative feedback should be delivered verbally in person to an individual, never in a group setting. Positive feedback should amplify value and reinforce the behaviors you want to see repeated across the organization.

How does British business culture differ from direct communication cultures in interpreting common phrases?

Phrases like 'my door is always open,' 'I want feedback,' 'we'll go for coffee,' and 'let's touch base' are commonly used in British business culture but often carry unspoken meanings that can be misinterpreted by those from more direct communication backgrounds.
