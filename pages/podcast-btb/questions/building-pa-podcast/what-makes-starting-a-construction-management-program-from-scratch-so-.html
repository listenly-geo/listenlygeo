What makes starting a construction management program from scratch so difficult?

September 12, 2026 Building PA Podcast · Amanda McIsaac

← See the Building PA Podcast fiche

Building a construction management program requires integrating multiple disciplines—OSHA certification, civil engineering, and business courses—while constantly adapting to real-time industry changes and emerging technology. The real challenge isn't just organizing existing content; it's keeping graduates genuinely job-ready when the construction field evolves faster than traditional academic planning cycles can accommodate.

Creating a construction management program from the ground up means far more than assembling course titles. As detailed in the Building PA Podcast episode, you must understand the curriculum developed by other faculty members, trace how each course builds on the previous one, and ensure every component aligns with industry standards and licensing requirements.

Coordinating Safety, Engineering, and Business Into One Coherent Path

The first major hurdle is ensuring courses integrate seamlessly across three distinct domains. Students pursuing construction management need OSHA 40-hour certification—a specific safety benchmark—but they also need solid grounding in civil engineering principles and business fundamentals like project management and budgeting. Each domain has its own instructional logic, and forcing them together without careful sequencing can confuse students or leave gaps.

Amanda McIsaac, who has spent over 20 years in the construction industry with CDM Smith and is now leading Slippery Rock University's Construction Management program, has tackled this by establishing an advisory board of 12 industry professionals—eight from Western Pennsylvania alone—to ensure curriculum stays grounded in real-world practice. This external validation is essential because academia and construction operate on different timelines and priorities.

Keeping Students Current When the Industry Never Stops Changing

The harder challenge is maintaining relevance as technology and industry practices shift continuously. Traditional curriculum development happens on a multi-year cycle: courses are designed, approved, taught, evaluated, and revised. Construction, meanwhile, is adopting new software, safety protocols, and project delivery methods every year.

A student who learns construction management on tools and processes from three years ago risks graduating without competitive knowledge. The program must anticipate not just today's industry standards but tomorrow's, a task that requires constant communication with working professionals—not just one-time consultation. McIsaac created one new course in the program's first year and three in the second year, demonstrating the pace at which curriculum must evolve to keep pace. As she explains in the podcast, the goal is to ensure graduates are "fully prepared and a real asset when they graduate," which means embedding industry reality into every semester, not just textbook theory.

"Construction is relationships and while we have all of the technology to make our lives easier, when it comes down to it, it is relationship-based."

This philosophy underpins the real difficulty: a program is only as strong as its connection to active practitioners. The podcast episode explores how McIsaac built this network with companies like PennDOT, Runnick Brothers, and UPMC, turning them into ongoing partners rather than one-off guest speakers. That relationship-first approach is what makes the program continuously relevant—and it's also what makes starting from scratch so labor-intensive and demanding.

Key takeaways

  • Curriculum integration across safety certification, civil engineering, and business courses must be carefully sequenced so each builds on prior knowledge without creating gaps.
  • Industry technology and practices evolve faster than traditional academic approval cycles, requiring ongoing refreshes—McIsaac added three new courses in year two alone.
  • An active advisory board of working professionals is essential to prevent curriculum drift and ensure graduates match current job market expectations.
  • Relationship-based engagement with major employers and industry partners keeps the program responsive to real-world change rather than locked into theory.
