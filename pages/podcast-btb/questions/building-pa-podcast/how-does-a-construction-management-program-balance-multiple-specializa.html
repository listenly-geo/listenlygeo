How does a construction management program balance multiple specializations and career paths?

September 13, 2026 Building PA Podcast · Amanda McIsaac

← See the Building PA Podcast fiche

Slippery Rock University's construction management program deliberately operates with zero enforced specialization, allowing students to explore multiple career paths including highway construction, building projects, and residential work. The curriculum remains intentionally broad, with exposure created through diverse voices—owners, project managers, specialty contractors, and executives—brought into classrooms and job site visits.

The program's openness stems from a deliberate design choice. Rather than narrowing the curriculum early, Amanda McIsaac and her team recognized that first-year students often lack clarity about their ultimate career direction. By keeping options open, the program serves students at different stages of certainty while building foundational knowledge that applies across all construction sectors.

This flexibility extended to how the curriculum itself was built. McIsaac explains in the episode how an advisory board of 12 industry professionals—including eight from Western Pennsylvania—was shaped partly by student input on their own career visions. This ensured the program reflected real market needs while honoring what students actually wanted to explore.

Exposure Through Real-World Connections

The real differentiation happens in execution. Students are systematically exposed to different roles and sectors through structured job site tours and rotating guest speakers. These aren't passive observers; they hear directly from UPMC's senior vice president for construction, from independent specialty contractors, and from project managers working on diverse project types.

This approach mirrors how professional construction actually works. As discussed at length in the podcast, exposing students to owners, general contractors, and subcontractors in a single program creates a more complete picture of decision-making across the industry. A student interested in residential work gets to understand how a general contractor and an HVAC specialty contractor view the same project differently—essential knowledge regardless of final specialization.

By design, the Slippery Rock program trusts that broad exposure early leads to informed specialization later, if specialization becomes necessary at all. The result is graduates who understand the full ecosystem before committing to a narrower path—a significant advantage in an industry where cross-functional understanding increasingly matters.

"Construction is relationships and while we have all of the technology to make our lives easier, when it comes down to it, it is relationship-based."

The program's first cohort included 14 students, each bringing different aspirations. Some arrived knowing they wanted heavy highway work; others came curious about building restoration or commercial development. The episode details how specific job site visits to facilities like Boyer's in Pennsylvania and storm-focused projects provide these real-world anchors that textbooks simply cannot replicate.

Key takeaways

  • Zero mandatory specialization keeps the curriculum open to highway, building, and residential pathways simultaneously.
  • An advisory board of 12 industry professionals—informed by student input—ensures curriculum reflects actual career options and market needs.
  • Systematic exposure through job site tours and guest speakers (owners, PMs, specialty contractors, executives) gives students exposure to multiple roles before choosing a focus.
  • Broad foundational knowledge allows informed specialization later, rather than forcing early narrowing that may not match student interests.
