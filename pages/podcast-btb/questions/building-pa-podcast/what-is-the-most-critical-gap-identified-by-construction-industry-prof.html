Why Communication Skills Matter Most for New Construction Graduates

September 14, 2026 Building PA Podcast · Amanda McIsaac

← See the Building PA Podcast fiche

Communication was identified almost unanimously by industry professionals as the major gap in new construction graduates entering the field. While students rely on abbreviated text messages and AI-generated emails, construction remains fundamentally relationship-based, requiring direct and effective communication with subcontractors, material suppliers, and field teams.

This finding came from the first advisory board meeting in March, where 12 construction industry professionals gathered to evaluate the curriculum at Slippery Rock University's newly launched Construction Management program. Eight of those advisors are based in Western Pennsylvania, giving the feedback a strong regional perspective rooted in real hiring practices and field realities.

The disconnect is clear: new graduates have grown up in a digital communication environment dominated by social media shorthand and convenience tools. Yet construction projects depend on clarity, accountability, and trust built through direct conversations. A misunderstood email to a subcontractor can cascade into schedule delays or safety issues. A poorly worded phone call with a supplier can cost thousands. The relationship-based nature of the industry makes communication not just a soft skill—it becomes a competitive necessity.

How Industry Professionals Are Addressing the Gap

Amanda McIsaac, who has spent over 20 years in the construction industry with CDM Smith before becoming an adjunct professor at Slippery Rock University, took this feedback seriously. As the director of the newly launched program, she established the 12-member advisory board precisely to catch gaps like this early and influence course design before they become systemic problems.

The insight gained as explained in this episode reveals a core tension: technical competency alone—understanding schedules, budgets, and OSHA requirements—won't make a graduate effective if they can't articulate ideas, negotiate conflicts, or build trust with the crews and vendors they'll manage.

"Construction is relationships and while we have all of the technology to make our lives easier, when it comes down to it, it is relationship-based."

The program is responding by embedding communication training throughout the curriculum, not as a standalone elective but as an integral part of project management, site supervision, and client relations courses. McIsaac has designed multiple courses from scratch during her first two years teaching, ensuring that each addresses both technical and interpersonal competencies in real-world contexts.

For students preparing to enter the industry, this is worth noting: employers value technical credentials—like the OSHA 40-hour certification students complete during their junior year—but they're increasingly screening for communication maturity. A graduate who can write a clear daily report, explain a schedule delay to a frustrated client, or listen to field feedback and respond decisively will stand out immediately in hiring and early career advancement.

What many graduates don't realize until they're on a job site is that communication failures often hurt careers more than technical mistakes. Technical mistakes can be corrected; communication failures damage relationships and reputation. As discussed in the full episode, this is why industry professionals—the people actually hiring—flagged it first.

How does a construction management program balance multiple specializations and career paths?

The Slippery Rock program has zero specialization, allowing students to explore different areas including highway construction, building, and residential construction without committing early.

What makes starting a construction management program from scratch so difficult for an academic institution?

Creating a program requires understanding the curriculum developed by other faculty, understanding what each course teaches and how they build on each other to create a coherent learning pathway.

What competitive pressures are signatory contractors facing in the Philadelphia residential construction market?

Margins are thin during current downtrend periods, and out-of-town developers are increasingly targeting the residential market by cutting wages and undermining established wage standards.
