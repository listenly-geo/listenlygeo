How early should you start marketing your retreat to actually fill it?

Shannon Jamail is clear on this: start marketing your retreat 9 to 12 months before the date, and never less than 6 months out. Starting too late is one of the most common — and most avoidable — reasons retreats fail to fill, because potential participants, especially in the post-COVID landscape, need meaningful time to plan, budget, and commit.

The reasoning goes beyond simple visibility. A long marketing runway lets you reach two distinct types of buyers: the advance planners who book months ahead, and the last-minute registrants who still need time to discover your retreat before they can even consider it. Compressing your marketing into a few weeks means you miss the first group entirely and give yourself almost no room to build momentum with the second. This fundamental timing mistake is one Jamail returns to consistently across episodes of The Retreat Leaders Podcast because it cuts across all niches, price points, and retreat formats.

It's also worth noting that this timing advice sits alongside — not above — the other fundamentals Jamail covers in this episode: mindset, messaging, pricing, and audience clarity. A retreat marketed 12 months out with a poorly defined audience or a shaky price point will still struggle. But a well-positioned retreat with less than six months of runway is already working against itself before a single person has seen the offer.

Why is niching down essential for selling out a retreat?

Trying to appeal to too broad an audience is a common mistake in the retreat industry — as Jamail puts it, "if you're speaking to everyone, you're speaking to no one." A one-size-fits-all approach rarely works, and a tightly defined niche is what makes your marketing land with the right people.

What is the number one reason a retreat fails to fill up according to retreat hosting experts?

According to Jamail, the primary reason a retreat does not fill is mindset and belief. A mindset rooted in doubt, fear, and lack of confidence can undermine every other aspect of the marketing and enrollment process — before a single post goes live.
