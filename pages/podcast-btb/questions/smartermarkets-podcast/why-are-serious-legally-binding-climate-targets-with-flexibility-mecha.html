Why are serious, legally-binding climate targets with flexibility mechanisms essential compared to aspirational targets?

2026-09-25 SmarterMarkets · Dirk Forrister

← See the SmarterMarkets fiche

Mandatory legal embeddedness replaces the old model of picking numbers and accepting failure. Serious targets work because companies stick to them when they're embedded in law, and built-in flexibility mechanisms—particularly market-based approaches—let businesses manage their own trajectories, find cost efficiencies, and actually achieve Paris Agreement objectives instead of simply missing aspirational goals without consequence.

The shift from aspirational to binding represents a fundamental change in climate accountability. Blind ambition—setting targets with no real enforcement mechanism—has proven ineffective because there was no penalty for missing them. Reality is now forcing governments and businesses to recognize that vague commitments lead nowhere.

As Dirk Forrister explains in this episode, the new approach demands that targets be grounded in law—making them enforceable—while also recognizing that businesses need room to innovate on *how* they achieve them. Flexibility mechanisms, especially market-based tools like emissions trading systems, are not loopholes; they are the practical pathways that allow companies to lower costs while meeting mandatory compliance.

"We want serious targets that people will stick to that are embedded in law, that allow flexibilities so that we can really achieve the objectives of the Paris Agreement."

Market mechanisms shift the burden of compliance from government auditing to business incentives. When companies face a legal obligation but can choose their own emissions reduction path—whether through internal efficiency, purchasing offsets, or trading credits—they have motivation to find the cheapest route. This self-interest drives real emissions reductions far more reliably than aspirational targets ever could.

The European Union, California, and Quebec have all moved in this direction, embedding carbon pricing in law and extending programs to meet tougher future targets. As discussed in the podcast, even the European Commission's July 2026 review of its emissions trading system reflects this hardening of targets while simultaneously expanding flexibility options—proving that legal rigor and market freedom are not contradictory but complementary.

This approach also creates international momentum for carbon market cooperation. Countries recognizing that flexibility works are joining coalitions and bilateral agreements under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, allowing them to cooperate on emissions reductions across borders while maintaining domestic legal enforcement. The result: more ambitious targets become achievable because the mechanism to reach them is realistic.

What are the emerging international coalitions shaping carbon market policy and coordination?

Several coalitions have emerged to provide political momentum for carbon market cooperation. The Coalition to Grow Carbon Markets brings together countries committed to expanding international carbon markets, driving coordinated policy on carbon pricing and emissions trading mechanisms.

What flexibility mechanisms does the Paris Agreement provide for countries to cooperate on carbon markets?

The Paris Agreement provides two main paths under Article 6: bilateral or plurilateral cooperation under Section 6.2, where countries are free to cooperate directly on carbon market solutions that meet their national climate commitments.

What is the current supply-demand imbalance in Corsia markets and how is it evolving?

When the year started, the Corsia market was roughly balanced with around 35 million tons of demand and approximately equal supply approved. Current demand has shifted dramatically to approximately 200 million tons of need, signaling explosive growth in aviation carbon offset markets.
