How Does a Marine Corps Culture Transform a Defense Company?

September 10, 2026 The Aerospace Executive Podcast · John Albers

← See the The Aerospace Executive Podcast fiche

Albers deliberately calls all staff "teammates" rather than employees to flatten hierarchy and signal that every role carries equal weight. This practice draws directly from Marine Corps leadership principles, where decision-making authority flows from competence and thinking is prized over blind adherence to procedure—everyone gets the same 24 hours in a day, and each contribution shapes the mission.

At Albers Aerospace, this terminology shift is not semantic decoration. It reflects a fundamental rejection of corporate rank-and-file structure in favor of a mission-driven culture modeled on military values. When an aircraft commander in the Marine Corps needed to execute a decision, rank mattered less than whether that person had thought through the problem and earned crew trust—a principle Albers has transplanted directly into his defense company's operations.

As detailed in this episode of The Aerospace Executive Podcast, this cultural foundation shapes how the company has scaled from a single-person venture in 2015 to nine operational sites across the country in just over a decade. The vocabulary of "teammate" becomes the visible anchor for deeper organizational behavior: collaboration without needless hierarchy, accountability distributed rather than concentrated.

"The truth is I'm a 32-year-old businessman in a 56-year-old man's body, and I got a lot to learn, studying my craft every day."

The cultural shift extends beyond vocabulary into formal leadership development. Albers recently launched a 12-week NCO leadership academy for middle managers, deliberately translating Marine Corps non-commissioned officer training into the business environment. He also rolled out a year-long Albers Leadership Development program for directors and above, ensuring that as the company expands, the foundational value system—thinking over rote compliance, mission alignment over status—remains legible at every level.

This approach stands in sharp contrast to traditional defense primes, where organizational layers and procedural rigidity often slow decision-making. By adopting Marine Corps language and decision-making culture, Albers Aerospace signals to new hires and clients alike that speed, accountability, and intellectual engagement matter more than titles or seniority for its own sake.

Embedding Marine Values Into a Growing Organization

The term "teammate" is the visible proxy for a deeper operational philosophy. In the Marine Corps, an aircraft commander's authority derived from demonstrated judgment, not rank alone. That same principle now runs through Albers Aerospace: people at every level are trusted to think, contribute, and own outcomes. The nine-site footprint and organic $12 million annual revenue growth (before acquisitions) suggest this cultural model is neither theoretical nor self-limiting—it scales when you hire people who accept joint ownership.

Albers himself has modeled this commitment to growth through deliberate study. He reads two business and leadership books monthly and treats organizational design as a craft requiring constant refinement. When he discusses scaling challenges and his leadership development initiatives on the podcast, the theme is consistent: culture is not inherited, it is practiced and reinforced through structure, language, and deliberate recruitment of people aligned to the mission.

Key takeaways

  • Albers uses "teammate" instead of "employee" to eliminate perceived hierarchy and affirm that every role is equally vital to mission success.
  • Marine Corps decision-making logic—valuing thinking and competence over rank—is transplanted directly into the company's operational culture.
  • Formal programs like the 12-week NCO leadership academy and year-long Albers Leadership Development program ensure the culture scales as the company grows across nine operational sites.
  • This cultural model has proven compatible with rapid growth and profitability, generating organic revenue while maintaining alignment on values.
