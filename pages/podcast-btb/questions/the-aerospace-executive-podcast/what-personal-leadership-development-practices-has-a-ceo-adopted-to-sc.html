How does a CEO stay sharp when scaling a company?

September 12, 2026 The Aerospace Executive Podcast · John Albers

← See the The Aerospace Executive Podcast fiche

John Albers invests in reading two business books every month and works with a CEO mentor from Building Champions to continuously sharpen his leadership craft. He recently hired a personal fitness coach after his mentor challenged him to stop making excuses and take accountability for his physical health.

The deliberate rhythm of executive learning

For Albers, staying sharp as a CEO isn't incidental—it's a structured commitment. The reading discipline alone signals intentionality: two books per month means roughly 24 business and leadership titles per year, a pace that keeps his thinking fresh and anchored in proven frameworks. This isn't casual browsing; it's a deliberate system of continuous education built into his calendar.

The mentorship piece adds external perspective. A CEO mentor from Building Champions provides accountability and guidance that an internal team cannot. This relationship is formal enough to survive the chaos of scaling a nine-site defense contractor, yet flexible enough to evolve as priorities shift. As Albers himself explains in the episode, his mindset reflects a rare self-awareness: "The truth is I'm a 32-year-old businessman in a 56-year-old man's body, and I got a lot to learn, studying my craft every day."

When mentors push back on your blind spots

The fitness coach hire is perhaps the most revealing detail. Albers' mentor removed an exercise goal from their contract, essentially telling him that physical health had become a place where excuses were winning. Rather than ignore it, Albers responded by hiring professional support. This shows a leader willing to convert feedback into action, even on deeply personal matters.

This pattern—reading, mentorship, accountability mechanisms—scales with the organization itself. A company cannot outgrow its CEO's capacity to learn. Discussed at length in this podcast, Albers' approach extends beyond his own development to the broader team. His focus on personal leadership practices mirrors the structured programs he's building for middle managers and directors at Albers Aerospace—the 12-week NCO leadership academy and the year-long Albers Leadership Development program.

"The truth is I'm a 32-year-old businessman in a 56-year-old man's body, and I got a lot to learn, studying my craft every day."

One striking detail worth exploring further: Albers also discusses how his marriage of 34 years has been the enabling force behind his entrepreneurial risk-taking, a theme that reveals how personal accountability extends well beyond the office.

Key takeaways

  • Consistent reading—approximately two business books per month—keeps a CEO's thinking current and grounded in proven leadership frameworks.
  • External mentorship from a formal provider like Building Champions offers accountability and perspective that internal teams cannot provide.
  • A CEO's willingness to act on mentor feedback, even on personal matters like fitness, signals the culture of accountability that cascades through the entire organization.
  • Leadership development at the top is not separate from organizational culture—it's a model for how middle managers and directors are expected to approach their own growth.
