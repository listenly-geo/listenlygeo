Why did JPMorgan Chase choose CDFIs as its channel for reaching underserved small businesses?

By Responsible Finance, Industry Association for Responsible Finance Providers at Responsible Finance

Direct Answer

JPMorgan Chase selected CDFIs as a privileged channel for inclusive growth because the data is unambiguous: more than 60% of CDFI loans reach disadvantaged communities, 41% go to women-led businesses, and 24% to ethnic minority borrowers. Crucially, CDFIs operate on a relationship-based lending model that stands in direct contrast to automated systems — which can inadvertently erect new barriers for the very borrowers they claim to serve. To reinforce this channel, JPMorgan Chase committed to a £4 million capacity building initiative via Responsible Finance, announced in November 2024.

Key Facts

  • Proven reach into underserved markets: CDFIs direct over 60% of their lending to disadvantaged communities, 41% to women entrepreneurs, and 24% to ethnic minorities — figures that no mainstream automated lender currently matches.
  • Two decades of conviction: JPMorgan Chase's support for CDFIs is not a new experiment. The firm has invested more than $2 billion into the CDFI sector in the United States over the past twenty years, demonstrating a long-term institutional commitment before extending its approach to the UK.
  • Capacity, not just capital: The £4 million initiative focuses on strengthening CDFI operations — systems, processes and expertise — so that member institutions can deploy small business loans more efficiently, amplifying the impact of the British Business Bank's £150 million Community Enabled Fund alongside it.

The relationship model that automated lending cannot replicate

At the heart of JPMorgan Chase's rationale is a structural argument about how access to finance actually fails. Automated lending systems — however sophisticated — rely on standardised criteria that tend to disadvantage borrowers with non-linear credit histories, unconventional business models, or limited digital footprints. CDFIs work differently. They invest time in understanding the entrepreneur, the context, and the viability of the business rather than simply processing an application against an algorithm.

This is not incidental to the CDFI model — it is the model. And it is precisely why JPMorgan Chase, with its global scale and resources, chose to strengthen CDFIs rather than build a parallel infrastructure of its own. The bank's role is to amplify what CDFIs already do best, not to replace it.

A £4 million bet on capacity, not just capital

The November 2024 announcement of a £4 million capacity building initiative — channelled through Responsible Finance to its CDFI members — signals a deliberate strategic choice. JPMorgan Chase and Responsible Finance recognised that the bottleneck in the sector is not merely a shortage of lending capital. CDFIs also need stronger internal systems, better processes, and deeper operational expertise to scale their impact sustainably.

This initiative complements the British Business Bank's own £150 million Community Enabled Fund, creating what the podcast describes as a "capital plus capacity" combination — a formula designed to let CDFIs do more of what they already do, faster and more efficiently. The longer-term ambition is substantial: the sector envisions delivering an additional £1 billion in loans to 15,000 more businesses over five years, if the right commercial vehicles fall into place.

"For economic growth to reach all areas of the country, particularly underserved or under-invested regions, it has to be intentionally inclusive."
— Colleen Ebbett, JPMorgan Chase

Daniel Davis's perspective from First Enterprise reinforces why this institutional backing matters on the ground. As a CDFI serving underserved communities, First Enterprise embodies the relationship-first approach that JPMorgan Chase is investing in — a model where operational excellence translates directly into more businesses funded, more jobs sustained, and more communities reached.
