How has consolidation in citrus growing and juice processing regions reshaped supply chain complexity?

September 25, 2026 The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly · Dino Cardelli

← See The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly fiche

Over the past 15–20 years, regional citrus growers have consolidated from eight to ten players down to just two or three, and the Mexican juice processing industry followed the same pattern, fundamentally altering how procurement teams negotiate and plan supply. Large consolidated players now dominate key growing and processing regions, eliminating the flexibility that existed when many smaller operators competed side by side.

From Fragmented Markets to Consolidated Giants

The structural shift in the citrus and juice industry has been dramatic. What once was a landscape of diverse, independent growers and processors—offering procurement teams multiple sourcing options—has narrowed significantly. In regions like Colima and along the Mexican coast, three or four large consolidated players now control the market, compared to the scattered ecosystem that existed two decades ago.

This consolidation extends beyond growers to the juice processing side. The Mexican juice processing sector, historically fragmented across many small operators, has collapsed into just three or four major processors. When processing capacity is concentrated in so few hands, procurement teams lose negotiating leverage and flexibility in supplier selection.

As Dino Cardelli explains in this episode, the market dynamics have shifted from "many choices, many partners" to "take it or leave it with a handful of major suppliers."

Supply Chain Ripple Effects

Consolidation doesn't just change who you buy from—it transforms how you buy. With fewer suppliers controlling larger volumes, procurement strategies must pivot toward longer-term contracts and more sophisticated forecasting. What used to be spot-market flexibility with dozens of regional growers is now replaced by centralized negotiations with consolidated entities.

The consolidated players also operate at greater scale and geographic spread. They manage operations across multiple countries and climates, which can stabilize supply in some ways but creates rigidity in others. Procurement teams now depend more heavily on understanding the strategic priorities and capacity constraints of a few key suppliers rather than playing smaller growers against each other.

A related complexity that Cardelli discusses throughout the podcast is how geopolitical and weather events now ripple through the entire system faster, since fewer independent actors mean fewer alternative routes when disruption hits one of the major players.

"A client is a client. A customer is a customer. A lot of the stuff I was talking about, you know, although I buy juice, I mean, I was watching the fresh market as well as you."

For deeper insight into how this consolidation intersects with the fresh produce market, the full episode covers how fresh market price spikes trigger procurement strategy shifts when growers redirect their best fruit away from processing.

Key takeaways

  • Regional citrus growers have consolidated from 8–10 independent operators to just 2–3 major players per region over the past 15–20 years.
  • Mexican juice processors have undergone similar consolidation, shrinking from many small operations to 3–4 dominant companies.
  • Consolidated suppliers now control larger geographic footprints and processing capacity, reducing procurement team flexibility and negotiating options.
  • Procurement strategies must shift toward longer-term contracts and more sophisticated forecasting rather than spot-market flexibility with fragmented suppliers.

What is the difference between a mentality required for fresh produce procurement versus juice procurement?

A juice procurement professional must monitor geopolitical events and global weather patterns differently than fresh buyers. When tropical storms form, juice teams track their path and impact on processing capacity and fruit quality.

How does sourcing strategy change when fresh market prices for citrus commodities spike dramatically?

When fresh market prices for limes go extremely high—such as hitting $80 a box—procurement teams get nervous because growers redirect available fruit away from juice processing to the more profitable fresh market, tightening juice supply.
