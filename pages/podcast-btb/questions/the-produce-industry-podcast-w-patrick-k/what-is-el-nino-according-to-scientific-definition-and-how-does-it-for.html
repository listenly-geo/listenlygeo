What triggers El Niño in the Pacific Ocean and how is it officially declared?

September 15, 2026 The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly

← See The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly fiche

El Niño is a climate pattern officially declared when equatorial Pacific Ocean temperatures rise at least 0.5 degrees Celsius above average for several consecutive months. The pattern forms when the Walker Circulation—the normal atmospheric circulation pattern over the Pacific—breaks down, allowing warm water to shift eastward toward South America instead of staying in the western Pacific.

How the Pacific's Circulation System Breaks Down

The Walker Circulation is normally a consistent atmospheric feature that drives trade winds westward and maintains cold water near South America. When this circulation weakens or reverses, warm water pools that usually sit near Indonesia and the western Pacific begin moving across the ocean toward the east coast. This eastward migration is the hallmark of El Niño formation.

The scientific definition relies on precise measurement. As Patrick Kelly explains in the episode, meteorologists track sea surface temperature anomalies in specific Pacific regions using data from organizations like NOAA and the World Meteorological Organization. When those anomalies cross the 0.5-degree threshold and persist across multiple months, the pattern transitions from a forecast concern to an officially declared El Niño event.

Walker Circulation: The normal atmospheric circulation pattern over the Pacific Ocean that typically drives trade winds westward and maintains a temperature gradient, with cooler water in the eastern Pacific and warmer water in the western Pacific. When this circulation breaks down or reverses, warm water migrates east and El Niño conditions develop.

This classification system matters because it triggers monitoring and forecasting by major meteorological institutions worldwide. The World Food Program, the Conference Board, and agricultural organizations use El Niño declarations to assess global risks—particularly for food security in vulnerable regions. The podcast covers how current El Niño conditions are already reshaping agricultural forecasts and supply chain planning for 2027.

The Current Super El Niño Event

What makes the current situation noteworthy is its strength and persistence. El Niño is firmly established and expected to strengthen further with nearly 100 percent likelihood that it will persist through February 2027, with peak intensity expected between September and December 2026.

The implications ripple across agriculture immediately. Temperature shifts alter precipitation patterns, affecting crop yields in Asia, Africa, and the Americas. The episode details how this Super El Niño is already influencing global food prices and produce market dynamics in ways that extend far beyond the Pacific itself. For industries like produce—where climate directly shapes yields and availability—understanding the scientific mechanism behind El Niño's formation is essential to strategic planning.
