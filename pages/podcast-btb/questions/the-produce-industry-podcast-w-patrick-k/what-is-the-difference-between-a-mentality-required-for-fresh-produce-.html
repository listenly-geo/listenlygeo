What's the difference between a mentality required for fresh produce procurement versus juice procurement?

September 24, 2026 The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly · Dino Cardelli

← See the The Produce Industry Podcast w/ Patrick Kelly fiche

Juice procurement professionals operate under a fundamentally different risk-management paradigm than fresh buyers. They must continuously monitor geopolitical events and global weather patterns—tracking tropical storms in the Gulf to predict citrus belt impact, or assessing earthquakes in the Colima area for immediate supplier disruption—rather than reacting to daily market shifts like fresh market operators do.

This proactive, globally-oriented mentality stems from the nature of juice fruit procurement itself. As a juice procurement professional explained in the episode, when a tropical storm forms in the Gulf, juice teams immediately begin scenario-planning: Where will it track? Will it hit the citrus belt? Which suppliers might be affected? This is not speculation—it's a structured intelligence gathering process that determines procurement decisions weeks or months in advance.

Fresh market procurement, by contrast, reacts to price signals and immediate supply availability. A spike in lime prices or a sudden availability shift reshapes buying patterns within days. But juice procurement cannot operate on that timeline. A juice procurement strategy must account for multi-month supply contracts, ingredient consistency requirements, and the reality that a weather event thousands of miles away can trigger cascading supplier impacts across multiple regions.

Geopolitical risk as a procurement lens

An earthquake in Mexico's Colima area isn't just a regional incident for a juice procurement team—it's an immediate trigger for outreach to every affected supplier, assessing damage, alternate sourcing, and timeline implications. This global, forward-looking risk management mindset differs fundamentally from fresh market thinking, where regional disruptions are absorbed and repriced in real time.

Dino Cardelli, who has spent over 35 years managing procurement across grower, processor, and executive roles for companies including Sun Orchard Juicery, PepsiCo, Tropicana, and Dole, draws this distinction clearly. He has worked and managed projects in over 50 countries, giving him direct exposure to how both market segments operate. The difference isn't in the commodities themselves—it's in the decision-making cadence and the breadth of variables considered at once.

A key detail discussed during the conversation involves how industrial consolidation has changed the game: regional grower pools have collapsed from eight to ten suppliers down to just two or three. This means juice procurement now requires even deeper geopolitical and weather intelligence, because any single disruption carries outsized impact.

"A client is a client. A customer is a customer. A lot of the stuff I was talking about, you know, although I buy juice, I mean, I was watching the fresh market as well as you."

The mindset gap extends to rejection tolerance and quality standards as well. Juice procurement operates under tighter margins—achieving a rejection rate of less than 1% on fruit supplied demands relentless supplier vetting and continuous environmental monitoring, not just seasonal pricing negotiations.

How does sourcing strategy change when fresh market prices for citrus commodities spike dramatically?

When fresh market prices for limes spike dramatically—such as hitting $80 a box—procurement teams get nervous because growers redirect fruit to the fresh market, impacting juice fruit availability and forcing immediate strategy pivots.

When is the Super El Niño event expected to peak and how long will its effects persist?

The strengthening El Niño is expected to peak between September and December of 2026, but its effects will continue through 2027, creating sustained volatility across the global produce supply chain.

What specific business impacts should fresh produce industry participants expect from Super El Niño disruptions?

The fresh produce industry should expect volatile prices across commodities, reduced yields in key export regions, and higher demand for imports as local production falters under climate stress.
