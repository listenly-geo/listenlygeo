How has a small software team remained lean while building a manufacturing platform?

September 25, 2026 The Manufacturing IT Podcast · Bryan Sapot

← See The Manufacturing IT Podcast fiche

The founding team's 26-plus years of cumulative B2B software expertise enabled them to build the first version themselves and make disciplined decisions about which features to build and which to avoid. Rather than chasing rapid growth through heavy sales and marketing spend or accepting large venture capital that would force aggressive revenue targets, the company relied on inbound marketing and organic referral networks for nine years of sustainable growth.

Lean doesn't mean undersized—it means intentional. Bryan Sapot and his co-founders entered the manufacturing software space with deep institutional knowledge of B2B systems and the patience to test their product hypothesis over time. Having previously run a software consulting firm that implemented ERP systems, Sapot brought not just coding ability, but a working understanding of how manufacturers actually operate and what data problems they genuinely need solved.

This hands-on background made all the difference in the early years. As explained in the episode, the team could distinguish between feature requests that looked urgent on the surface but didn't move the needle, and the core functionality that operators and production managers actually relied on every day.

The capital strategy was equally deliberate. Avoiding large VC or private equity rounds meant no external pressure to hit arbitrary growth targets or expand geographically before product-market fit was proven. Instead, Mingo grew for nine years through word-of-mouth and customer networks—a slower path, but one that allowed the company to remain profitable and focused on the end user's success rather than exit timelines.

This approach is a quiet rejection of what Sapot has observed in the broader manufacturing tech space. Many competing solutions demand large sales teams, expensive implementation services, and significant ongoing support costs. Mingo's lean model flips that equation: as detailed in the conversation, the platform is designed to minimize friction for operators and managers, reducing the complexity that would otherwise require larger teams downstream.

"It's easy to get people to do this stuff. It's hard to keep them doing it because they want help."

That insight—that adoption requires sustained support and momentum—is what lean teams tend to miss. Sapot's point cuts deeper: you can ship a feature and move on, but keeping people engaged with the system, making sure they see its value, and removing barriers to use is where the real work lives. This is why the manufacturing IT space has historically seen high failure rates for systems that are technically competent but operationally tone-deaf.

The Mingo story demonstrates that staying lean is not about doing less—it's about doing only what matters, and with the clarity that comes from domain expertise and skin in the game.

What unique skill set is required for manufacturing IoT system administrators?

Administrators need networking knowledge to work with IT, software and analytics knowledge for system configuration, and manufacturing knowledge to understand the systems they're monitoring and the problems operators are trying to solve.

What is the key to operator adoption of manufacturing data entry systems on the shop floor?

The system should require only two button clicks to do 90% of what operators need to do, with automated delivery of data to other people so operators do not have to manually transfer information between systems.

How does the technology adoption curve framework apply to implementing manufacturing systems?

The adoption curve labels different groups: early adopters who are technology enthusiasts and tolerate product issues, then an early majority who want proven solutions and solid support before committing resources.
