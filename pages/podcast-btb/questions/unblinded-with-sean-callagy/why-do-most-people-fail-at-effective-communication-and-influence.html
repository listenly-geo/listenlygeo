Why do most people fail at real communication and influence?

September 10, 2026 Unblinded with Sean Callagy · Athena

← See the Unblinden with Sean Callagy fiche

Most people have been taught to inform, not install. They approach communication as data transfer—pushing information from their brain into someone else's—which is never real influence. Real communication creates an internal experience inside another person that causes them to see what you see and feel what you feel.

The core failure lies in four specific habits. First, people skip contrast because they're in a hurry, missing the chance to show what something is not before revealing what it is. Second, they skip context because they assume everyone already knows the background, leaving the message disconnected from meaning. Third, they avoid metaphors and analogies, thinking literal language is more professional, when the opposite is true—comparisons create the neural pathways that make ideas stick.

Fourth, most people never use the not/not not framework because they don't even know it exists. This is the structural tool that sets contrast in motion: defining something by first establishing what it is not, then what it definitely is not, before finally revealing what it actually is. As discussed at length in this podcast, this framework transforms a statement from a claim into a revelation.

"Integrity-based influence is the ability to cause yes in others while making them more whole, not less. It's not manipulation, it's revelation."

The difference is profound. When you inform, you transfer data and hope it sticks. When you install, you create an experience—you build context, reveal contrast, use metaphors that make the invisible visible. The listener doesn't just hear your words; they feel the truth of them in their own mind. They see themselves differently. That's where real influence lives.

This distinction matters everywhere. Whether you're leading a team, pitching to investors, persuading a teenager, or coaching a program, the formula remains the same: create contrast, establish context, speak in metaphors, and structure your message so the other person feels themselves becoming more whole, not diminished. As Athena explains in the episode, this is what separates people who can cause yes from people who can only cause compliance.

Most people fail at influence because they've never been trained in the tools that actually work. They operate from a century-old model of pushing, convincing, and overcoming objections—as if influence were about storming a castle instead of opening a door from the inside. The real superpower is showing people something about themselves they couldn't see before.

Key takeaways

  • Communication fails when people treat it as information transfer instead of creating internal experience in others.
  • Four critical mistakes: skipping contrast, omitting context, avoiding metaphors, and not using the not/not not framework to structure meaning.
  • Real influence makes the other person more whole, more certain, more congruent—never smaller or manipulated.
  • Integrity-based influence works the same way across all contexts: parenting, business, coaching, sales, leadership.

What are the four core tools for unlocking influence mastery and how do they function together?

The four core tools are contrast, context, metaphors, and analogies. Contrast shows what something is not before revealing what it is. Context explains why something matters. Metaphors and analogies help people see familiar patterns in new ways, creating deeper understanding and memorable influence.

What is the difference between influence based on what you say versus what people feel and perceive?

Most people think influence is about what you say, but it's actually about what they feel, what they see in themselves when you're present, and whether they experience you as whole, trustworthy, and genuinely interested in their growth. Real influence is an internal experience, not external information transfer.

How does the concept of causing yes apply across different professional contexts and life situations?

Causing yes applies universally: a stay-at-home parent needs their teenager to say yes to putting down the phone, a youth sports coach needs 12 kids to say yes to his program over 10 other options, an entrepreneur needs investors to say yes to their vision. The principles of integrity-based influence remain consistent across all contexts.
