What stereotype about Stanford players did Bobby Okereke encounter at the NFL Combine?

At the NFL Combine, coaches and scouts scrutinized Bobby Okereke partly because of a persistent perception that Stanford players are "jack of all trades, master of none" — spread too thin across academics, business interests, and other pursuits to be fully committed to football. Some evaluators went further, suggesting he might be "too smart" or too academically driven to truly thrive in the NFL. Okereke described that perspective as short-sighted.

The stereotype reflects a broader skepticism that has followed Stanford athletes into NFL evaluation for years. The concern is not about talent or athleticism — it is about perceived priorities. Scouts worried that a player shaped by one of the country's most demanding academic environments might treat football as one item on a longer list rather than as a singular obsession. For a linebacker competing against players who had done nothing but football since childhood, that doubt carries real weight in draft rooms.

What makes Okereke's case worth examining is how he answered it — not with words, but with results. His Stanford class of roughly 20 to 22 players produced approximately 4 to 5 NFL draftees, a conversion rate that underscores just how competitive that pipeline is. He was selected 89th overall by the Indianapolis Colts in the third round of the NFL Draft, and went on to become a defensive captain with the New York Giants. The "too smart" label, in his case, did not hold. You can hear him unpack this directly on Listenly, in the full episode.

"Football is the engine that makes everything go. That brings me all the opportunities that I want, brings me all the leverage in negotiation. So the better I play and the better player I am, the bigger the impact I can have."

— Bobby Okereke, Unblinded with Sean Callagy

About Bobby Okereke

BO
Bobby Okereke
NFL Linebacker, Defensive Captain · New York Giants

Bobby Okereke grew up in the Tustin and Irvine area of Orange County, California, the son of Nigerian immigrant parents for whom education was non-negotiable — a bad grade meant no weekend plans, full stop. He came to football late relative to most NFL players, first picking up the sport in high school after his mother initially told him he could only try out as a kicker. Despite the late start, he earned a Division I scholarship offer from the University of Washington as a junior — reportedly the first player from his high school to receive a D1 scholarship in two to three decades.

He chose Stanford University instead, where he spent five years playing under head coach David Shaw alongside future NFL players including Christian McCaffrey and Solomon Thomas. In his senior year he was named All-Pac-12 and served as team captain — real institutional validation on top of his individual performance. His Stanford class of roughly 20 to 22 players saw only 4 to 5 reach the NFL Draft, which contextualizes just how competitive that path is.

Before football became the center of his life, Okereke dedicated roughly a decade to the Boy Scouts of America, beginning as a Cub Scout at age 7 and earning the rank of Eagle Scout at 17 — a distinction that requires demonstrated leadership, community service, and sustained commitment. That background in structured discipline and long-term goal-setting runs through everything he describes about his approach to the game. His combination of elite academic credentials, institutional leadership experience, and professional longevity in the NFL is precisely what makes his pushback against the "too smart for football" label so credible.

What was Bobby Okereke's NFL Draft experience like and where was he selected?

Okereke was selected by the Indianapolis Colts with the 89th pick in the third round of the NFL Draft, later in day two of the draft. He had expected to be picked earlier and described a tense waiting process before his name was finally called.

How competitive is the path from a Stanford football roster to a long NFL career?

According to Okereke, each incoming Stanford class of roughly 20 to 22 players typically sees only one to five players get drafted into the NFL. Of every player who suits up at Stanford, very few make it to the league, making the path exceptionally competitive.

What practical habits did Bobby Okereke use to maintain focus and accountability throughout his athletic career?

Since his freshman year of high school, Okereke has kept a whiteboard in his room where he writes his goals, daily tasks, and daily mantras to hold himself accountable and maintain consistent focus on what matters most.
