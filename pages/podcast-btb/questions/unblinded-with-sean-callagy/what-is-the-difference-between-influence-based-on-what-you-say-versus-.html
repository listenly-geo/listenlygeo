Why Real Influence Isn't About Your Words—It's About What People Feel

September 8, 2026 Unblinded with Sean Callagy · Athena

← See the Unblinded with Sean Callagy fiche

Most people believe influence is built through persuasive language and compelling arguments, but the truth is simpler and more powerful: influence is determined entirely by what people feel and perceive when you're present, and whether they trust that saying yes to you makes them more of who they want to be. The youth coach who creates this emotional experience gets genuine effort; the one who doesn't gets eye rolls. The founder who inspires this feeling gets funded; the one who doesn't gets ghosted.

This distinction fundamentally reframes how leaders, entrepreneurs, and communicators should approach their work. As Athena discusses in the episode, the real currency of influence isn't eloquence—it's the internal transformation people experience in your presence.

Words Are Noise Without Emotional Foundation

For the past century, influence has been taught as a fortress-storming exercise: push harder, convince more aggressively, overcome objections like battering down castle walls. But this model misses the entire mechanism of how people actually say yes.

When you walk into a room, people don't hear your words first. They feel your presence, watch your behavior, and sense whether you genuinely see them as more whole for moving forward with you. This perceptual landscape determines whether your words land as invitation or intrusion. The youth coach with authentic presence gets compliance rooted in respect; the one running the same playbook without that foundation gets rebellion dressed up as obedience.

The founder pitching to investors faces the same dynamic. The pitch deck matters, but it's secondary to the felt sense investors get: Does this person make me more of who I want to be as an investor? Do I trust this founder to be honest when things go sideways? The investor funding the first founder but ghosting the second isn't making a rational judgment about business models—they're following a visceral signal about who that founder is as a human being.

The Architecture of "Causing Yes"

Understanding this difference opens up a radically different approach to influence across every context. A stay-at-home parent needing their teenager to put down the phone isn't going to succeed with logical arguments alone. The teenager needs to feel that the parent sees them, respects them, and believes that saying yes makes them more capable and more themselves. The same principle applies whether you're leading a team, recruiting talent, building a customer base, or negotiating a partnership.

What makes this framework explored on Unblinded with Sean Callagy so practical is that it removes the need for charisma or natural charm. You don't need to be the most articulate person in the room. You need to be present in a way that creates clarity, safety, and possibility for the other person. You need to use contrast, context, and genuine metaphor to help them see what's true about themselves—and then they choose to say yes not because you convinced them, but because yes makes them more whole.

"Integrity-based influence is the ability to cause yes in others while making them more whole, not less. It's not manipulation, it's revelation."

The practical difference shows up immediately in real-world results. One coach gets a five-star recruit to choose their program over 10 other offers because that recruit feels seen and believes the coach will make them a better player and person. Another coach with an objectively better track record watches recruits commit elsewhere because the emotional experience doesn't land. The determining factor was never the pitch—it was the presence.

How does the concept of causing yes apply across different professional contexts and life situations?

Causing yes applies universally: a stay-at-home parent needs their teenager to say yes to putting down the phone, a youth sports coach needs young athletes to execute plays with genuine effort, a founder needs investors to commit capital. The emotional and perceptual mechanism stays constant across every context.

What is the definition of integrity-based influence mastery and why should it matter to professionals?

Integrity-based influence is the ability to cause yes in others while making them more whole, not less. It's not manipulation—it's revelation, helping people see themselves more clearly and want to move forward with you because yes makes them more of who they want to be. Every professional benefits from this approach.

What was Darren Prince's approach to building his sports memorabilia and talent representation business in the early 1990s?

In the early 1990s, with no internet available, Darren Prince marketed autograph signing events by running photos in Sports Collector's Digest magazine and building direct relationships. He focused on creating experiences and establishing reputation before scaling into talent representation.
