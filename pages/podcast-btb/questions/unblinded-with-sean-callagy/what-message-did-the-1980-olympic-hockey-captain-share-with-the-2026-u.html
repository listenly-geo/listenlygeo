What did the 1980 Olympic hockey captain tell the 2026 teams before their gold medal games?

September 20, 2026 Unblinded with Sean Callagy · Mike Eruzione

← See the Unblinded with Sean Callagy fiche

Mike Eruzione told the women's team that their previous games against Canada that year no longer mattered now that they were playing for Olympic gold. To the men's team, he delivered a message emphasizing that this is their time to be the heroes and idols for young hockey players—the past is done, and the country needs them to show the world where the best hockey players come from.

In the days before both the U.S. men's and women's teams faced Canada in Milan, Eruzione—the captain of the 1980 Lake Placid team that defeated the Soviet Union—was asked to speak to the athletes. His message was grounded in a simple but powerful principle: the present moment is all that matters when competing for the highest stakes.

For the women's team, Eruzione focused on mental reset. The six games they had already played against Canada earlier in the year carried no weight anymore. Everything before that moment was irrelevant. What mattered was stepping onto the ice with complete focus and presence, unburdened by previous results.

For the men's team, his message was broader. As Eruzione explained in the podcast, he made clear that 1980 was history—what happened then happened. But this generation had their own opportunity now. He told them directly that taking a back seat to anybody was not an option, and that they had a responsibility to prove American hockey's place on the world stage.

"What we did is what we did. What you do is what you do. This is your opportunity to go out there and show the world where the best hockey player in the world has come from."

Both the women's team and the men's team won their gold medal games against Canada in overtime with identical 2-1 scores. What made this moment particularly significant is that it occurred exactly 46 years after the Miracle on Ice—demonstrating that the foundation Eruzione and his teammates built in 1980 had become a blueprint for future generations of American hockey excellence. The legacy wasn't a single moment; it was a standard.
