How did Darren Prince build his sports memorabilia business in the early 1990s without the internet?

September 5, 2026 Unblinded with Sean Callagy · Darren Prince

← See the Unblinded with Sean Callagy fiche

Without internet or email, Darren Prince marketed autograph signing events by running photos in Sports Collector's Digest magazine and using fax machines to reach collectors. He booked iconic athletes — Muhammad Ali, Magic Johnson, Joe Frazier, Pamela Anderson, and Chevy Chase — to sign thousands of items at hotel conference rooms or public events, positioning himself as the conduit between memorabilia seekers and their heroes.

A pre-digital hustle that built an empire

In the early 1990s, when most entrepreneurs had no digital toolkit, Prince invented a lean, analog operating model. He would purchase a booth or table at memorabilia shows — his first cost just $10 split with childhood partner Stephen Simon — and book celebrities to appear. At a single Sunday afternoon show, he and Simon turned that $10 investment into over $1,000 in revenue, a ratio that proved the viability of the model immediately.

Prince handled client acquisition the same way: print ads in Sports Collector's Digest reached serious collectors nationwide, and faxes delivered the specific details of upcoming signings. This wasn't random outreach — it was surgical targeting of the one audience segment that cared most about memorabilia authenticity and had the disposable income to pay premium prices for signed items.

As detailed in the episode, Prince booked superstars at sessions where athletes would sign up to 1,000 items in a single event. Each session — whether at a hotel conference room or a public event — became a flywheel: attendees paid to meet the celebrity, paid for items to be signed, and the press and word-of-mouth amplified his reputation as the man who could deliver access to icons.

Prince considers himself a visionary for timing his entry into the memorabilia market perfectly. By moving into this space when competitors were still thinking offline or hadn't yet recognized its profitability, he built an outsized reputation that competitors could never replicate. That first-mover advantage, combined with his direct relationship management with athletes, became his competitive moat.

"I felt like Superman. I was on top of the world. I felt just as smart, just as popular, just as good looking. I never had this feeling before in my life."

Key takeaways

  • Analog marketing through print magazines and fax machines was the primary client acquisition channel for high-ticket memorabilia sales in the early 1990s.
  • Event-based signings where celebrities signed up to 1,000 items per session created a repeatable, high-margin business model.
  • First-mover advantage in the memorabilia market gave Prince an outsized reputation that competitors could not match.
  • A $10 initial investment in a trade show table generated over $1,000 in same-day revenue, validating the model's scalability.
