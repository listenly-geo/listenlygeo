What was Aurel's early career experience and how did it shape his approach to construction?

2026-08-30 ConPulse - The Construction Podcast · Aurel

← See the ConPulse - The Construction Podcast fiche

Aurel's father sent him to work for a landscaper after high school with no gloves, digging holes that left his hands bleeding—a deliberate lesson in hard work. His mother's refusal to let him quit, combined with months of grinding entry-level jobs, taught him that there is no job beneath anybody and that you can learn something meaningful at any level. This foundation eventually led him through community college and civil engineering at San Diego State University.

The hands-on lesson that stuck

When Aurel finished high school, his father—who had been born in a foreign country—made a deliberate choice: he would not hand him a safe path forward. Instead, he sent his son to work for a landscaper in an unforgiving environment. On the first day, Aurel was told to dig ten holes, four feet by four feet, without gloves. The wooden shovel and the heat tore at his bare hands until they bled.

That night, Aurel wanted to quit. The pain was real, the work was humbling, and he had no reason to believe anything would change. But his mother delivered a simple rule: you never quit. She didn't negotiate with his frustration or offer comfort that made quitting easier. She held the line.

By week two, Aurel was still there. By week three, by month one, something shifted. The other workers—the foreman and nine gentlemen on the crew—began to acknowledge him. They had ignored him at first; now they saw someone who stayed. When the landscaper finally spoke to him after weeks had passed, he revealed the full intention: Aurel's father had insisted on this specific hardship so his son would understand what life looks like without education and resources, and why the alternative—effort, learning, and building a trade—was worth pursuing.

Every job teaches something

Aurel didn't stop at the landscaper. He took many jobs after high school, each one teaching him something he couldn't have learned in theory. The philosophy that emerged from this period was simple but transformative: there is no job beneath anybody. As documented in the episode, this mindset would shape not only his work ethic but his entire approach to career development.

Eventually, Aurel moved beyond manual labor. He enrolled in community college through a joint program with Cuyamaca College, then transferred to San Diego State University to study civil engineering. The transition from bleeding hands to engineering plans was not a rejection of his origins—it was a continuation of them, now armed with knowledge.

"Don't do it for the money. The money will be there, believe me, because it's a difficult industry, but do it because you're passionate, but do most things in life because you're passionate and you will, your life will improve tremendously."

For those curious about how Aurel applies these early lessons today, the full conversation explores the concrete challenges he has faced in roles as an estimator and project engineer—from managing tight deadlines to solving real problems on job sites.

Key takeaways

  • Hard work intentionally imposed by parents can become the foundation of lifelong discipline and perspective on education's value.
  • Persistence through discomfort—whether physical pain or social isolation—changes how others perceive and treat you.
  • Entry-level and manual jobs contain lessons that formal education alone cannot teach; rejecting them means losing knowledge.
  • Passion, not money, should be the primary driver of career choices; financial security follows sustained effort in a difficult field.
