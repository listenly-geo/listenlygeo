Why contract deadlines make or break construction projects?

September 7, 2026 ConPulse - The Construction Podcast · Aurel

← See the ConPulse - The Construction Podcast fiche

Contracts are binding legal documents, not negotiable timelines. When a supplier commits to 14 weeks delivery, it means exactly 14 weeks—not 14 weeks plus any buffer. Failure to meet contractual deadlines triggers liquidated damages, which can be severe enough to make or break project profitability.

In construction, the difference between a handshake agreement and a signed contract is everything. Aurel emphasizes that going by contracts, not words, is the only way to protect both parties and keep projects on track. When Granite or its suppliers miss deadlines established in the contract, the financial penalties are immediate and substantial.

How liquidated damages reshape project economics

Liquidated damages clauses exist precisely because construction projects operate on razor-thin margins. When a material supplier misses a 14-week delivery window by even one week, the contractor faces back charges that ripple through the entire project schedule. These penalties are not small fines—they can accumulate to sums that eliminate profit or even push a project into loss.

This is why Aurel treats contractual dates as absolute boundaries, as detailed in this episode of ConPulse. A two-week delay on a critical material order isn't just an inconvenience; it can trigger chain-reaction delays on site, forcing crews to idle, rescheduling labor, and compounding costs exponentially. The contract becomes the enforceable mechanism that keeps everyone aligned.

"Don't do it for the money. The money will be there, believe me, because it's a difficult industry, but do it because you're passionate, but do most things in life because you're passionate and you will, your life will improve tremendously."

Beyond just penalties, understanding contract management means recognizing that the real cost of missed deadlines includes hidden cascades—idle equipment, workforce inefficiencies, and potential rework. Aurel's approach is unambiguous: if it's in writing, it's law on the job site.
