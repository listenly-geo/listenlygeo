What environmental compliance best practice does Aurel recommend to construction professionals?

September 8, 2026 ConPulse - The Construction Podcast · Aurel

← See the ConPulse - The Construction Podcast fiche

Obtain a QSP (Qualified SWPPP Practitioner) certification and draft weekly BMP (Best Management Practice) reports in coordination with a consultant—this approach saves money and represents industry best practices. Understanding environmental regulations from the outset prevents costly problems down the line.

Environmental compliance often feels like an afterthought on construction sites, but Aurel frames it differently: viewing environmental regulations as strategic tools rather than bureaucratic obstacles changes everything. As he explains in the episode, this perspective shift alone can help teams avoid expensive mistakes.

QSP Certification: The Foundation of Compliance

The first concrete step Aurel recommends is straightforward: pursue QSP certification. A Qualified SWPPP Practitioner holds the technical knowledge needed to oversee Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plans on construction sites—a critical regulatory requirement in most jurisdictions. This certification isn't prohibitively expensive, making it an accessible investment for any serious construction professional.

Once certified, Aurel stresses the importance of taking certification responsibilities seriously. A QSP isn't just a credential to display; it's an active role requiring ongoing attention to site conditions and regulatory compliance throughout the project lifecycle.

Weekly BMP Reports: Documentation as Defense

Weekly Best Management Practice reports are non-negotiable, according to Aurel. These reports document the measures in place to prevent stormwater pollution, track site conditions, and demonstrate proactive management to regulators. The key detail here is consistency: reports must be drafted every week, not sporadically.

Aurel recommends never doing this work in isolation. Coordination with a qualified environmental consultant brings expertise and ensures compliance with local and federal regulations. While this adds a cost, it pales in comparison to fines, project delays, or liability from environmental violations. As detailed in this podcast discussion, the expense of compliance is negligible next to the expense of non-compliance.

SWPPP (Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan): A site-specific plan required by the Clean Water Act to control stormwater runoff and minimize erosion, sediment, and pollutant discharge during construction. A QSP (Qualified SWPPP Practitioner) is the certified professional responsible for overseeing and documenting these controls.

Why does Aurel emphasize understanding regulations rather than simply following a checklist? Because regulations exist to solve real problems—protecting waterways, preventing erosion, managing runoff. When you understand the "why," you make better decisions on site and anticipate problems before they arise. This knowledge compounds: the longer you work within environmental frameworks, the more intuitive compliance becomes.

"Don't do it for the money. The money will be there, believe me, because it's a difficult industry, but do it because you're passionate."

How does contract management impact project delivery timelines and costs?

Aurel emphasizes going based on contracts, not words. If a contract states delivery in 14 weeks, he requires it in 14 weeks—not 14 weeks plus additional time. Clear contractual language protects both parties and keeps projects on schedule.

What technology tool does Aurel recommend for earthwork modeling and why is it challenging?

Aurel recommends learning AgTech, an earthwork modeling program, because it is the most challenging software he has ever worked with. Mastering it gives professionals a competitive edge in site planning and cost estimation.

How did Aurel and his team resolve a storm drain mainline design conflict?

The original design called for a storm drain mainline at a slope of 0.25% to 0.33%, but it would have run into an electrical conduit bank, creating a conflict that required creative problem-solving and coordination with multiple stakeholders.
