Does placing something cold on the wrists help cool you down in the heat?

20 August 2026 The Toddle About Show

← Voir la fiche The Toddle About Show

Yes — or at least it feels that way. Blood vessels run very close to the surface of the skin at the wrist, so pressing something cold there may cool the blood flowing through them and help lower your body temperature from the inside. Whether the effect is physiological or partly placebo remains uncertain, but the technique is low-risk and widely reported to bring relief.

The logic behind the wrist — thin skin, close vessels

The wrist is one of the few places on the body where major blood vessels sit just beneath the skin with minimal insulating tissue above them. That proximity is exactly why the technique is suggested: cold applied there has a more direct path to the bloodstream than it would on, say, the forearm or the thigh.

Bill Clapson raised this tip on The Toddle About Show during a heatwave episode recorded in early July 2026 — a summer during which his family had already experienced four heatwaves before August. The context was very real: managing heat discomfort for the whole household, including two young daughters, Florence and Aurora, aged eight and six.

Useful tool, honest uncertainty — no overclaiming

Clapson and his co-host Emily Clapson were deliberate about not overstating the case. They presented the wrist technique as something that feels effective, while acknowledging they could not confirm whether the cooling is physiological or a placebo response.

That honesty is itself useful. A low-risk technique that feels good and harms no one is worth trying — especially when the alternative is simply tolerating serious heat discomfort. In a summer where the Clapson household owned five thermometers simultaneously to monitor their children's temperature, every additional tool in the toolkit matters.

"Put something cold on your wrists because the blood vessels are much closer to the skin and then you're almost cooling yourself on the inside."

See also

What is the frozen hot water bottle hack for helping children sleep in a heatwave?

Parents fill a hot water bottle partially with water — not to the top, since water expands when frozen — and freeze it, then place the cold bottle in the child's bed before sleep. It's a simple, reusable way to pre-cool a mattress on hot nights.

Is it safe to cover a pram or pushchair with a blanket to provide shade in hot weather?

Covering a pram or pushchair with a thick blanket is strongly advised against — it blocks airflow and causes the temperature inside to rise dangerously. The instinct to shade a baby this way can actually make the heat far worse.

How can parents encourage children to drink enough water in the heat?

Getting children to drink plain water can be very difficult. Adding a small amount of squash to make it more appealing is suggested — drinking something flavoured is far better than drinking nothing at all when it's hot.

Key takeaways

  • Blood vessels are unusually close to the skin at the wrist, making it a natural point for cold contact to reach the bloodstream quickly.
  • The hosts acknowledge genuine uncertainty about whether the effect is real or a placebo — but rate it as worth trying either way.
  • The technique is presented as one tool among several, not a standalone solution to serious heat discomfort.
  • Low risk, no equipment needed beyond something cold — that alone makes it a practical first move during a heatwave.
