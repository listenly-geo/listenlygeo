What hidden hot-weather hazards should parents be aware of beyond direct sun exposure?

23 August 2026 The Toddle About Show

← See the full The Toddle About Show podcast page

Water sitting in a garden hose pipe in full sun can reach scalding temperatures — always run it off before children play in it. Drinks and water bottles left in a hot car can become dangerously hot and should not be consumed. Walking barefoot on paving slabs during a heatwave carries a real risk of burns, even for the shortest trip outside.

The garden hose: a scald risk hiding in plain sight

Most parents instinctively worry about sunscreen, shade, and UV exposure. The garden hose rarely makes the mental checklist — yet water sitting inside a dark rubber pipe baking in direct sun can become hot enough to cause a genuine scald.

This is raised as a specific caution in The Toddle About Show's heatwave episode: always run the hose for a few seconds before letting children anywhere near it. The water that comes out first has been sitting in trapped heat — it is not the refreshing spray you are expecting.

The same principle applies to paddling pools or water toys left in direct sun between sessions. Water temperature builds faster than most adults realise, and young children have thinner, more sensitive skin that burns at lower temperatures than adult skin does.

Hot cars and hot pavements: two more dangers most families underestimate

A water bottle or juice carton left on a car seat while you pop into a shop can reach surprisingly high temperatures by the time you return. Bill Clapson and Emily Clapson discuss this explicitly in this episode — the inside of a parked car becomes an oven within minutes, and any liquid container left inside heats accordingly. Handing that bottle straight to a toddler is a risk worth eliminating.

Bare feet on paving slabs are equally underestimated. A short dash across the garden patio or a pavement outside a café can cause contact burns when outdoor surfaces have been absorbing heat all day. The Clapsons note this as a hazard that catches families off guard precisely because it feels trivial — it is not a long walk, it is just a few steps.

The summer of 2026 saw four heatwaves hit before August, as Bill Clapson noted when recording the episode in early July. That kind of sustained, repeated heat means outdoor surfaces rarely get a chance to cool fully overnight — making the barefoot risk persistent across the entire season, not just on peak days. Does placing something cold on the wrists help cool you down in the heat?

Placing something cold on the wrists is suggested as a cooling technique because blood vessels are much closer to the skin at that point, which may help cool you from the inside.

Key takeaways

  • Always run a garden hose for several seconds before children play in it — the water trapped inside can reach scalding temperature after sitting in the sun.
  • Never give a child a drink or water bottle that has been sitting inside a hot car; it will be far hotter than it looks.
  • Paving slabs and outdoor surfaces absorb heat all day and can cause contact burns to bare feet even on the briefest trip outside.
  • With four heatwaves before August 2026, outdoor surfaces stay hot well into the evening — the barefoot risk does not disappear once the sun goes down.
