How does Rugby Tots use imaginative play to develop physical skills in young children?

16 September 2026 The Toddle About Show · Tom Brown

← See The Toddle About Show fiche

Rugby Tots frames movement drills as imaginative adventures—children stomp like dinosaurs over hurdles to build knee-lifting and body control, or catch and pass balls framed as frogs jumping onto lily pads instead of formal throwing drills. This imaginative framing makes skill development feel like pure play, so children absorb fundamental physical abilities without realising they're learning.

The method works because young children are naturally drawn to narrative and storytelling. When a movement activity is wrapped in a compelling image—a dinosaur, a frog, a jumping animal—the child's imagination takes over, and the body follows naturally. There's no pressure, no sense of "drilling," just play with an adult they trust.

As discussed in this episode of The Toddle About Show, Rugby Tots structures every session with a clear spine—a starting activity, a catch and pass game, a movement skill challenge like an obstacle course, a kicking activity, and a finishing game. But within that spine, every activity is given an imaginative hook that turns repetition into exploration.

The real power lies in repetition across a two-week cycle. Children see the same activity framework one week, then return the next week already familiar with it—but the imaginative framing may shift slightly, or they may discover new ways to move through it. This repetition builds competence without boredom because the narrative keeps changing.

Parents are part of this too. Tom Brown emphasizes that grown-ups join the play, moving and imagining alongside their children. This turns a skill-building session into a shared family experience where confidence grows naturally—not because someone told a child they were learning, but because they played together and succeeded.

"We want to build confidence in young children, we want to give opportunity for those lightbulb moments, we want to work on movement literacy to really develop a really supportive platform that children can grow in without really knowing what they're developing."

The broader philosophy behind this approach is rooted in movement literacy—the foundation of physical confidence that children carry into sport, dance, and everyday life. Every hurdle becomes a dinosaur's stride, every catch becomes a creature's leap. Tom Brown explains in the full podcast episode how this imaginative scaffolding lets children build these skills without self-consciousness—which is exactly when young children learn fastest.

What does a typical Rugby Tots session look like and how is it structured?

Rugby Tots sessions follow a structured spine: a starting activity, a catch and pass activity or game, a movement skill such as an obstacle course or relay, a kicking activity or game, and a finishing activity or game. This structure gives children familiarity and a learning framework while keeping each week's actual game a surprise.

What hidden hot-weather hazards should parents be aware of beyond direct sun exposure?

Water left in a garden hose pipe that has been sitting in the sun can become dangerously hot, hot enough to scald, and should always be run off before use on children or pets.

What extra precautions should pregnant or breastfeeding women take during a heatwave?

Pregnant and breastfeeding women are advised to prioritise hydration above all else, as they require more water than usual even outside of hot weather.
