How did Kim Porter manage imposter syndrome across a 40-year engineering career — and did it ever go away?

Kim Porter describes her imposter syndrome as following a downward trajectory over time — much like a declining stock market chart. Early in her career she internalized it and assumed she was the only one struggling. But each stretch role she successfully completed chipped away at it, and by the end of her 40-year career the self-doubt had largely subsided — replaced, in the end, by a different frustration: that people were no longer listening to her proven expertise.

What makes Porter's account particularly striking is the context in which that imposter syndrome developed. For nearly her entire career, she was the only woman in all-male engineering environments — a ratio that, in mechanical engineering, has barely shifted in four decades. When Porter was a student at Iowa State University in the early 1980s, the field was roughly 1 woman for every 9 men. That ratio remains the same today. Navigating that environment in isolation, with no visible peers experiencing the same thing, made early self-doubt feel uniquely hers — even when it wasn't. The pattern Porter identifies — imposter syndrome fading through accumulated evidence rather than through mindset work alone — mirrors what Sheryl Sandberg described in Lean In: the inner voice that insists you are not good enough eventually loses credibility when the track record becomes undeniable. For Porter, that track record was built across decades, from developing next-generation spacesuit gloves in the wake of the 1986 Challenger disaster, to product design at Nike, to building her own consultancy and eventually HatchOne, an online engineering community with a dedicated Women in Engineering group. Each successful role was, in effect, data she could no longer ignore.

"No opinions, all data, no emotion. Here's the data, here are the results, this is my recommendation — and then you don't say anything. Silence is power."

— Kim Porter, Ordinary To Badass, episode 452

About Kim Porter

KP
Kim Porter Engineer, Founder, and Community Builder HatchOne

Kim Porter earned an engineering science and mechanics degree with an emphasis in biomedical engineering from Iowa State University in the early 1980s — at a time when biomedical engineering did not yet exist as a recognized standalone discipline. That early positioning at the edge of an emerging field set the tone for a career defined by firsts and by working without a map.

Within one month of the January 1986 Challenger disaster, Porter joined the team working on next-generation spacesuit gloves — her first major professional role, immediately operating at the intersection of human safety and engineering precision. She later moved into product design at Nike, bringing the same rigor to consumer applications, before spending the bulk of her career running Kim Porter Consultancy as an independent engineer.

Throughout all of it — across every position she held — Porter was the only woman in the room. That lived experience of near-total professional isolation, sustained over four decades in an industry where the gender ratio in mechanical engineering has remained unchanged at roughly one woman to nine men, is precisely what makes her account of imposter syndrome so credible and so specific. She is not describing a theory; she is describing what actually happened, year by year, as the evidence of her own competence accumulated.

Porter has since founded HatchOne, an online engineering community that includes a Women in Engineering subgroup. Now semi-retired from full-time work, she continues to lead the community and her consultancy — teaching the next generation of engineers things, as she puts it, that nobody taught her.
