How can someone identify that they are ready for a promotion even when they doubt themselves?

September 4, 2026 Ordinary To Badass

← See the Ordinary To Badass fiche

Self-doubt does not mean you are not ready. Look for external signals: your boss actively encouraging you to apply, receiving responsibilities others are not getting, and genuine support from coworkers. These indicators matter far more than your internal confidence level.

Most people wait until a promotion opening appears before they begin preparing. By then, it is already too late. The real preparation happens months in advance—when you are taking on tasks others are not, when your boss is nudging you toward advancement, and when your team is rooting for your success. These are the signs that count.

Self-doubt is remarkably common among capable professionals. As an example, Sheryl Sandberg did not feel qualified for her role when she first took it on—yet her family members pushed her to pursue it anyway, and she became one of the most recognized leaders in tech. Her internal doubt bore no relationship to her actual readiness.

Your boss's encouragement is the clearest signal

When your supervisor is actively encouraging you to apply for a promotion, they see something you may not see in yourself. They work with you daily and understand your capabilities. That encouragement is not casual—it is evidence. A boss who invests time in pushing you forward has already assessed your readiness; they are not wasting effort on someone unprepared.

This signal often appears before the job opens publicly, as discussed in detail across the Ordinary To Badass podcast episodes on promotion strategy. Pay attention to whether your leadership is giving you extra visibility, assigning you stretch projects, or having conversations about your career path. These are the moments that matter.

You are receiving responsibilities others are not

If you are given tasks or projects that your peers at your current level do not receive, your organization is testing you for the next level. This informal trial run is how organizations quietly prepare people for promotion. You are not imagining it—management is intentionally seeing if you can handle more.

These expanded responsibilities often come without an official title change or fanfare. But they are real tests of whether you belong in a higher role. If you are consistently trusted with work above your current pay grade, that is a concrete indicator that readiness is already present.

Coworker support tells you something about your impact

When colleagues are encouraging you to go for a promotion, they are reflecting back what they see: your competence, your reliability, your worth to the team. This peer validation is especially significant because it comes with no agenda—they are not trying to advance you for their benefit; they simply see that you belong at the next level.

Before you apply, listen for this encouragement. Do teammates ask you for advice? Do they speak up for you in meetings? Do they suggest you should be in the running? These signals often arrive before you even consider applying. As Marie Sonneman explains in the episode, the people around you frequently recognize your readiness before you do.

"If you spend this whole time getting all your ducks in a row and getting ready and talking to mentors and getting this training that you need, that is what makes you the obvious choice for a promotion."

Preparation is the bridge between doubt and confidence. You do not need to feel certain about your readiness—you need evidence of it. The evidence comes from what others see in you, not from how you feel about yourself. When your boss believes in you, when your work demonstrates growth, and when your peers support your advancement, that is your readiness made visible.

One detail that often goes overlooked: the host addresses a striking gender gap in promotion confidence, noting that men tend to apply for promotions when they feel approximately 60% ready, while women often wait until they feel 80–90% ready. This gap rarely reflects actual readiness—it reflects confidence patterns. Knowing this pattern can help you recognize whether self-doubt is a real barrier or simply a familiar habit.

Key takeaways

  • Your boss actively encouraging you to apply is a clear sign of readiness, regardless of how you feel internally.
  • Receiving responsibilities others at your level do not receive signals that management is preparing you for advancement.
  • Peer support and encouragement from colleagues reflect genuine recognition of your capability and impact.
  • Self-doubt is common even among highly qualified professionals and does not indicate lack of readiness.

What should you do if a promotion opens up before you feel ready?

The host advises going for it anyway, arguing that applying early still provides valuable experience and puts candidates ahead of those who have not yet started preparing.

Should you apply for a promotion even if you do not meet 100% of the qualifications?

Yes — the host emphasizes that not meeting 100% of listed qualifications should not disqualify a candidate from applying, unless requirements are strictly mandatory.

How can someone build a mentor relationship without formally asking someone to be their mentor?

Instead of directly asking someone to be a mentor, the host recommends asking a respected colleague for advice on a specific topic, implementing that advice, and reporting back on the results.
