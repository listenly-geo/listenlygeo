Why does excellent work behind the scenes not automatically lead to recognition and promotion?

2026-09-11 Ordinary To Badass

← See the Ordinary To Badass fiche

Senior leadership only sees the final PowerPoint, not the disaster you prevented or the employee you stopped from quitting. Because your competence makes half of what you do become invisible—people stop noticing the absence of problems. You must actively document your wins and give context about your role and impact to build reputation and trust.

The invisibility of competence

When you excel at managing complexity behind the scenes, you erase the evidence of your work from sight. The crisis that never materialized, the six months you spent developing someone into a stronger contributor, the systemic flaw you caught before it reached production—these victories leave no visible trace.

Leadership operates on what they can observe in the moment. A polished presentation lands on an executive's desk, and they see only the surface output. The groundwork, the relationship-building, the invisible labor that made that outcome possible remains entirely unknown to them. This isn't malice or oversight—it's simply how attention works at scale.

As discussed in Ordinary To Badass, this creates a paradox: your excellence actually works against your visibility. The more competent you are at solving problems quietly, the fewer problems senior leaders perceive, and the less they understand the value you're generating.

Documentation as a visibility strategy

The solution is not to do less excellent work or to wait for recognition to arrive on its own. Instead, you must intentionally document your impact and communicate the context of your results. This isn't bragging—it's building an accurate record of what you do.

Share the scope of what you managed, not to seek praise but to establish clarity about your contribution. Explain the challenges you navigated, the people you developed, the systems you improved. Give senior leaders the visibility they need to understand the full picture of your role.

A key insight from the episode reinforces this approach: being right and being influential are not the same thing. You can do excellent work perfectly and still persuade nobody of its value if you never make that work visible. Documentation closes that gap.

This practice serves a dual purpose: it protects your reputation as advancement decisions are made, and it builds trust between you and senior leadership by giving them concrete evidence of your judgment and results.

What is Hedy Lamarr's contribution to wireless communication technology and how was it recognized?

During World War II, Hedy Lamarr worked with composer George Antheal on a frequency hopping system for radio-controlled torpedoes that was patented in 1942. This technology later became the foundation for secure military communications and modern wireless systems.

How can workplace categories and reputation limit career advancement opportunities?

Once people have categorized you in the workplace, their brains stop questioning that categorization. If you are seen as the technical expert but want to become a leader, that existing perception becomes a barrier to advancement, regardless of your actual capabilities.

Why is building career leverage more powerful than making yourself valuable to a single organization?

If you build all your value inside one system, one boss controls your opportunities, one organization controls your title, and one person controls whether you advance. Career leverage diversifies your power across multiple relationships and opportunities.

Key takeaways

  • Excellence creates invisibility: when you prevent problems and manage complexity well, senior leaders see no evidence of the work required.
  • Documentation is not optional: you must actively communicate the scope, context, and results of your contribution to build accurate reputation.
  • Visibility drives influence: being right is not enough—people must understand your role and impact in order to trust and advance you.
