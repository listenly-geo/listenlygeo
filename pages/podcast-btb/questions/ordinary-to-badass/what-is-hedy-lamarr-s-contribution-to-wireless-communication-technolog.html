How Hedy Lamarr's Frequency Hopping Patent Built Modern Wireless Communication

September 10, 2026 Ordinary To Badass

← See the Ordinary To Badass fiche

During World War II, Hedy Lamarr partnered with composer George Antheal to develop a frequency hopping system patented in 1942 designed to make radio-controlled torpedoes resistant to detection and jamming. This breakthrough laid the technical foundation for modern secure wireless communication, though popular narratives often oversimplify her legacy by claiming she "invented Wi-Fi."

The frequency hopping concept was genuinely innovative for its time. Rather than transmitting on a single radio frequency—vulnerable to enemy interception—the system rapidly switched between multiple frequencies in a coordinated pattern that only the sender and receiver could follow. This made it nearly impossible for an adversary to jam or intercept the signal without knowing the hopping sequence in advance.

Lamarr's work with Antheal demonstrated that technological breakthroughs often require collaboration across disciplines. A composer and an actress might seem an unlikely pair to solve a military engineering problem, yet their combined perspective resulted in a patent that addressed a real wartime need. As explored in this episode of Ordinary To Badass, the story of how women navigate male-dominated fields often involves bringing unconventional thinking and courage to spaces where it isn't expected.

Recognition and the Gap Between Invention and Impact

The recognition of Lamarr's contribution has been uneven. The patent itself was filed and granted, creating an official record of her intellectual property. However, during the war years, the technology remained classified, limiting its immediate commercial application. It was not until decades later—well after the patent's expiration—that the underlying principles were widely adopted in military and civilian communications systems.

Claims that Lamarr "invented Wi-Fi" represent a misunderstanding of technical history. Her frequency hopping concept was indeed foundational to secure communication protocols, and modern technologies like Bluetooth and military spread-spectrum communications trace their lineage to her work. However, Wi-Fi as a specific standard emerged from much later research and engineering effort by many contributors. The oversimplification does her a disservice—her actual achievement was already remarkable without need for exaggeration.

What makes Lamarr's story compelling in a leadership context, as discussed in Ordinary To Badass, is that she moved beyond simply being talented or famous. She took action on a problem she identified, collaborated with the right expertise, and produced a tangible innovation. Yet the full scope of her work remained hidden from public view for years—a reminder that recognition is not automatic, even for genuine breakthroughs.
