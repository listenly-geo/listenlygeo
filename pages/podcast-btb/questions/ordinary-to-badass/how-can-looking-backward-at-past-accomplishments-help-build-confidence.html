How can looking backward at past accomplishments help build confidence when facing new challenges?

September 24, 2026 Ordinary To Badass · Bridget Truxillo

← See the Ordinary To Badass podcast fiche

When facing a new challenge, stop looking forward at the unknown and instead inventory what you've already survived—a divorce, the death of a loved one, difficult conversations at work. Those past victories are proof that you can handle hard things, and that evidence shifts your mindset from future anxiety to demonstrated resilience.

The shift is simple but powerful: when nervousness creeps in and you feel unprepared, your instinct is to focus on what might go wrong ahead. Instead, Bridget Truxillo advises turning your attention backward to moments when you thought something was impossible—and you did it anyway.

Every difficult thing you've survived leaves a mark, and that mark is concrete evidence of your capability. You didn't skip past those challenges—you went through them, learned from them, and came out the other side. That's not luck. That's resilience.

The goal is to treat your past wins as a reference library. When Bridget discusses confidence-building on this episode of Ordinary To Badass, she emphasizes that this backward look isn't about arrogance—it's about honest accounting. You've already proven you can do hard things. The hard thing in front of you now is just the next one on the list.

"You don't just get to skip the line or skip trying to get ready or the experiences that give you the confidence. You only ever feel ready by doing the thing."

The mechanics of this perspective matter. Confidence doesn't come from certainty about the future—it comes from certainty about your ability to survive what comes. You're not promising yourself the outcome will be perfect. You're reminding yourself that you've handled uncertainty before, and you can do it again.

This approach works because it's rooted in truth, not wishful thinking. You're not telling yourself you're unstoppable or that everything will work out. You're simply reviewing the evidence: the times you stayed in a difficult conversation instead of walking out, the moments you asked for help, the grief you moved through. Those are real accomplishments, and Truxillo's full conversation explores how this evidence builds genuine confidence rather than false bravado.

Key takeaways

  • Confidence is built by doing hard things, not by feeling ready before you start—look back at past difficulties you've survived as proof of your capability.
  • When facing new challenges, shift your focus from future anxiety to demonstrated resilience by reviewing what you've already accomplished.
  • Past victories—whether personal (surviving loss or divorce) or professional (speaking up in meetings, difficult conversations)—are concrete evidence you can handle hard things again.
  • This backward-looking perspective turns confidence from an emotional guess into a factual reference point based on real experience.
