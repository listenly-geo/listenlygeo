Why do women avoid reporting workplace misconduct or discrimination despite the mental health costs?

September 23, 2026 Ordinary To Badass · Bridget Truxillo

← See the Ordinary To Badass fiche

Women often fear being labeled "that girl"—the one who complains, reports issues, or becomes a target—and this silence carries a real cost to their mental health. Bridget Truxillo explains that she spent her entire law enforcement career trying to prove herself without speaking up about unfair treatment, a choice that eventually contributed to her leaving the field. For mental health reasons alone, she emphasizes that women must overcome this fear and speak up professionally.

The Cost of Staying Silent

During her time on the SWAT team, Truxillo noticed a stark pattern: she was being disciplined and singled out for mistakes that her male colleagues made without consequence. After two and a half years of absorbing this treatment without reporting it, she realized the toll was unsustainable. The belief that she had to prove her capability by weathering unfair treatment silently became a trap—one she now recognizes cost her years of psychological strain.

This dynamic reflects a broader pattern in male-dominated fields where women internalize the pressure to be "one of the guys" by never complaining, never appearing weak, and never drawing attention to discriminatory practices. As Truxillo discusses in the episode, this silence comes at a significant psychological cost that shapes careers and wellbeing long after the actual incidents occur.

Speaking Up Professionally Without Fear

The path forward, Truxillo argues, is not to stay silent for the sake of fitting in—it's to speak up professionally and respectfully. Speaking up for your own mental health is not the same as being unprofessional or emotional; it can be done calmly and clearly. She provides a direct example: "You've got to say something for your own mental health, and you can do it professionally: with all due respect, you know, I'm not going to do that."

This approach protects two things at once: your own mental health and your professional integrity. Rather than waiting until mistreatment has compounded over years, addressing unfair treatment early sets a boundary and signals that you will not accept it going forward. The fear of becoming "that girl" is real, but Truxillo's experience shows that the alternative—silence—costs far more.

Bridget Truxillo's story reveals a crucial detail that extends beyond this fiche: the episode covers how she later left civil litigation entirely to build a law firm specifically designed to support the very professionals who face these challenges—a personal mission born directly from her silence.

Key takeaways

  • Fear of being labeled "that girl" leads women to internalize mistreatment rather than report it, damaging mental health over time.
  • Silence in response to unfair discipline or discrimination is not protective—it reinforces the behavior and harms the individual.
  • Speaking up can be done professionally and calmly, without sacrificing respect or credibility in male-dominated fields.
  • Addressing mistreatment early sets a boundary and signals that you will not accept unfair treatment going forward.
