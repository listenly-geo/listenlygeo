At what percentage of readiness do men versus women typically apply for promotions?

30 August 2026 Ordinary To Badass

← See the Ordinary To Badass fiche

Men typically apply for a promotion when they feel approximately 60% ready, while women tend to wait until they feel 80 to 90% ready. This readiness gap directly undermines women's career progression and advancement opportunities.

The confidence gap that holds women back

This disparity reveals a fundamental confidence gap between genders when it comes to career advancement. Women's tendency to wait longer before applying isn't about lacking qualifications—it's about how they perceive their own readiness compared to men's self-assessment.

As Marie Sonneman explains in Ordinary To Badass, this hesitation costs women real opportunities. When a woman waits until she feels 80–90% ready, she's often already overqualified for the role. Meanwhile, a man who applies at 60% readiness moves forward with the same or greater actual capability—he's simply more willing to take the leap.

The host argues that feeling 60% ready likely means being closer to 80 or 90% ready in reality. The gap isn't about actual competence; it's about perception and willingness to take calculated risks.

Why preparation matters more than waiting

Rather than waiting until you feel "completely ready," the real strategy is to prepare strategically, as detailed in the Badass Promotion Blueprint. The host recommends starting preparation six to twelve months before a promotion is expected to open—not after it's announced.

This proactive approach flips the equation: instead of waiting until you meet every single criterion, you identify gaps early and close them deliberately. As discussed in this podcast, the women who advance are those who begin building their readiness long before the opportunity appears, then apply with confidence at the right moment—not when they've checked every box.

"If you spend this whole time getting all your ducks in a row and getting ready and talking to mentors and getting this training that you need, that is what makes you the obvious choice for a promotion."

See also

What is the H-P-B method for evaluating promotion readiness?

The H-P-B method involves reviewing each knowledge, skill, and ability listed in a job announcement and marking it with an H if you have it, a P if you can learn it, or a B if you need to build it.

How should candidates use a job announcement to prepare for a promotion?

Candidates should read the full job announcement to identify qualifications, preferred experience, required knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs), then map their own experience against these requirements.

When is the biggest mistake people make when preparing for a promotion?

Most people start preparing for a promotion once a job opening is announced or they hear someone is retiring. By that point it is way too late.

Key takeaways

  • Men apply for promotions at approximately 60% perceived readiness; women wait until 80–90%, creating a significant advancement gap.
  • Actual competence levels are often far closer than perceived readiness suggests—the difference is confidence and willingness to apply early.
  • Strategic preparation six to twelve months in advance, using tools like the H-P-B method, is more effective than waiting until you feel completely ready.
  • Women who advance are those who close readiness gaps proactively and apply with confidence before a promotion is publicly announced.
