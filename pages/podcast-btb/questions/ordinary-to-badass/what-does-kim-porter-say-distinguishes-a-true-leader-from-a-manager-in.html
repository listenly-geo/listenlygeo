What distinguishes a true leader from a manager in engineering environments, according to Kim Porter?

According to Kim Porter, the defining difference is psychological safety. A true leader openly owns their mistakes and actively creates an environment where their team can fail without consequences, while someone who micromanages, conceals errors, and claims credit for others' work is not a leader at all — they are simply someone who does not think well enough of themselves to lead authentically.

Porter grounds this distinction in the specific conditioning engineers receive. Engineers are trained from the start to be precise, accurate, and as close to perfect as possible — traits that serve technical problem-solving but can actively undermine leadership. To become an effective leader, she argues, engineers must consciously unlearn part of that training: the discomfort with being wrong in front of others is exactly what prevents them from building the trust that genuine leadership requires.

For Porter, this isn't abstract — it is the standard she has held herself to throughout a 40-year engineering career spent as the only woman in rooms full of men. She describes standing firm against directives that would have undermined her team's trust, telling supervisors directly that she would not act against the team she had spent years building a relationship with. That posture — confident, boundaried, and rooted in personal integrity — is what she means when she draws the line between managing and leading. About Kim Porter

KP
Kim Porter
Engineer, Founder, and Community Builder · HatchOne
Independent Consultant · Kim Porter Consultancy

Kim Porter earned her engineering science and mechanics degree with an emphasis in biomedical engineering from Iowa State University in the early 1980s — at a time when biomedical engineering was not yet a recognized discipline. Within one month of the January 1986 Challenger disaster, she joined the project developing next-generation spacesuit gloves, setting the tone for a career defined by high-stakes, precision-driven environments. She subsequently moved into product design at Nike before spending the remainder of her career in independent consultancy.

What makes Porter's perspective on engineering leadership particularly authoritative is the consistency of her experience: across every role and every employer over 40 years, she was the only woman in the room. Mechanical engineering, the field she navigated her entire career, has maintained a ratio of roughly one woman for every nine men — unchanged from when Porter was a student in the early 1980s. Her insight into what separates true leadership from mere management is not theoretical; it was forged in environments where the cost of getting that distinction wrong was immediate and professional.

Porter has since founded HatchOne, an online engineering community that includes a dedicated Women in Engineering subgroup, where she now transmits everything she was never taught. She has recently stepped back from full-time work while continuing to lead the community and run her consultancy.

What is Kim Porter's "silence is power" communication strategy in professional settings?

Kim Porter coaches that once you have presented a data-driven recommendation, you should stop talking completely and let silence sit. She explains that silence, in that moment, becomes a source of power rather than discomfort — a deliberate tool rather than a gap to fill.

How does Kim Porter recommend women engineers respond when told they are "too assertive" or "too aggressive" in performance reviews?

Kim Porter says early in her career she would apologize and promise to do better, but later she pushed back directly with facts — pointing out specific evidence that contradicted the characterization and refusing to accept a framing she had not earned.

What is Kim Porter's advice for early-career engineers choosing their first job?

Kim Porter warns that entry-level engineers who take a first job without researching whether it aligns with their interests risk being pigeonholed — for years, or even for their entire career — in a specialty they never actually chose.
