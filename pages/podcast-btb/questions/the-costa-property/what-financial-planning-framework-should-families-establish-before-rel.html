How should families calculate their monthly survival budget before moving abroad?

September 17, 2026 The Costa Property Podcast · Warner Unknown

← See the The Costa Property Podcast fiche

Families must establish a clear monthly breakeven point—the minimum income needed to survive in their new location—and ensure that figure comes in consistently through stable, contracted employment in the local social security system. Without this baseline number defined before the move, savings will dwindle far more rapidly than expected, leaving families vulnerable to unexpected setbacks.

The monthly breakeven point is not a luxury budget; it is the bare minimum required to cover housing, utilities, food, transportation, and other essential living costs in your target country. This calculation must be done before you relocate, not after, because it determines whether your savings and income will actually sustain your family long-term.

As Warner Unknown explains in his account of relocating to Spain, relying solely on savings without secured income is extremely risky. The family prioritized ensuring that at least one partner had stable, contracted permanent employment in the social security system, which provided both predictable monthly cash flow and legal security in their new country.

Employment security is as important as the number itself

A breakeven budget is only meaningful if your income is reliable. Freelance work, seasonal employment, or uncontracted informal income creates uncertainty—especially in a foreign country where you may face language barriers, unfamiliar labor laws, and visa restrictions.

When the family made their move from Ireland to the Costa del Sol in 2016, they structured their finances so that one person held permanent, contracted employment in the local social security system. This provided not only guaranteed monthly income but also access to healthcare, pension contributions, and legal residency protection—three critical safety nets that casual work cannot provide.

The second income stream—whether from savings, part-time work, freelance projects, or a partner's employment—becomes a cushion, not the foundation. Many families reverse this priority and risk financial collapse when the secondary income dries up unexpectedly.

"If you tell me that I'm going to fail at something, I will sell my soul to prove you wrong."

This mindset—commitment to making the plan work—is inseparable from sound financial planning. Families must enter a relocation with both a realistic budget and the determination to honor that budget, even when homesickness, cultural adjustment, or unexpected costs tempt them to abandon the plan.

Before you even begin researching schools, rental markets, or neighborhoods, listen to how this family structured their move and the financial safeguards they put in place to give you concrete reference points for your own situation.

Key takeaways

  • Calculate your exact monthly breakeven point before relocating—this is the minimum income needed to cover all essential expenses in your new country.
  • Secure stable, contracted employment in the local social security system as your primary income source, not a secondary cushion.
  • Ensure at least one household member has permanent, contracted employment to guarantee visa security, healthcare access, and pension contributions.
  • Treat savings as a safety net, not as your main income strategy; relying on savings alone causes them to dwindle rapidly under the pressure of unexpected costs.
