What community and professional development activities does a commercial real estate broker engage in beyond transactions?

September 22, 2026 WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women · Kesi Milligan

← See the WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women fiche

Commercial real estate brokers extend their impact far beyond deal-making through active community involvement, policy advocacy, and mentorship. This work spans volunteering with neighborhood organizations, leading educational initiatives with universities, and ground-up advocacy for systemic change in underserved communities.

From Real Estate to Neighborhood Leadership

Kesi Milligan's engagement with her community reflects a deeper commitment than transaction volume. She volunteers actively with the Blue Hills Neighborhood Association, channeling her expertise in urban development into real-world neighborhood advocacy. Her work with the Centers for Neighborhoods at UMKC alongside Dr. Jacob Wagner extends this mission into institutional spaces, helping shape the next generation's understanding of community development.

Church involvement rounds out her spiritual and community anchor. Milligan treats these roles not as side projects but as integral to who she is as a professional and advocate. She leads a youth group during summer months, directly investing in young people's development and mentorship—a commitment that mirrors her larger professional mission of creating pathways for underrepresented voices in commercial real estate.

Advocacy Work as a Business Strategy

Beyond volunteering, Milligan emphasizes ground-up policy advocacy as essential work for brokers who understand systemic barriers. As discussed in this episode of WIRED, her transition to commercial brokerage at Select Sites LLC was partly motivated by a gap she observed at AltCap, a Community Development Financial Institution (CDFI) where she worked. She identified that minorities and small business owners lacked proper guidance navigating commercial real estate—a structural inequity she now addresses directly.

This advocacy work is not charity; it reflects a strategic understanding that policy change and community trust are prerequisites for market access. Milligan's involvement with these organizations creates the conditions under which underrepresented entrepreneurs can participate in real estate development at all. The work is both personal and systemic.

"When it gets right at the difficult moments when you think you just can't take it anymore, that's when the breakthrough comes."

The deeper philosophy here, evident in Milligan's conversation on the podcast, is that breakthrough moments in business and community work are inseparable. Struggling through difficult seasons—whether navigating a three-year climb into commercial real estate or addressing institutional barriers—is where real learning and transformation occur. Her community roles are where that philosophy gets lived out daily.

How does over-education function as an obstacle to entrepreneurial action?

Over-educating oneself or not having enough education can become a crutch to avoid starting, transitioning, or moving to the next level. Kesi Milligan recognized she had already mapped out her professional direction—she simply wasn't acting on it.

Fear should be treated as a north star because once fear starts to set in, that means you are getting closer to what you are trying to accomplish. You are courageous because you're doing the things you're afraid of.

How does motivation beyond financial gain drive entrepreneurial success in real estate?

Understanding the challenges of being a small business owner, a person of color, and a woman, somebody has to be an advocate for the community and drive policy change at the institutional level.
