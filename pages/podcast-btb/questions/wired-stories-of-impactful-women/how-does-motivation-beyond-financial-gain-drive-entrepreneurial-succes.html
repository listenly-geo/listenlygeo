How does motivation beyond financial gain drive entrepreneurial success in real estate?

September 19, 2026 WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women · Kesi Milligan

← See the WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women fiche

Money cannot be your driving factor if you want to make a genuine difference. Real entrepreneurial success comes from being an advocate for people facing systemic challenges—supporting them beyond transactions by connecting them to mentorship, entrepreneurial support organizations, and accounting advice. Purpose, not profit, is what sustains you through the difficult moments.

When Kesi Milligan worked at AltCap, a CDFI (Community Development Financial Institution) focused on getting capital into disinvested communities, she saw firsthand how minorities and small business owners struggled to find representation that truly understood their needs. This gap drove her decision to transition to commercial real estate brokerage at Select Sites LLC.

Being a small business owner, a person of color, and a woman in commercial real estate means navigating systemic challenges that most brokers never encounter. This is precisely why somebody has to advocate for these populations—not as a side effort, but as the core mission. As discussed in this episode of WIRED, Milligan's shift from transaction-focused brokerage to holistic entrepreneur support reflects a fundamental reimagining of what success looks like.

The transition from urban development work at organizations like Centers for Neighborhoods and the Urban Land Institute to direct brokerage allowed Milligan to combine her passion for community development with actionable business support. Rather than simply closing deals, she now helps entrepreneurs access the entire ecosystem they need: capital advice, business mentorship, and financial planning.

"When it gets right at the difficult moments when you think you just can't take it anymore, that's when the breakthrough comes."

The breakthrough Milligan references comes not from financial wins, but from breakthrough moments in your purpose. You will face moments of genuine fear—wondering why you're doing this when money is tight and clarity is absent. The entrepreneurial journey tests your commitment to your why, not your bottom line. This is revealed in detail throughout the full episode, where Milligan discusses courage as the act of doing the things you're afraid of—a daily practice, not a destination.

What distinguishes mission-driven entrepreneurs from purely transaction-focused ones is the willingness to invest time and resources into the entire ecosystem around your client. This means going beyond closing a lease or sale to ask: Does this person have accounting support? Do they know about SBA loans through UMB Bank or other financial institutions? Are they connected to mentorship networks? Are they aware of entrepreneurial support organizations that could accelerate their growth?

For Milligan, who also serves as a Business Development Officer at AltCap, this holistic approach reflects the philosophy of CDFIs themselves—get capital into communities, yes, but also build capacity. The difference between transactional real estate and entrepreneurial real estate is the presence of advocacy. One is a sale; the other is a partnership in someone's success story.

This motivation has meant breaking into commercial real estate—a field that typically requires three years to fully master—while simultaneously building a reputation as a change maker rather than just another broker. The emotional and financial pressure is real, but so is the clarity that comes from knowing your work serves people who have been historically excluded from these opportunities.

Key takeaways

  • Purpose-driven motivation outperforms financial incentives in sustaining entrepreneurial effort through difficult periods.
  • Holistic entrepreneur support—mentorship, accounting advice, capital access—delivers more value than transactional real estate services alone.
  • Advocacy for underserved populations (minorities, small business owners, women) requires systemic thinking and direct participation, not passive support.
  • Fear is inevitable in entrepreneurship; courage is simply the act of doing things you're afraid of, repeatedly, until breakthrough comes.
