How can leadership skills help professionals stay consistent when pivoting between different industries?

September 23, 2026 WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women · Cyndi Garza

← See the WIRED: Stories of Impactful Women fiche

Leadership skills are fundamentally transferable across industries because they're built on curiosity and a growth mindset rather than technical domain knowledge. Recognizing burnout as a signal to pivot, rather than a failure, allows professionals to sustain momentum—and staying true to your multifaceted identity (parent, leader, entrepreneur) rather than forcing yourself into a single professional box ensures consistency through change.

Transferable skills transcend industry boundaries

When you build your leadership foundation on core competencies—listening, vision-setting, accountability, and the ability to learn—these capabilities work across any sector. Cyndi Garza spent over a decade in mortgage banking before pivoting to coach entrepreneurs and eventually opening a luxury boutique coffee wine bar in Sarasota. The same leadership principles that made her effective managing teams in financial services applied directly when building her coaching company and later her boutique.

The key is approaching each new industry with genuine curiosity rather than defensiveness. Leaders who are naturally interested in how other sectors operate, who ask questions instead of assuming their old methods are universal, adapt faster and build credibility in unfamiliar spaces. This mindset—"I want to understand this industry"—is what sustains you when the technical details feel foreign.

Burnout is a compass, not a stop sign

Many professionals interpret burnout as a personal failure or reason to abandon their career altogether. Instead, burnout often signals a readiness for a growth spurt—a moment when your current role has stopped stretching you. Garza describes this as the natural timing for a pivot: when one industry or role no longer energizes you, that's the moment to invest your energy in learning something entirely new.

This reframing removes the shame from leaving a field and replaces it with agency. You're not running from something; you're running toward the next chapter of your development. As Cyndi explains in the episode, the ability to recognize when you need to pivot—and then actually do it—is a leadership skill in itself.

"We can't put ourselves into one box. We have a lot of things going on. We're mom, we're Mimi, we're leader, all of those things. And that's what just has really helped me to just stay true to who I am."

Identity consistency beats career consistency

The pressure to build a linear, single-industry career can actually undermine your authenticity. When you accept that you are more than one role—professional, parent, community member, entrepreneur—simultaneously, you free yourself from the false choice of "picking one lane." This multiplicity isn't a scattered weakness; it's a source of grounding.

Staying true to your core values and character traits—your integrity, your work ethic, your capacity to care for people—allows you to move between industries without losing yourself. The mortgage banker who becomes a life coach and then a boutique owner isn't three different people; she's one person applying consistent values to different contexts. That consistency through change is explored in depth in the podcast, where Garza discusses how surrounding yourself with accountability partners—people who know all your roles and believe in you across all of them—reinforces this integrated identity.

For professionals anxious about industry switches, the real question isn't "Will my skills transfer?" It's "Am I staying true to who I am?" Leadership skills absolutely transfer; your character does too. Both are your real competitive advantage when navigating change.
