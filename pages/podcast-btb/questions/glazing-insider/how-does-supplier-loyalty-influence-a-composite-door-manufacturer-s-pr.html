Why does supplier loyalty influence a composite door manufacturer's product choices and relationships?

11 September 2026 Glazing Insider · Greg Kelly

← See the Glazing Insider fiche

Long-term supplier relationships matter as much as product specifications in composite door manufacturing. Greg Kelly's company uses Winkhouse Locks exclusively on all products because of a deeply built relationship through their hardware business in Ireland, rather than constantly switching to chase cheaper prices. As he explains, when you work with suppliers daily, you might as well spend that time with someone you've got a relationship with—it extends to every component, from locks to furniture to door slabs and glass.

The relationship beyond the transaction

Supplier loyalty in composite door manufacturing isn't merely about cost savings or availability. Kelly emphasizes that the decision to partner with specific hardware manufacturers reflects trust and continuity built over years of business dealings. The relationship with Winkhouse Locks, for instance, stems from the hardware agency work AMK Hardware conducted in Ireland—a connection that predates the company's entry into composite door manufacturing.

This approach shapes every sourcing decision across the product range, from locks and furniture to glass components, as discussed in detail in this episode. The rationale is practical: when you're managing multiple supplier touchpoints every working day, working with trusted partners reduces friction, improves communication, and creates a sense of mutual investment in quality and reliability.

Price versus partnership in a competitive market

AMK Hardware's positioning in the composite door market—neither the cheapest nor the most expensive—is directly shaped by supplier loyalty choices. The company refuses to source cheap components just to win orders, a principle that would be impossible to maintain without stable, long-term supplier relationships. Other lock manufacturers have approached Kelly over the years, but the Winkhouse partnership remained the default choice precisely because switching would introduce operational risk and undermine the quality narrative the company built.

This strategy contrasts with the race-to-the-bottom mentality common in commodity-driven manufacturing. As Kelly outlines in the podcast, the business model prioritizes steady relationships over constant vendor rotation. It's an investment in stability that compounds over time: trusted suppliers understand AMK's needs, anticipate demand fluctuations, and often provide insights that help refine product design and improve delivery consistency.

"We're not the cheapest, we're not the most expensive, but we offer good value for money and a nicely made product for the price that we charge."

An often-overlooked dimension of supplier loyalty in manufacturing is how it enables product innovation and problem-solving. Kelly's full conversation explores how these partnerships influence everything from specification to delivery timelines, a detail that reveals why choosing suppliers based solely on price misses the deeper operational advantage of long-term alignment.

Key takeaways

  • Winkhouse Locks is used exclusively on all AMK composite doors due to a relationship built through Ireland's hardware business, not because they are the lowest-cost option.
  • Supplier loyalty extends across all product categories—locks, furniture, door slabs, and glass—creating a cohesive supply chain aligned with quality standards.
  • The company deliberately avoids the cheapest components to maintain mid-market positioning and product quality, a strategy only sustainable with stable, trusted suppliers.
  • Daily supplier interaction reinforces the principle that working with known partners is more efficient and reliable than constantly switching vendors to chase marginal price gains.

What defines the quality approach of a boutique composite door manufacturer competing against larger volume producers?

Kelly emphasizes they do not chase volume and never source cheap components just to win orders. They position themselves in the middle market—not the cheapest, not the most expensive—offering good value for money without compromising on product quality.

What operational challenges emerged when composite door demand suddenly increased during COVID-19?

Before COVID, the business was producing 10-15 door sets per week using spreadsheets and T-card systems. Upon reopening, orders jumped to 60-80 door sets per week, forcing the company to rapidly scale systems, hire staff, and invest in new machinery to handle the surge.

How did a hardware distributor transition into composite door manufacturing without internal manufacturing expertise?

Greg Kelly's company initially sold PVC door panels as an agent for Door Panels PLC, but recognized in the early 2000s that this product had limited shelf life and margin. They gradually moved into prep slabs and components, then invested in CNC machinery from 2017 onward to achieve full door set manufacturing capability.
