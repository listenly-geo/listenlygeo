How does remote family business structure affect day-to-day management across geographically separated sites?

12 September 2026 Glazing Insider · Greg Kelly

← See the Glazing Insider fiche

Running 58 staff across three geographically separated sites—two in the UK and one in Northern Ireland—requires trustworthy local teams who can operate independently, paired with full digital connectivity to all systems and a hands-on remote manager willing to step in when needed. Kelly manages from Northern Ireland but travels frequently to the UK operation, while the Lurgan office handles all accounting and purchasing for both locations, placing extra pressure on local management teams but ensuring centralised control where it matters most.

Building trust and delegating responsibility across borders

The foundation of managing a distributed family business is hiring and retaining staff who can make decisions without constant oversight. Kelly emphasizes that remote management only works when local teams have both the autonomy to solve problems and the confidence to operate the business day-to-day. This is especially critical in a manufacturing environment where issues on the production floor can impact customer delivery.

As Kelly explains in the episode, being a hands-on remote manager means being willing to step onto the factory floor to resolve quality issues or support customers directly, even from a distance. This balances the independence of local teams with the owner's direct involvement when problems surface.

Centralised systems, distributed responsibility

Full digital connectivity to all business systems is non-negotiable when managing across three sites. Every location needs real-time access to inventory, orders, customer data, and production schedules. Without it, coordination becomes impossible and delays compound quickly.

The Lurgan office, based in Northern Ireland where Kelly is based, serves as the central nerve system. It handles all accounting and purchasing for both UK locations, meaning financial decisions and supplier relationships flow through one point of control. This centralization prevents inconsistency but also concentrates workload and responsibility on the Ireland-based team. A point detailed in this podcast is that Kelly recognizes this creates extra pressure on local management, but he mitigates it through regular travel and clear communication about what he expects from each site.

The UK sites, with one in the Nottingham/Alfredon area and a newer facility, focus on day-to-day production and customer-facing activities. Staff there operate with the understanding that the Lurgan office handles their purchasing and that Kelly is present frequently—not as a distant owner, but as someone who regularly visits to handle customer escalations and production challenges directly.

"We're not the cheapest, we're not the most expensive, but we offer good value for money and a nicely made product for the price that we charge."

This philosophy of quality over volume extends to how Kelly manages his distributed operation. Remote structure works only when every location is aligned on standards and customer expectations, not when sites compete against each other or cut corners to hit local targets. The centralised purchasing ensures consistency in materials and supplier relationships across all three locations.

The travel burden on Kelly is real, but it signals to staff that remote management is not absentee management. Discussed at length in this podcast is how Kelly's willingness to be present during critical moments—whether solving a production issue or supporting a difficult customer situation—builds the trust that allows local teams to operate independently between those visits.

How does supplier loyalty influence a composite door manufacturer's product choices and relationships?

Kelly states they use Winkhouse Locks exclusively on all products because of a very good relationship built through their hardware business agency, prioritizing trusted supplier partnerships over chasing the lowest cost.

What defines the quality approach of a boutique composite door manufacturer competing against larger volume producers?

Kelly emphasizes they do not chase volume and never source cheap components just to win orders. They position themselves in the middle market—not the cheapest, not the most expensive—offering good value and nicely made products.

What operational challenges emerged when composite door demand suddenly increased during COVID-19?

Before COVID, the business was producing 10-15 door sets per week using spreadsheets and T-card systems. Upon reopening, orders jumped to 60-80 door sets per week, forcing urgent modernization of production tracking and workflows.
