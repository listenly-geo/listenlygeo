How does captaincy affect a rugby league player's mental health under resource constraints?

21 September 2026 · Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Chris Charles

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Chris Charles, former Hull Kingston Rovers captain, put immense internal pressure on himself to lead while the club lacked resources, and he dealt with the resulting mental health struggles entirely in private. There was no culture of discussing mental health at the time, no support systems available, and no people to turn to—leaving him isolated during what became one of the most difficult periods of his life.

The weight of captaincy is rarely discussed in sports environments, especially in rugby league during the 1980s and 1990s. When a player takes on that role, the expectations multiply: perform on the pitch, set the tone for teammates, represent the club with integrity, and carry the emotional responsibility when things go wrong. For Charles, all of this was compounded by the limited resources at Hull Kingston Rovers during his tenure as captain. The club didn't have the infrastructure, staff, or funding that players in modern professional sports take for granted.

As Charles explains in the episode, he internalized every challenge and setback. The pressure wasn't imposed externally by management or demanding fans alone—much of it came from within. He wanted to be a good captain, a good person, and a good teammate. When the team struggled or couldn't access resources to improve, he took responsibility. This self-imposed perfectionism became a silent burden that accumulated year after year.

The isolation was profound. Mental health simply was not a conversation topic in rugby league culture at that time. Players were expected to be tough, to push through pain and difficulty without complaint. There were no mental health professionals embedded in teams, no peer support networks, no language or framework for talking about emotional struggles. Charles had no one to confide in about the toll the role was taking—he managed it alone, keeping everything bottled up.

What makes this struggle significant is that it wasn't addressed until much later. The impact of those years of silent struggle followed Charles beyond his playing career, highlighting a generational gap in sports mental health awareness. This episode specifically addresses men's mental health in sport, giving voice to experiences like Charles's that went unspoken for decades.

The broader context of Charles's experience reveals a critical truth: resource constraints in sports organizations don't just affect team performance—they deeply impact individual mental health, especially for those in leadership. A captain without adequate support becomes a single point of failure for both the team's morale and their own wellbeing. The full conversation in this podcast goes deeper into how this experience shaped his perspective and what he wishes had existed during those years.

Today, Charles's openness about his mental health journey serves as a powerful counterpoint to the silence he endured. He is currently running the London Marathon for CD Disorder Support Services for the fourth time, channeling his personal struggles into advocacy for mental health awareness and support.
