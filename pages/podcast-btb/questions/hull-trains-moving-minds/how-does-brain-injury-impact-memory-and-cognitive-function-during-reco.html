How does brain injury impact memory and cognitive function during recovery, and what support strategies can help compensate for these deficits?

September 13, 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Paul Spence

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

After brain injury, working memory deteriorates dramatically—tasks that took minutes now consume hours, and survivors struggle to retain procedures, tools, and information. Unlike a broken arm with a predictable recovery path, brain injury is neurologically complex and highly individual, leaving survivors with few clear answers. The solution lies in structured compensation strategies: lists, reminders, and deliberate environmental setup that work around the deficit rather than fighting it.

When Paul Spence suffered a frontal lobe brain hemorrhage in 2012 after an unprovoked Easter bank holiday weekend attack, the damage to his working memory became immediately apparent during his recovery. Tasks that previously took five minutes now consumed half an hour, and he found himself repeatedly forgetting tools, policies, and procedures—cognitive failures that had never happened before his injury.

The frustration intensified because, as Spence explains in this episode of Hull Trains – Moving Minds, medical professionals offered limited guidance on how to manage these deficits. Brain injury recovery is fundamentally different from other trauma: the brain's complexity means that no two recoveries follow the same trajectory, and no standardized treatment plan exists.

Working memory loss and the gap in recovery support

The core challenge Spence faced was that the brain does not respond to injury the way broken bones do. With a fracture, the path is clear—immobilize, wait six weeks, and healing follows a predictable arc. Brain injury offers no such certainty. Every survivor's experience is unique; their deficits, their timeline, and their recovery plateau are all individual variables.

This lack of answers creates secondary trauma. Spence describes how daunting it was to live through a recovery with no clear framework, no guarantee of progress, and no definitive endpoint. The medical system, he emphasizes, struggles to provide the concrete support that brain injury survivors desperately need during their most vulnerable years.

Compensation strategies: building scaffolds around cognitive loss

Over time, Spence developed practical coping mechanisms that allowed him to function despite his working memory deficits. Lists, written reminders, and environmental design became his tools for compensating. Rather than trying to restore memory function—which may never fully recover—he restructured his daily life to work around the limitation.

"Setting yourself up to win," as Spence frames it, means deliberately creating conditions where cognitive deficits cause fewer failures. If you cannot remember a procedure, write it down in a consistent place and make it part of your routine to check it. If you forget tools, organize your space so everything has a designated spot. These are not cures, but they are functional adaptations that restore independence.

However, Spence is clear that not every survivor has access to the professional guidance that helps identify these strategies. A neuropsychologist can accelerate the discovery of what works for each individual brain; without that support, survivors navigate trial-and-error alone, wasting months or years before finding effective compensation patterns.

"I am different, I am no less—I've been judging myself as a less of a person, and it was not fair. I needed encouragement, kindness and compassion."

One detail that emerges from Spence's full conversation with host Gemma Oaten is how emotional and psychological support proved equally crucial to functional recovery—a dimension often overlooked in medical discussions of brain injury rehabilitation.

Key takeaways

  • Brain injury causes severe working memory deficits that make routine tasks take significantly longer and create frequent lapses in recalling procedures and information.
  • Unlike fractures or other injuries with clear treatment protocols, brain injury recovery is highly individual with no standardized recovery path or guaranteed outcomes.
  • Compensation strategies—structured lists, written reminders, and deliberate environmental organization—allow survivors to function despite ongoing cognitive deficits.
  • Neuropsychology support and access to professional guidance accelerate the discovery of effective coping mechanisms, but many survivors lack this resource.
