How did Calum Scott's life change after the Britain's Got Talent golden buzzer moment?

25 August 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Calum Scott

← Voir la fiche Hull Trains – Moving Minds

Calum Scott described it as an overnight transformation. After the golden buzzer audition, he returned to his Hull City Council job for two or three more months — without telling anyone what had happened. Then the audition aired, he watched it at the North Ferriby Legion Club — the very first pub he ever performed in — and from that night, his phone "just blew up and it's been blowing up for 10 years."

Silence first, then a single pub screening that changed everything

Between taping and broadcast, Scott sat on the secret entirely. He carried on clocking in at his council job as if nothing had happened — a surreal double life that lasted another two to three months. There was no announcement, no warning to friends.

The night the audition finally aired, he chose to watch it somewhere deeply personal: the North Ferriby Legion Club, the first pub stage he had ever performed on. It was a full-circle moment, and it was the last quiet night he would have for a decade. Scott recalls that after the screening, the reaction was instant and unstoppable — a detail explored in depth on Hull Trains – Moving Minds.

Before the audition aired, his Britain's Got Talent announcement post on Facebook had just 3 likes. After the show went out, it jumped to 10,000 likes. That single data point captures the scale of the shift: from near-total obscurity to national recognition, overnight.

Ten years of momentum — and the imposter syndrome that quietly ran alongside it

The golden buzzer was not a launching pad Scott had been building toward — it hit him sideways, fast and total. In the decade that followed, he released three studio albums, collaborated with Lost Frequencies on "Where Are You Now?" (which went on to accumulate 1.7 billion streams), worked with the Whitney Houston estate, performed at King Charles III's coronation, and sold out Wembley Arena.

Yet Scott admits that for much of that decade, a quiet voice in the back of his mind kept saying it was only a matter of time before it all went away. Imposter syndrome — the feeling of not being worthy of what he had achieved — ran underneath the success throughout. He discusses this candidly in the Moving Minds episode, connecting it directly to his identity as a lad from Hull.

It was his third album, Avanoir, released in October of the previous year, that he credits with finally quieting that voice. Reaching a third record felt like proof — proof that he was not about to disappear. That shift in confidence, he says, is what allowed him to write songs that weren't only designed to make people cry. Hear him explain it in his own words on Hull Trains – Moving Minds.

"Therapy, hands down, saved my life. Debs, my mum Debs, definitely was the core anchor as to why I'm still here. But therapy helped me reinforce all of that strength and helped me build a strong and unwavering support mentally."

Key takeaways

  • Scott kept the golden buzzer secret and returned to his council job for two to three months before the audition aired publicly.
  • The broadcast moment was anchored at the North Ferriby Legion Club — his very first performance venue — and his Facebook post went from 3 likes to 10,000 likes in a single night.
  • Ten years of global success (1.7 billion streams, Wembley Arena, the coronation) did not silence his imposter syndrome — his third album Avanoir is what finally did.
  • Scott credits therapy and his mother as the two forces that kept him grounded through the decade of relentless momentum that followed the golden buzzer.
