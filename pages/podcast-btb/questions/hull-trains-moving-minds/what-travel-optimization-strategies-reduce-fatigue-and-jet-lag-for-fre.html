How to reduce travel fatigue and jet lag during frequent train and location work?

12 September 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Jack Marriner-Brown

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Travel early in the day rather than late at night, bring books instead of scrolling on your phone, and use an eye mask and earplugs to improve rest quality during your journey. Taking a daytime walk around your destination upon arrival helps preserve your natural sleep cycle and reduces the disorientation that comes from late-night travel.

Timing and Mental Preparation: The Foundation

The core principle behind reducing travel fatigue is aligning your travel schedule with your body's natural rhythms. When you leave early in the day, you arrive during daylight hours, allowing your body to reset through natural light exposure and outdoor activity rather than fighting jet lag and disorientation that come with late-night arrivals.

How you spend those travel hours matters just as much as when you travel. As explored in the episode with Jack Marriner-Brown, replacing three hours of phone scrolling with a book creates a fundamentally different mental state—you arrive calmer, less overstimulated, and genuinely rested rather than digitally drained.

The Physical Comfort Layer: Tools for Better Rest

An eye mask and earplugs are non-negotiable for train travel rest. They eliminate the unpredictability of your environment—sudden light changes, conversation noise, or station announcements—allowing you to maintain consistent sleep quality regardless of external chaos. These small tools create a micro-recovery space within an inherently uncomfortable setting.

The daytime arrival ritual is the final piece: stepping off the train and taking a walk around your destination during daylight hours consolidates your circadian adjustment. Natural light exposure, fresh air, and gentle movement signal to your body that you've arrived and reset, rather than perpetuating the disorientation of travel fatigue.

"For me getting outdoors is what sorts it out when I feel anxious or up in the air, especially with the massive switch in my industry and the imposter syndrome that comes with it."

Why does this approach work so consistently? Jack Marriner-Brown explains in the podcast that the combination addresses fatigue at three levels: circadian rhythm (early travel + daytime arrival), mental fatigue (books instead of scrolling), and physical discomfort (eye mask + earplugs). Together, they prevent the compounding exhaustion that typically follows frequent travel for location work.

Key takeaways

  • Travel early in the day to arrive during daylight and reset your natural sleep cycle more effectively than late-night arrivals.
  • Bring books and avoid phone scrolling during travel to arrive mentally refreshed rather than digitally overstimulated.
  • Use an eye mask and earplugs to maintain consistent sleep quality on trains, regardless of environmental disruptions.
  • Take a daytime walk around your destination upon arrival to consolidate circadian adjustment and signal your body that recovery is complete.

What are effective wellbeing practices for managing anxiety in media and entertainment careers?

Jack recommends vocalizing thoughts through conversation—either with friends, colleagues, or by talking aloud while walking outdoors—rather than keeping anxious thoughts internal. This practice complements travel recovery by preventing mental fatigue from compounding physical exhaustion during frequent location work.

Why is outdoor activity and manual work beneficial for someone working in high-stress broadcasting environments?

Jack describes outdoor work as a 'soother' that helps him manage the imposter syndrome, nerves, and anxiety that come with the rapid industry transition. The daytime walk component of travel recovery leverages this same principle—movement and fresh air are both travel fatigue reducers and anxiety management tools.

What mental health strategies help manage imposter syndrome when rapidly advancing through high-visibility media roles?

Jack uses what he calls an 'evidence pot'—a mental technique where he recalls concrete proof that contradicts negative thoughts. Quality travel recovery through early departures and proper rest directly supports this practice by ensuring your mind is clear and resourced enough to access positive evidence when needed.
