How can rugby league players focus on controllable factors when transitioning between rival clubs amid negative social media commentary?

23 September 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Chris Charles

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Focus exclusively on what you can control—your own actions, reactions, and daily effort. Social media will always contain both praise and criticism, but neither determines your value or performance. The strategy is straightforward: live in the present, improve each day, and represent your club and yourself with integrity; external commentary is something to acknowledge but not absorb.

The Practice of Selective Mental Filtering

Jack Charles advises treating social media as background noise rather than instruction. He recognizes that negative comments are inevitable during a club transition, especially when moving between rival organizations, but dwelling on them serves no practical purpose.

His approach centers on a single principle: separate what lies within your control from what does not. Training quality, mental resilience, team representation, and personal conduct—these are controllable. Social media sentiment, opponent criticism, and public opinion—these are not. As discussed in the episode, Jack emphasizes that external pressure becomes manageable once you stop treating it as your responsibility to manage.

Protecting Family from the Noise

Charles extends this mindset to those around him, particularly advising family members to actively avoid reading negative comments online. Protecting emotional space—both personal and familial—is part of the discipline. When family members absorb negativity on behalf of the player, it creates secondary stress that can undermine focus.

This boundary-setting is not avoidance; it is active curation. The player remains aware of public discourse without letting it become intrusive. A detailed look at how this mental resilience develops differently across generations is explored in the full conversation between Chris and Jack.

"I don't really see it as a pressure right like this is what I've always wanted to do I love it to bits and I'd die doing it but I've never thought of it all as a burden."

The quote reveals the deeper psychology at work: when you reframe a transition as opportunity rather than threat, the emotional weight shifts. Internal motivation—doing something you love—naturally outlasts external judgment. This is especially powerful for players moving between rival clubs, where the stakes feel personal.

There's also a fascinating parallel between Chris's era and Jack's approach in how each generation handles adversity; you can hear more about the generational shift in mental health resources and openness in this episode.

Key takeaways

  • Distinguish between what you control (effort, daily improvement, personal conduct) and what you don't (social media opinion, external commentary).
  • Social media criticism during a club transition is inevitable but irrelevant to your performance—treat it as background noise, not feedback.
  • Protect family members by encouraging them to avoid reading negative online commentary on your behalf.
  • Frame club transitions as opportunities to prove yourself, not as burdens or threats to your identity.
