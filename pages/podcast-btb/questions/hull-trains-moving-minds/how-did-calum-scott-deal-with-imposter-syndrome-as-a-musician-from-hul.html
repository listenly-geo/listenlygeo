How did Calum Scott deal with imposter syndrome as a musician from Hull?

30 August 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Calum Scott

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Calum Scott carried a persistent voice in the back of his mind telling him it was only a matter of time before his success disappeared—a feeling amplified by being "a lad from Hull" in an industry where he often felt he didn't belong. Writing his third album 'Avanoir' silenced that doubt, allowing him to reach a new level of confidence and creative freedom he had never experienced before.

For much of his career, Scott battled the classic imposter syndrome narrative: despite his achievements and recognition, he couldn't shake the feeling that he wasn't truly worthy of what he'd accomplished. As discussed in Hull Trains – Moving Minds, this inner voice intensified with the spotlight on him, creating a cycle of self-doubt that many high-achievers recognize.

What changed was the realisation that he was genuinely here to stay. By the time he began working on his third album, Scott made a conscious shift: he stopped accepting the narrative that his career was temporary or fragile. This mindset breakthrough opened the door to a completely different creative process.

From self-doubt to creative freedom with 'Avanoir'

The album 'Avanoir', released in October, became a turning point. Scott didn't just record songs; he reclaimed his artistic identity. In the podcast episode, he reflects that with this album he "just feel[s] more confident and more content than I ever have."

This confidence translated into a broader creative range. Instead of limiting himself to what he thought the audience wanted—emotional, tear-inducing ballads—he felt free to explore different styles and themes. The album allowed him to write what he genuinely wanted, not just what he believed would be commercially successful or emotionally manipulative.

"I think with this album in particular, I just feel more confident and more content than I ever have."

The turning point wasn't external validation—it was internal conviction. When Scott committed to his third album, he made a decision: "I'm not going anywhere anytime soon." That single belief dismantled the imposter voice that had shadowed him for years, a journey he explores in depth in this episode.

Key takeaways

  • Imposter syndrome often intensifies with public visibility and spotlight, making high-achievers question their worthiness regardless of measurable success.
  • A shift from "temporary survival" thinking to "I'm here to stay" thinking was the catalyst that silenced Scott's inner doubt.
  • Creative freedom emerged when Scott stopped trying to produce only emotionally manipulative songs and began writing what he genuinely wanted.
  • Album 'Avanoir' marked a personal and professional breakthrough, giving Scott the confidence to explore new artistic directions.
