Why do likes and follows say nothing about your real talent or worth?

5 September 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Calum Scott

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Social media metrics are a completely unreliable measure of talent or value. Calum Scott describes how a Facebook post announcing his Britain's Got Talent audition received only three likes before the audition aired — one from his mother — yet the same post accumulated 10,000 likes once the episode was broadcast. The shift had nothing to do with his actual ability; it was purely the visibility effect of television exposure. Scott argues that you can go from zero to hero with one post, which proves that authentic talent and genuine artistry are what truly matter, not follower counts or engagement numbers.

This perspective runs deeper than a dismissal of vanity metrics. Scott's observation highlights a fundamental truth: social validation online is divorced from creative merit. The three likes before his audition reveal that no existing audience had validated his work in isolation. His talent remained unchanged between those three likes and those 10,000. What changed was external amplification, not the quality of who he was or what he could do.

For emerging artists and creators, this distinction carries real weight. Early in a career, when follower counts are small and engagement feels impossibly slow, the temptation to equate low numbers with low worth is powerful. As Scott explains in the episode, the viral nature of social platforms means that one moment — one post, one video, one audition — can shift perception overnight. But that overnight shift reflects algorithmic luck or media attention, not a sudden improvement in the person behind the work.

Scott's earlier struggles with imposter syndrome on his third album, 'Avanoir', underscore why this message matters. Despite a successful career spanning a decade since his golden buzzer moment, he carried doubt about whether he truly deserved his achievements. Social media—where the metrics are visible, quantifiable, and often humble in the early stages—can amplify that self-doubt. Yet his own journey from three Facebook likes to international collaborations with Lost Frequencies (1.7 billion streams) and work alongside the Whitney Houston estate demonstrates that persistence and authenticity ultimately outweigh any single metric.

"Therapy, hands down, saved my life. Debs, my mum Debs, definitely was the core anchor as to why I'm still here. But therapy helped me reinforce all of that strength and helped me build a strong and unwavering support mentally."

Scott also discusses in this podcast how his viral praying mantis concert video from his South Africa tour generated an estimated 3 to 5 million views—a stark contrast to his pre-audition Facebook silence. Yet he frames this not as vindication of his worth, but as evidence of how arbitrary online visibility can be. The video went viral because of its novelty and shareability, not because he had suddenly become a better artist. His talent was constant; the algorithm's attention was the variable.

For anyone navigating the pressure of social metrics—whether starting out or established—Scott's message is clear: keep your sense of self separate from the numbers. A low follower count does not diminish your work. A high engagement rate does not validate it. Your worth as a creator, or as a person, exists independent of what any platform displays. The real measure is whether you are making work that feels true to you and finding the people who connect with it authentically, regardless of scale.
