How do you manage anxiety in high-pressure media careers?

11 September 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Jack Marriner-Brown

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

The most effective strategy is to vocalize your thoughts through conversation—whether with friends, colleagues, or by talking aloud while walking outdoors—rather than letting concerns fester internally. Getting active, socializing even when it feels difficult, and speaking to anyone available prevents the catastrophizing spiral that internal monologues create, replacing it with the clearer perspective that full sentences and dialogue generate.

When anxiety builds silently within, it tends to spiral into worst-case scenarios. Speaking your thoughts aloud transforms vague worry into concrete language, making problems feel more manageable. This principle applies especially in media careers, where the combination of visibility, rapid transitions, and high stakes can intensify internal pressure.

Jack Marriner-Brown emphasizes that social engagement is not optional when managing anxiety—it is a deliberate practice. Reaching out to a colleague in the break room, calling a friend, or even verbalizing your concerns to yourself while walking outdoors all serve the same function: they interrupt the feedback loop of unexpressed thoughts and introduce clarity through language.

"For me getting outdoors is what sorts it out when I feel anxious or up in the air, especially with the massive switch in my industry and the imposter syndrome that comes with it."

The combination of movement and conversation amplifies the benefit. Walking outdoors while vocalizing thoughts introduces physical activity—which independently reduces anxiety—while simultaneously creating the dialogue effect. This dual approach works especially well for broadcasters and media professionals who spend much of their time indoors or in high-stress studio environments.

Beyond casual conversation, Marriner-Brown discusses in the episode how consistent socialization acts as a preventive measure against anxiety accumulation. Waiting until you feel overwhelmed to reach out is less effective than building regular dialogue into your routine—with colleagues, peers, or anyone willing to listen. The specificity of the conversation matters less than the act of externalizing thought.

Breaking the Internal Monologue Cycle

Internal monologues are anxiety's primary fuel. Without an external listener or the structure of forming complete sentences, thoughts accelerate into catastrophizing. Dialogue interrupts this cycle by forcing coherence—you cannot catastrophize as rapidly when you must articulate full ideas in language another person can follow.

For media professionals navigating imposter syndrome and the pressure of high-visibility roles, this principle becomes crucial. As Marriner-Brown explains in the podcast, verbalizing anxieties reduces their perceived magnitude. The gap between what feels catastrophic in your head and what sounds manageable when spoken aloud is often the difference between paralysis and action.

Marriner-Brown also references how outdoor work and gardening serve as natural opportunities for this practice—solo work in fresh air creates space for vocalization, whether literal self-talk or the mental clarity that movement generates. This method is particularly valuable during high-stress industry transitions, when the pressure to succeed internally can be overwhelming.

Why is outdoor activity and manual work beneficial for someone working in high-stress broadcasting environments?

Jack describes outdoor work as a soother that helps him manage the imposter syndrome, nerves, and anxiety that come with the rapid industry transition. Physical activity and time outdoors provide concrete relief from the mental strain of high-visibility media roles.

What mental health strategies help manage imposter syndrome when rapidly advancing through high-visibility media roles?

Jack uses what he calls an "evidence pot"—a mental technique where he recalls concrete proof that contradicts negative thoughts, such as remembering times he succeeded or positive feedback he received. This strategy counteracts the internal monologue that feeds imposter syndrome.

How does someone transition from manual labour and reality television rejection into professional broadcasting roles?

Jack's path involved getting eliminated early from The Traitors, experiencing anxiety during the months-long non-disclosure period before broadcast, then gradually building a broadcasting career through BBC Radio Humberside and BBC Morning Live, while maintaining freelance gardening work.

Key takeaways

  • Vocalize anxious thoughts through conversation rather than keeping them internal—this interrupts catastrophizing patterns.
  • Combine dialogue with physical activity, such as walking outdoors, for amplified anxiety relief.
  • Build regular socialization into your routine as a preventive measure, not just a response to acute stress.
  • Speaking thoughts aloud forces clarity and coherence, making problems feel more manageable than internal monologues allow.
