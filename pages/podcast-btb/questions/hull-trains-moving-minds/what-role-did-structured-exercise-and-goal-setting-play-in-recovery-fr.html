How did structured exercise and goal-setting help recovery from brain injury?

15 September 2026 Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Paul Spence

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Exercise and goal-setting transformed Paul Spence's brain injury recovery by providing daily structure, accountability, and a concrete path forward during an otherwise uncertain period. Starting with just five press-ups, five sit-ups, and five squats at home, he progressed to running, eventually organizing a half marathon that raised £17,000 for Hull Royal Infirmary—turning physical rehabilitation into a meaningful mission that gave his family and community tangible hope.

From Five Press-Ups to a Half Marathon: The Power of Incremental Progress

The recovery process began with simplicity. Paul's first home workout—five press-ups, five sit-ups, and five squats—was deliberately modest, but its impact was profound. This small structured routine gave his family hope that recovery was possible and provided his day and week with rhythmic purpose. When you are told you may never be the same again and face months of uncertainty, even a five-exercise routine becomes a psychological anchor.

From there, the progression followed a clear trajectory: run-walk around the block, then a full mile, then longer distances. Each milestone built on the last, creating what Paul Spence discusses in the episode as a series of achievable targets. This wasn't random fitness; it was purposeful rehabilitation with visible endpoints.

From Personal Recovery to Community Mission

The turning point came on the anniversary of his brain hemorrhage, when Paul organized a half marathon with 70 friends and family members. The event raised £17,000 for Hull Royal Infirmary—the hospital that had saved his life. This single event crystallized the psychological shift: his recovery was no longer just about him. It was about giving back, creating meaning, and transforming his struggle into a collective act of gratitude and support.

As detailed in this conversation, the goal-setting and training structure provided accountability during his darkest recovery period. When recovery is unpredictable and answers are scarce, having a tangible training plan and a community event to train for offers psychological stability and forward momentum that neurological science alone cannot provide.

"I am different, I am no less—I've been judging myself as a less of a person, and it was not fair. I needed encouragement, kindness and compassion."

If you're curious about how Paul's brain injury also affected his memory and working cognition—and what practical compensation strategies helped him manage cognitive deficits during his career—there's a deeper exploration of those challenges in the full podcast.

Key takeaways

  • Small, structured routines—even five simple exercises—provide hope and daily purpose when recovery feels uncertain.
  • Progressive goal-setting creates accountability and measurable milestones that sustain motivation through long rehabilitation periods.
  • Linking personal recovery to a community cause transforms exercise from self-focused rehabilitation into meaningful action that benefits others.
  • Training for a specific event (like Paul's half marathon) harnesses the psychological power of a shared deadline and collective support.
