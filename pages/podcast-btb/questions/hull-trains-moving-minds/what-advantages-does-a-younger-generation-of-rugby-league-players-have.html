How young rugby league players benefit from open mental health conversations today?

22 September 2026 · Hull Trains – Moving Minds · Chris Charles

← See the Hull Trains – Moving Minds fiche

Younger rugby league players have access to openly discussed mental health support and resources that simply did not exist in previous generations. They can view their career as something they love without the burden of silent suffering that characterized earlier eras, when players coped privately with no support systems at all.

A generational shift in how rugby league talks about mental wellbeing

Jack Charles, who recently signed with Hull Kingston Rovers, operates in a fundamentally different environment from his father Chris Charles, who played during the 1980s and 1990s. Chris recalls an era when mental health was barely mentioned in rugby league circles, forcing players to process their struggles alone. The contrast is striking: where Chris had to navigate mental challenges in silence, Jack benefits from a culture where wellbeing is openly discussed and normalised within the sport.

This shift matters because the absence of stigma allows younger players to address mental health proactively rather than reactively. Jack does not view his career as a burden or something he must endure—he describes it as something he loves deeply and would dedicate his life to doing. This fundamental difference in mindset stems directly from the safety and openness that modern rugby league provides.

As discussed in this episode of Hull Trains – Moving Minds, the younger generation benefits from knowing that help is available and that talking about struggles is encouraged, not hidden away.

"I don't really see it as a pressure right—like, this is what I've always wanted to do. I love it to bits and I'd die doing it, but I've never thought of it all as a burden."

The practical implications are significant: younger players now have access to mentors, counselors, and peer support networks that Chris's generation had to discover on their own or, more often, never found at all. This infrastructure—coupled with cultural permission to acknowledge vulnerability—creates a protective layer around mental health that makes the professional demands of rugby league more manageable.

What makes this generational advantage even clearer is how Chris and Jack explore their different experiences within the same family and sport, revealing how rapidly attitudes toward mental wellbeing have evolved in professional athletics.
