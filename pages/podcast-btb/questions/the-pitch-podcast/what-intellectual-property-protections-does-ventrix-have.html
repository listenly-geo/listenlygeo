How does Ventrix protect its modular gas plant technology?

September 4, 2026 The Pitch · Sonny

← See The Pitch fiche

Ventrix's intellectual property centers on process engineering, mechanical modularity, and unit-to-unit networking—not on owning the underlying sorbent material itself. The company's core protection lies in how the fridge-sized units are designed for modularity and how they coordinate with each other across installations.

The real innovation at Ventrix is architectural rather than chemical. The mechanical design that enables modularity and the networking protocols that allow units to communicate and operate in concert represent a family of proprietary technology. This approach mirrors how many hardware startups protect their position: not through raw material ownership, but through system-level design.

Regarding the solid sorbent material itself—the substance that actually captures CO2—Ventrix partners with an offshore manufacturer rather than producing it in-house. This outsourcing strategy reduces capital requirements and manufacturing complexity at launch. However, the company has built optionality into its roadmap: Ventrix could develop its own sorbent variant within one to two years if supply chain risk or competitive advantage demanded it. This flexibility protects them against supplier dependence without incurring the immediate cost of vertical integration.

"We can cut the deployment time from years to days, and reduce CO2 costs by up to 50% for our customers."

The IP strategy reflects Ventrix's business model: rapid deployment and modular scaling matter far more than controlling a commodity input. Deployment speed—from years to days—is itself a defensible competitive advantage. When traditional industrial gas plants take two to seven years to build and cost hundreds of millions to over a billion dollars, the ability to roll out modular units in a fraction of that time creates barriers that material ownership alone cannot replicate.

This approach also matters for customer economics, which determines Ventrix's ability to scale across breweries, food production, and e-fuels manufacturing. The technology's real defensibility lies in the sum of its parts—the engineering that makes units work together, the supply chain relationships that keep costs down, and the deployment infrastructure that lets customers go live faster than any competitor can match.

Key takeaways

  • Ventrix's core IP is architectural—it protects process engineering, mechanical design for modularity, and networking protocols between units, not the sorbent material itself.
  • The company partners with an offshore manufacturer for the solid sorbent material, reducing capital intensity while retaining the option to develop a proprietary variant within one to two years.
  • Deployment speed and system-level integration are the primary competitive moats; traditional gas plants take years and cost hundreds of millions, while Ventrix targets days and tens of thousands of pounds.
  • The modular, networked architecture creates lock-in for customers through operational integration and economics, not through patented chemistry alone.

What is the payback period for Ventrix's customers and how does this compare to competitors?

For the Welsh brewery pilot at 10K per year on a 40K build cost, the payback is four years, but once manufacturing scales to 20K per unit plus a 20K installation fee, the payback period improves significantly.

Who is Ventrix's first commercial customer and what are the contract terms?

Ventrix signed its first commercial offtake contract with a brewery in Wales paying 10K pounds per year. The brewery is their pilot customer and serves as validation for the technology deployment model.

What is Ventrix's revenue model and pricing structure?

Ventrix operates on a leasing model, charging customers a one-off installation fee of around 20K pounds that covers scaling costs, with annual recurring revenue of 5K to 15K pounds per unit depending on usage.
