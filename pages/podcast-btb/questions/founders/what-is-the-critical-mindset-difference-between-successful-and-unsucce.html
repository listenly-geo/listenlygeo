How do successful entrepreneurs view risk differently?

September 25, 2026 Founders · Michael Bloomberg

← See the Founders fiche

Successful entrepreneurs don't flee from risk—they embrace the thrill of the unknown as the core driver of their work. Rather than viewing challenges as dangers to avoid, they experience that gnawing feeling in their stomach as a signal they're doing something genuinely hard, something most people say cannot be done. The difference isn't courage born from ignorance; it's a fundamental rewiring of how one experiences difficulty.

This mindset shift separates those who build from those who manage. When you stop treating risk as a threat and start treating it as the price of entry into meaningful work, your entire relationship with entrepreneurship transforms. The unknown stops being a source of anxiety and becomes the source of genuine excitement and engagement.

As Michael Bloomberg explains in this episode, this isn't about recklessness. It's about recognizing that being a participant in the arena—competing against the best, creating something tangible, waking up eager to practice your craft—delivers a real high that sitting safely on the sidelines can never match.

From employee to founder: learning to live with the unknown

Bloomberg's own journey illustrates this transformation starkly. After 15 years at Salomon Brothers working 12-hour days, six days a week, he was terminated at age 39 when the firm's leadership decided to push him out. He walked away with $10 million in compensation—a safety net most entrepreneurs never have—yet he chose not to retreat to comfort. Instead, he took that capital and launched Bloomberg with a $300,000 initial deposit, betting everything on a market no one yet believed in.

The critical moment wasn't the firing itself; it was what Bloomberg didn't do. He didn't look over his shoulder. He didn't visit the old firm to reminisce. As he describes in the podcast, once something was finished, it was gone—life continues forward. That psychological clean break allowed him to redirect all his energy into the unknown territory of his startup, rather than dividing his attention between two worlds.

Bloomberg built that initial $300,000 into a billion-dollar business within 15 years—not through a five-year plan or careful risk mitigation, but through a daily commitment to showing up and outworking everyone else in the room. The thrill of the unknown was the fuel, not the obstacle.

"The more you work, the better you do. It's that simple. I've always outworked the other person."

Bloomberg's insight cuts through the noise: entrepreneurial success isn't determined by how smart you are, how well-connected, or how much capital you start with. He explores deeper in the full episode how his mindset about work itself—loving the competition, thriving on the gnawing sensation of danger, treating difficulty as proof of genuine opportunity rather than proof of foolishness—became the actual competitive advantage.

One remarkable detail Bloomberg shares is how his father never earned more than $6,000 per year, yet Bloomberg himself started at Salomon Brothers with a $9,000 annual salary (plus a $2,500 loan to cover New York living costs in 1966). That trajectory—from working-class roots through disciplined work into founding a global institution—was built on a mindset that reframed risk as invitation, not warning.

The daily practice of embracing uncertainty

The difference between unsuccessful and successful entrepreneurs often isn't a single bold decision. It's the daily choice to stay engaged with difficulty rather than retreat from it. Bloomberg describes work as a very big part of his life—not as a burden, but as something he loves. He looks forward to waking up early, practicing his profession, creating something real, and competing against the best.

This reframing—from "I have to do this hard thing" to "I get to do this hard thing"—is the essence of the entrepreneurial mindset. It's not denial of risk. It's a genuine preference for the excitement of risk over the deadness of safety. When that preference becomes real in your nervous system, not just in your head, everything changes.

Key takeaways

  • Successful entrepreneurs experience risk and challenge as invitations, not threats—the gnawing feeling in your stomach signals you're doing something worth doing.
  • The psychological ability to close one chapter completely and move forward (rather than look back) is a competitive advantage that multiplies the impact of your next venture.
  • Work quality and entrepreneurial success correlate directly with how much you genuinely enjoy the work itself, not with how much safety you've accumulated.
  • Outworking competitors through consistent daily effort and willingness to stay engaged with difficulty is a more reliable path than relying on initial capital, connections, or planning.
