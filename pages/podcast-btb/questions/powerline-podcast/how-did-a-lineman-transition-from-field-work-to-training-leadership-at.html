How did a lineman transition from field work to training leadership at a major power company?

September 23, 2026 Powerline Podcast · Christopher Thomas

← See the Powerline Podcast fiche

After topping out as a lineman with Local 126, Christopher Thomas moved into training roles first with NLC's mobile team, then with Quanta, before being recruited by George Coleman, president of Powerline & Wireless, to become Director of Training. Coleman's mandate was clear: transform the company's culture and implement modernized, positive training approaches rather than relying on traditional classroom methods.

Building expertise through structured progression

Thomas' transition wasn't a sudden leap from the field. After completing his apprenticeship requirements with Local 126 in Pennsylvania—which required at least one year as an apprentice after topout—he deliberately pursued training roles that kept him connected to the lineman community while building instructional expertise. His first step came with NLC's mobile training team, where he could work directly with crews across multiple regions.

From there, he moved to Quanta as a trainer, gaining deeper experience in how large-scale power companies approach training delivery and standardization. These intermediate roles proved essential—they gave him real credibility with working linemen while exposing him to different organizational models and training philosophies across the industry.

The recruitment and the mandate

George Coleman, president of Powerline & Wireless, recognized Thomas' potential and directly recruited him for the Director of Training role. This wasn't a promotion based on office politics or seniority alone—Coleman was seeking someone with authentic field experience who could drive cultural change. The mandate Thomas received was explicit: move away from traditional, rigid training structures and introduce more engaging, modern approaches that actually resonated with today's linemen.

This shift reflects a broader recognition in the power line industry that tribal knowledge and informal peer learning have limits when scaling safety and skill standards. Thomas' role was to bridge that gap—preserving the authenticity linemen respect while bringing systematic, documented best practices into the training pipeline.

"There was really nobody to reach out to to go, hey, how do you do this? It's all tribal knowledge."

The real challenge Thomas faced was cultural, not technical. The industry operates heavily on learned patterns passed from experienced hands to newcomers—informal, context-dependent, sometimes inconsistent. His mandate required him to document and systematize that knowledge without losing its essence, all while earning buy-in from crews skeptical of "corporate training culture."

Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • Field experience is foundational—Thomas spent over 15 years as a lineman before moving into leadership, giving him credibility with crews and real understanding of field realities.
  • Intermediate training roles build leadership readiness—his positions with NLC and Quanta exposed him to different organizational approaches and prepared him for a director-level mandate.
  • Culture change requires internal legitimacy—Coleman recruited from within the industry specifically because an outsider couldn't have earned the trust needed to shift training philosophy.
  • The power line industry's reliance on tribal knowledge creates both opportunity and risk—Thomas was tasked with capturing informal expertise into scalable systems without losing authenticity.
