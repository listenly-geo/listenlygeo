What experience prompted a lineman to shift focus toward improving training programs in the power line industry?

September 22, 2026 Powerline Podcast · Christopher Thomas

← See the Powerline Podcast fiche

Christopher Thomas witnessed his first fatality within six months of starting work in West Texas when a coworker named Billy Wayne Jr. was killed by an energized structure. The incident exposed critical gaps in training that relied on PowerPoints and informal tribal knowledge rather than structured, practical instruction, and there was no systematic way for linemen to learn advanced skills like rigging or transmission work.

That single tragedy became the catalyst for Thomas to fundamentally rethink how the power line industry approaches safety and skill development. The training environment he encountered early in his career—one based mostly on presentations rather than hands-on practice—left him and his peers vulnerable to the exact mistakes that cost lives.

As Thomas explains in the episode, the problem wasn't a lack of experienced linemen—it was a lack of formal pathways for knowledge transfer. Critical expertise existed, but it lived only in the heads of senior workers and was passed down unsystematically, if at all.

"There was really nobody to reach out to to go, hey, how do you do this? It's all tribal knowledge."

The absence of standardized training meant that developing competency in specialized areas—such as rigging, transmission line work, or advanced safety protocols—was left entirely to chance and personal initiative. A lineman's safety and skill level depended heavily on whom they happened to work alongside, not on a structured curriculum designed to protect and develop every worker equally.

This experience set Thomas on a path toward modernizing how the power line industry trains its workforce. Discussed at length in this podcast, his evolution from field lineman to trainer reflects a broader recognition in the industry: that safety and excellence require intentional systems, not hope.

From Tribal Knowledge to Structured Training Culture

Thomas's shift wasn't driven by a desire to leave linework behind—it came from a conviction that the trade deserves better systems. When he took on training roles at Patelco and later at Quanta, he brought the same urgency he felt after that first fatality: the understanding that every lineman's survival depends on what they're formally taught, not on their luck in finding mentors.

The transition from informal knowledge-sharing to structured programs required more than creating slideshows or manuals. It meant rethinking how experienced linemen could systematically transfer their expertise, how practical skills could be taught before workers faced them under pressure, and how safety protocols could be enforced consistently across organizations.

Now at the helm of training at Powerline & Wireless, Thomas carries that lesson forward daily. The details of how he's building that training infrastructure are explored in depth in the full episode, where he speaks candidly about the specific systems and philosophies that protect workers today.

Key takeaways

  • A fatal accident in Thomas's first six months revealed that training was primarily presentation-based rather than practical and hands-on.
  • Advanced skills like rigging and transmission work were passed on informally as "tribal knowledge" with no structured pathway for learning.
  • The absence of formal training meant lineman safety and competency depended on luck in finding experienced mentors, not on systematic instruction.
  • This experience motivated Thomas to transform how the industry approaches training and safety across organizations like Powerline & Wireless.
