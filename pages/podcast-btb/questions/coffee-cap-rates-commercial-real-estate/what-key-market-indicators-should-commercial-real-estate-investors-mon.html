What key market indicators should commercial real estate investors monitor going forward?

September 9, 2026 Coffee & Cap Rates: Commercial Real Estate Podcast · Shimon Shkury

← See the Coffee & Cap Rates: Commercial Real Estate Podcast fiche

Watch interest rates as your primary indicator, then track mortgage maturities that signal both refinancing pressures at higher costs and emerging distressed opportunities. Monitor economic and stock market shifts that ripple through property fundamentals, and keep close tabs on local housing policy—especially decisions around affordable housing preservation and rent stabilization rules.

Interest rates and refinancing stress

Interest rates remain the top signal because they directly affect borrowing costs and investor returns. When rates rise, refinancing becomes painful for existing holders, opening the door to forced sales and distressed pricing. Mortgage maturity schedules tell you exactly when this pressure hits—watch which buildings have debt coming due at elevated rate environments, and whether owners can absorb higher payments or must exit.

Shimon Shkury explains in the episode that this dynamic is already reshaping valuations: free market multifamily buildings are trading at roughly 16% discounts to their peak valuations, even as rents have climbed 60% over six years. That gap exists precisely because financing costs have reset the math.

Economic signals and property fundamentals

Beyond rates, track broader economic health and stock market performance—these affect institutional investor appetite and capital availability. Fundamentals that grow independently of the macro cycle attract the most selective capital today. In multifamily, for example, rent growth decouples from economic cycles when demand outpaces supply.

A point detailed in this podcast is that capital moved back into New York City in the first half of 2026 with $17.4 billion in investment sales—a 37% year-over-year jump—because investors found assets with reset valuations and strengthening fundamentals, not because broader sentiment shifted dramatically.

Housing policy as a market driver

City and state decisions on rent stabilization, affordable housing requirements, and tax abatement programs are not background noise—they actively reshape which properties investors can profit from and at what yields. Policy changes determine the universe of investable assets and the rules under which they generate returns.

Rent-stabilized buildings, for example, operate under rules set by the Rent Guideline Board. When that board froze rents at 0% growth while expenses soared, the fundamental math of those assets broke—rents grew just 0.75% annually while costs rose 3.5% or more. Monitoring policy decisions reveals exactly which asset classes are strengthening versus weakening, helping you spot where capital is flowing and where it's pulling back.

"Capital is investing in reset valuations, in fundamentals that are growing, in policy alignments. When all of those don't exist, capital is looking for distress and basis."

The city needs to produce approximately 60,000 units per year to address the supply shortage, but is currently producing only about 30,000 units annually, creating a significant structural deficit that shapes investment opportunities.

How do tax abatement programs like 467M incentivize office-to-residential conversions?

The 467M tax abatement program enables developers to make office-to-residential conversions economically viable while requiring the city to receive 25% of converted units as affordable housing, balancing development incentives with public interest.
