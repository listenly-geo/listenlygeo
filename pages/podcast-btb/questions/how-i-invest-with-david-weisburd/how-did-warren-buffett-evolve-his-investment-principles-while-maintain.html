How did Warren Buffett evolve his investment principles while maintaining discipline?

September 14, 2026 How I Invest with David Weisburd · Michael, Founder and CEO of American Securities Capital Partners

← See the How I Invest with David Weisburd fiche

Warren Buffett never abandoned his core investment framework; instead, he applied unchanging principles to evolving market conditions. The same analytical approach that made him avoid European markets in 1986 later enabled him to invest in Japan and become a major Apple shareholder—because the conditions that triggered those original decisions had fundamentally shifted, not the principles themselves.

In 1986, when Buffett was invited to speak at Stanford Business School, he explained plainly why he wouldn't touch European stocks: he didn't understand the accounting standards, didn't speak the languages, couldn't decipher the legal systems, and the market was too fragmented to offer him a genuine edge. Those weren't dogmatic rules—they were disciplined boundaries tied to his requirement to understand what he was buying.

Yet as the episode explores, everything changed when structural conditions shifted. The European Union emerged, creating unified accounting and transparent regulations. American investment banks opened deal pipelines across borders, and materials began to be professionally translated. Suddenly, the barriers Buffett had identified were no longer barriers—so his skepticism became irrelevant, and he could deploy capital with the same confidence he brought to U.S. securities.

The same logic explains his evolution on technology stocks. Buffett was famously dismissive of the technology sector for decades, insisting that he couldn't predict which firms would thrive and that the competitive moats were too uncertain. But when he could finally analyze Apple with the same rigor he applied to industrial companies—studying brand loyalty, ecosystem lock-in, and predictable cash flows—he recognized he could apply his existing principles to a business he once considered unknowable.

A remarkable detail from this conversation is how Michael, the American Securities founder, has replicated this exact discipline at his own firm: maintaining a "clear screen" about what his team is genuinely good at—U.S. industrial businesses—while the world shifted around them. He neither chased every trend nor abandoned adaptability. The lesson: staying true to your principles while being ruthlessly honest about whether market conditions still support your original thesis.

"Money is the ultimate commodity, so all private equity firms are in a commodity business, but we're really in the people business."

What separates a disciplined investor from a rigid one is the willingness to revisit the facts, not the principles. Buffett's evolution—from avoiding Europe to investing there, from dismissing tech to backing Apple—wasn't a betrayal of his framework. It was precisely what this episode demonstrates: the discipline to recognize when conditions have changed enough that the same principles now point in a different direction.

Discipline and Adaptation Are Not Opposites

The confusion often stems from confusing principles with positions. A principle is timeless—understand before you invest, seek businesses with durable competitive advantages, demand a margin of safety. A position is temporary—never own European stocks, never touch technology, only buy railroad stocks. Buffett held firm principles but was willing to abandon stale positions the moment the underlying conditions justified it.

Michael's American Securities, which has outperformed for over 30 years, demonstrates the same pattern. The firm has remained intensely focused on U.S. industrial businesses, but the specific companies, market dynamics, and leverage structures have evolved dramatically since 1994. The discipline wasn't to avoid change; it was to stay within their circle of competence while being radically honest about how that circle could apply to new opportunities.

Key takeaways

  • Buffett's refusal to invest in Europe in 1986 wasn't dogma—it was a reasoned position based on information barriers that later dissolved when the EU unified regulations and English-language translations became standard.
  • His shift from dismissing technology to becoming Apple's largest shareholder shows that the same disciplined analytical framework can be applied to business categories he once considered unknowable.
  • True discipline means regularly testing whether the conditions that justified your original decision still exist, not mechanically repeating positions after circumstances have fundamentally changed.
  • Staying within your circle of competence doesn't mean refusing to expand it—it means being honest about what you can and cannot understand, and moving only when genuine understanding becomes possible.
