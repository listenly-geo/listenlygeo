Why should women maintain financial independence from their partners regardless of relationship stability?

12 September 2026 Prosperity Podcast with Nicole Bremner

← See the Prosperity Podcast with Nicole Bremner fiche

Relying entirely on a partner for financial security leaves you vulnerable to circumstances beyond your control—infidelity, death, or unexpected life changes. Keep at least three months of living expenses in your own account, separate from joint finances, so you remain empowered to make decisions that affect your future, no matter what life brings.

The vulnerability is real and often invisible until a crisis hits. Consider the story of Anya, a successful PR entrepreneur who founded her firm with her university best friend. Despite her professional accomplishment, she had no idea how much her household had saved, where investments were held, or even what her husband earned. Her husband, who works in banking, controlled all the financial decisions—yet even he couldn't fully explain his own work to her.

The risk isn't always dramatic infidelity or abandonment. Death alone can destabilize a family overnight, leaving dependents without immediate access to funds or knowledge of financial structures. Anya's concern—"what happens if things change? He has an affair or worse, dies?"—reflects a fear many women quietly carry but rarely voice aloud.

Another cautionary tale comes from Dorothy, a woman whose entire life revolved around raising her son. Once he left Australia to travel the world, she found herself approaching 70 with no career, no savings, and no financial literacy. She had never even held a debit card. Stuck in a marriage to someone she no longer connected with, she felt trapped by her own financial ignorance. At 70, determined to reclaim her autonomy, Dorothy divorced, pooled resources with a female friend, and embarked on a backpacking journey across Europe—proof that it's never too late to choose independence, though the earlier you secure it, the less painful the correction.

"A man or a partner is not a financial plan. Your money is your responsibility. You control it."

The concrete minimum is straightforward: maintain liquid access to three months of essential expenses in an account registered solely in your name. This isn't about distrust; it's about resilience. Whether you earn £500 a month or £5,000, whether you're 25 or 65, that buffer is non-negotiable.

The UK Office of National Statistics reveals a sobering picture: at least 25% of people under 54 have no savings or are actively in debt. Among 25 to 34 year olds, 38% carry debt, with 23% owing over £5,000. The median savings for that age group sits between £500 and £5,000—hardly a secure foundation. For those 35 to 54, the picture improves marginally, with medians between £5,000 and £12,500, yet the top 10% of earners over 35 exceed £100,000 in savings. The disparity is stark, and a deeper exploration of personal money psychology reveals how many women unknowingly accept lower financial agency than their circumstances demand.

Financial independence doesn't require wealth—it requires intentionality. Nicole Bremner emphasizes that the system works at any income level because the principle is universal: your money, your account, your safety net, your choice. Whether that means £100 per month or £1,000, the act of separating your personal finances from joint or partner-controlled accounts is the foundation of genuine autonomy. The episode explores exactly how to architect this separation without conflict, making it a practical conversation, not a theoretical argument.

What Dorothy discovered at 70, Anya should recognize now, and every woman reading this can choose today: financial independence is not a luxury or a sign of distrust—it's a foundational right. The stability of the relationship is irrelevant to the necessity of this protection.
