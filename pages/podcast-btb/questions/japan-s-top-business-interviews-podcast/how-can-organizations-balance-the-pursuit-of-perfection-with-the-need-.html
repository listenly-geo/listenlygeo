How can organizations balance the pursuit of perfection with the need to move forward during transformation?

September 21, 2026 Japan's Top Business Interviews Podcast · Laura Bonamici

← See the Japan's Top Business Interviews Podcast fiche

Transformation requires movement before everything is fully defined, and the goal is not to abandon precision but to find precision through movement. Rather than arguing against legitimate concerns about governance and accuracy, start small with focused use cases in a safety space with low risk and high learning, then make the learning visible so the conversation shifts from 'is this perfect' to 'what are we learning and how can we improve it.'

This tension between waiting for perfection and acting on incomplete information is one of the most paralyzing forces in organizational change. Laura Bonamici encountered this directly during her four years as Global Head of Marketing at Fujitsu, where she led transformation initiatives aligned with the company's midterm plan and the launch of the Uvance digital services portfolio.

The insight is deceptively simple: precision and movement are not opposites. Many organizations frame this as a binary choice—either you move fast and risk mistakes, or you move carefully and miss windows of opportunity. The real practice is to design for learning in motion. This means creating bounded experiments where the stakes are managed, the risks are explicit, and the outcomes are treated as data rather than failures or successes to celebrate alone.

Small, Safe Spaces as a Foundation for Larger Shifts

When organizations face skepticism about new approaches—whether AI in marketing, digital transformation, or organizational restructuring—the instinct is often to defend against the objections. Instead, Bonamici's approach accepts the concerns as legitimate and builds them into the design. Work with governance concerns by embedding them into your small experiments, not by waiting until you have all the answers before you start.

A focused use case in a low-risk environment allows multiple things to happen simultaneously: the team gains real experience, concerns about accuracy and control can be tested against actual data, and skeptics can see what is actually working rather than argue about hypothetical risks. This approach is detailed in the episode as a core strategy for navigating the complexity of multinational transformation.

The visibility of learning is critical. When your team and stakeholders can see the learning loop—what you tried, what you learned, how you adjusted—the psychological safety around experimentation increases. The conversation stops being about whether something is perfect and starts being about whether it is improving. That shift is the turning point where transformation actually accelerates.

"Leadership is increasingly an act of translation. By that, I don't mean language translation, but context translation—the ability to move between worlds, between perspectives, between competing realities."

This idea of translation captures the essence of the balance itself. Perfection often means precision in one specific context, but transformation happens across multiple contexts—different markets, different business units, different organizational readiness levels. The episode explores how this translation works in practice, particularly in how organizations like Fujitsu adapted global ambitions to local execution.

The real competitive advantage during transformation is not having all the answers before you move. It is the ability to learn faster than your constraints change. Small, intentional experiments with visible learning loops are how organizations build that capability while still respecting legitimate concerns about risk and accuracy.
