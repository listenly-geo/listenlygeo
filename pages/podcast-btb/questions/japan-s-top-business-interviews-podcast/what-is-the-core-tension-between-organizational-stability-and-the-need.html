How do Japanese companies balance organizational stability with the need for business speed?

September 20, 2026 Japan's Top Business Interviews Podcast · Laura Bonamici

← See the Japan's Top Business Interviews Podcast fiche

The tension isn't resolved by choosing one or the other—it's solved through translation, not disruption. Japan's legendary strengths in process, alignment, and consistency remain assets, but the path to speed runs through creating protected experimental spaces where cross-functional teams can test ideas, learn from failures, and prove success before scaling, allowing velocity to emerge from proven results rather than imposed change.

Structure as a platform for controlled experimentation

Japanese organizations have built decades of competitive advantage on discipline, consistency, and clear hierarchies. These strengths haven't become obsolete—they've become the foundation for something faster. The gap isn't between "traditional Japan" and "modern speed"; it's about using structural discipline to protect spaces for learning.

Small cross-functional teams, given explicit permission to experiment within bounded timeframes and clear accountability checkpoints, can move at pace without destabilizing the larger organization. This isn't chaos imported into order; it's applied structure creating room for velocity. The six-month and twelve-month review cycles that shape transformation assignments become feedback loops rather than constraints—they mark when experiments prove themselves or reset course.

When Laura Bonamici led transformation initiatives aligned with Fujitsu's three-year midterm plans, she wasn't working against Japanese organizational DNA—she was translating global ambition and market urgency into the local language of process, alignment, and measured progress.

Market velocity has compressed; context translation has not

Technology cycles accelerate. AI becomes standard. Market windows close in months, not years. But these external pressures don't erase what made Japanese companies strong—they raise the stakes for reading context before acting.

Leadership in this environment becomes "an act of translation," moving between competing realities: the need for speed and the need for buy-in, global strategy and local execution, experimentation and accountability. This isn't a problem to solve through one grand restructuring; it's a continuous navigation between worlds—the kind that requires small teams with room to prove themselves, measured progress, and explicit permission to fail.

As discussed in the episode, this translation framework has delivered tangible results: Fujitsu's most successful fiscal year on record followed the four-year transformation cycle, published at the end of April.

"Leadership is increasingly an act of translation. By that, I don't mean language translation, but context translation—the ability to move between worlds, between perspectives, between competing realities."

The real discovery isn't that Japanese companies need to abandon structure for speed—it's that they can use protected experimental spaces to create velocity without sacrificing alignment, accountability, or the disciplines that built their reputation.

Key takeaways

  • Speed emerges from proven success within protected spaces, not from disrupting organizational structure.
  • Cross-functional teams with explicit permission to test and learn, paired with clear checkpoints, can move fast while maintaining organizational coherence.
  • Translation—moving between competing realities—is the core leadership skill when balancing stability and velocity.
  • Japanese organizational strengths in process and alignment become assets for controlled experimentation, not obstacles to overcome.
