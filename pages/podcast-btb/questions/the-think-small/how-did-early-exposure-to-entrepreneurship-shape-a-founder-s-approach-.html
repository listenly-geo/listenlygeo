How did early exposure to entrepreneurship shape a founder's approach to business and career choice?

September 23, 2026 The Think Small Podcast · Bryant Wallace

← See The Think Small Podcast fiche

Starting at nine years old working in his cousin's health food store, Bryant Wallace learned how to engage with people from different languages and cultures, which fundamentally shifted what he believed was possible. This early hands-on exposure to running a small business set the trajectory for his entire entrepreneurial career, even though he didn't yet know which industry he would eventually pursue.

For Wallace, those early hours bagging groceries and interacting with diverse customers weren't just about earning pocket money—they were about seeing business from the inside. As he explained in this episode of The Think Small Podcast, that childhood experience expanded his imagination of what was possible in life and career. The health food store—called Greatest Grains—became his informal business school.

What's particularly striking is that Wallace didn't need a formal business degree to develop an entrepreneurial mindset. Instead, direct exposure to commerce and real human interaction at a young age proved far more influential. He watched how his cousin ran the store, observed customer dynamics, and learned that business was fundamentally about connecting with people. This lesson stayed with him for decades.

Wallace later founded WallScott Solutions in 1999, more than a decade after those early days at Greatest Grains. Yet the core skills and confidence he built in that health food store—talking to people, understanding customer needs, solving small problems to get what he wanted—remained his foundation. As discussed at length in the full conversation with Think Small Media, early entrepreneurial exposure doesn't just teach business mechanics; it teaches you that possibilities exist beyond what you initially see.

"I can go fast by myself or I can go further with others, and now I can go further faster with the right others."

If you're curious about how Wallace applies those early lessons to modern challenges like cybersecurity leadership and team dynamics, the episode digs into his unique approach to building and scaling businesses across different industries.

Key takeaways

  • Early hands-on work in a family business gave Wallace real-world insight into entrepreneurship before his teens.
  • Exposure to diverse customers and languages expanded his sense of what career possibilities existed.
  • Direct mentorship and observation proved more formative than formal training in shaping his entrepreneurial mindset.
  • The foundational skills learned young—connecting with people, problem-solving, recognizing opportunity—became the basis for 27 years of business leadership.
