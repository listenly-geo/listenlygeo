Why has pessimism in emerging market pricing created a compelling entry point?

September 5, 2026 The Single Source · Nisheh Goel

← See the The Single Source fiche

Pessimism regarding India is largely reflected in current prices, creating an unusual disconnect: 2024 and 2025 were the first back-to-back years of negative foreign outflows from India in a century, yet earnings growth has actually bottomed and the government has delivered major reforms over the same period. This market sentiment mismatch suggests opportunity for contrarian investors.

Extreme Pessimism Meets Concrete Fundamentals

The prevailing negative sentiment about India today appears significantly overblown. Investors have withdrawn capital at a scale unseen in over 100 years, signaling deep skepticism about the nation's investment prospects. Yet this exodus is occurring at precisely the moment when underlying business fundamentals are strengthening rather than deteriorating.

As Nisheh Goel explains in the episode, the timing of this pessimism is particularly striking because earnings have bottomed—meaning they are poised to expand—while the government has simultaneously enacted substantial structural reforms. This creates a rare scenario where negative sentiment and positive fundamentals diverge sharply, typically a precursor to market reversal.

Historical patterns support this view: every time India has underperformed other emerging markets, that underperformance has reversed within 12 months. The consistency of this reversal cycle suggests the current pessimism phase is transitory, not structural.

"There is a significant amount of pessimism when it comes to India today. And I do think that pessimism is largely in the price."

Market Dispersion as Investment Clarity

Beyond sentiment, the actual structure of Indian markets reveals compelling opportunities. Nearly 50% of companies in the BSE 500 index have fallen more than 30% from their recent highs, despite the benchmark itself trading only slightly below its 10-year average valuation. This extreme divergence indicates that pessimism is not uniformly priced—it is concentrated in specific securities and sectors.

This dispersion matters because it shifts the investment challenge from "Is India attractive?" to "Which Indian businesses have been punished unfairly?" A point detailed in this podcast, Duro Capital's approach capitalizes on this granular opportunity set by running a concentrated portfolio of 15 to 20 high-conviction positions, where each company is underwritten to a 25% return hurdle. In a market where half the index is down 30% or more, disciplined selection among fundamentally sound businesses becomes a powerful advantage.

Key takeaways

  • 2024 and 2025 marked the first back-to-back years of negative foreign outflows from India in a century, driven by widespread pessimism.
  • Earnings growth has bottomed while government reforms have been delivered, creating a fundamental-sentiment mismatch.
  • Nearly 50% of BSE 500 companies are down over 30% from highs, indicating concentrated opportunity within the broader index.
  • Historically, India's underperformance versus other emerging markets has reversed within 12 months every cycle.
