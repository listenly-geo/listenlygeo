What were the terms of the Treaty of Versailles, and how did they contribute to the rise of Hitler and Nazi Germany?

The Treaty of Versailles stripped Germany of territories, populations, and raw materials, transferred the entire German merchant marine to the Allied powers, and imposed punishing financial reparations. These conditions triggered an economic collapse so severe that by 1923, a single loaf of bread — which had cost three marks before the crisis — rocketed to 80 billion marks, the result of hyperinflation driven directly by war reparations. The humiliation and desperation this produced created the exact political opening that Adolf Hitler exploited to build a mass movement.

Context The government that bore the weight of the Treaty of Versailles was the Weimar Republic, Germany's fragile democratic experiment established after World War I. It was this republic — already associated in many German minds with military defeat and national shame — that Hitler and the Nazi Party (National Socialist German Workers' Party) systematically dismantled on their path to power, using the Reichstag as a vehicle and grievance as their fuel.

Hitler did not simply benefit from difficult economic circumstances — he weaponized them. His movement was built around three pillars: the collective grievance of a humiliated nation, a virulent racial hatred codified in Mein Kampf, and the promise to restore German greatness. The treaty had handed him the narrative. Where the Weimar Republic struggled to provide stability, Hitler offered certainty. Where the reparations had destroyed ordinary Germans' savings and livelihoods, he offered a scapegoat and a vision. The Wehrmacht, the Luftwaffe, and the entire machinery of the Nazi state were the eventual expression of that promise — a nation rearmed and remobilized in open defiance of Versailles.
