Why should confidentiality be a two-way commitment in mentor-mentee relationships?

19 September 2026 Executive Wins Podcast · Eric Dillon

← See the Executive Wins Podcast fiche

Confidentiality must flow in both directions because mentors share sensitive real-world case studies, board packages, and live scenarios as teaching tools, which require the mentee to protect them just as the mentor protects the mentee's personal development information. Without this mutual trust foundation, neither party can fully open up.

When a mentor brings authentic business materials into the mentoring relationship—strategic decisions, board-level discussions, financial details—those materials become proprietary information the mentee must guard carefully. The mentee's confidentiality pledge isn't just a courtesy; it's essential protection for real business knowledge being shared.

Equally, the mentee may share career struggles, personal goals, or sensitive feedback about their organization. The mentor's duty is to protect that vulnerability with the same seriousness as the mentee protects the mentor's business insights. When both sides understand this mutual obligation, trust deepens immediately.

As Eric Dillon explains in the Executive Wins Podcast, he addresses this two-way confidentiality expectation directly in preliminary meetings before the mentoring relationship begins. This upfront clarity prevents misunderstandings and signals that both parties take the relationship seriously.

Rather than relying on legal agreements as the primary enforcement mechanism, Dillon views a confidentiality statement as a reinforcement of commitment—a mutual acknowledgment that what happens in the relationship stays within it. He vets mentees carefully and conducts preliminary conversations to ensure cultural alignment before moving forward, making the agreement more about shared values than legal protection.

"The primary job of leaders is to build more leaders. Like before the business success, before celebrate the results for the year or whatever the case may be, the primary job of leaders is to actually build more leaders."

The deeper insight here is that Dillon's mentoring scorecard tracks each mentee's progress and goals with the same rigor he applies to confidentiality agreements. This systematic approach ensures that confidentiality isn't just declared—it's lived consistently across all his mentoring relationships.

Building trust through transparent confidentiality frameworks

The mentee who receives a real board package or strategic scenario needs to understand that they're being granted temporary access to sensitive material. This responsibility clarifies the mentee's role: they are not just a passive learner but a trusted steward of business information. That elevation in responsibility often shifts how mentees engage with the mentoring process itself.

Preliminary meetings become the ideal moment to establish this mutual understanding. Rather than waiting until the third or fourth mentoring session to mention confidentiality, addressing it at the outset signals that the mentor takes the relationship—and the mentee's integrity—seriously. It also gives the mentee a clear framework for what "confidentiality" actually means in this context.

When mentees know they will be exposed to proprietary business insights, they mentally shift from a passive learning mode to a custodian mindset. That shift often improves retention, increases focus during meetings, and creates a stronger sense of partnership between mentor and mentee. The confidentiality commitment becomes less of a legal box-ticking exercise and more of a badge of trust.
