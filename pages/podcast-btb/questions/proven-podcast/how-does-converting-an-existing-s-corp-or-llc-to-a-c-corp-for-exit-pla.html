How do you convert an existing S Corp or LLC to a C Corp for exit planning?

September 12, 2026 Proven Podcast · Garrett Gunderson

← See the Proven Podcast fiche

The timing clock for your tax-free exit eligibility resets the moment you convert to a C Corporation. Whether you convert an LLC through a tax election, transform an S Corp into a C Corp, or launch a brand new C Corp, the three to five year requirement for accessing Section 1202 benefits starts fresh from that conversion date—not from when you originally started your business.

Conversion itself is straightforward from a legal standpoint. You have multiple pathways available depending on your current structure. As Garrett Gunderson explains in the Proven Podcast, the real decision isn't whether you can convert—it's whether the timing works for your exit strategy.

Why the conversion clock matters for your exit

The Section 1202 exclusion allows you to sell your C Corporation stock completely tax-free, but only if you've held it for the minimum required period from the conversion date. Hold for three years, and you can exclude up to $7.5 million of gains per shareholder. Wait five years, and that exclusion jumps to $15 million per partner.

This is why the conversion date becomes your new starting line. If you've been running an S Corp for ten years but only convert to a C Corp this year, you don't inherit the decade of history—you begin your three or five year countdown today. As discussed in this podcast episode, many business owners miss this critical timing detail because they work with tax historians rather than tax strategists.

The conversion itself involves a formal election with the IRS. For an LLC, you typically file an entity classification election. For an S Corp, you make a formal C Corporation election. These elections are not risky or exotic—they're straightforward tax code provisions, yet many accountants hesitate to recommend them simply because they're unfamiliar with proactive planning.

"Delaying tax and saving tax are completely different things."

The conversion timing decision also depends on your overall exit timeline. If you're planning to sell within two years, converting now to a C Corp won't help you—you'd miss the minimum holding period. But if you're eighteen months away from a planned three year point, conversion becomes a powerful move. The Proven Podcast explores exactly how to coordinate this timing with attorneys and accountants working in tandem, something most business owners never do.

Delaware is the preferred jurisdiction for C Corporation incorporation in most exit scenarios, though the specific choice depends on your current state of operation and asset protection needs. The conversion process itself—whether from LLC or S Corp—is administrative; the real complexity lies in ensuring your professional advisors understand the tax code provisions you're trying to access.

Key takeaways

  • Your Section 1202 tax-free exit eligibility clock restarts on your conversion date, not your original business launch date.
  • Three years of C Corporation ownership unlocks $7.5 million in tax-free gains per shareholder; five years unlocks $15 million per partner.
  • Converting an LLC or S Corp to a C Corp is a standard tax election, not a complex or risky restructuring.
  • Conversion timing is critical—you must hold the C Corp stock for the full required period after conversion to qualify for the exclusion.

What tax advantages do C Corporations offer compared to S Corps and LLCs?

C Corporations allow different share classes for raising capital, the ability to retain earnings without immediate taxation, medical reimbursement accounts, and access to powerful tax-free exit strategies like Section 1202.

What is the difference between delaying taxes and actually saving taxes?

Delaying tax and saving tax are completely different things. Most CPAs focus on delay strategies, filing taxes after the fact and suggesting postponement, while true tax savings eliminate the liability permanently.

What is the strategy for paying family members through a business structure?

You can pay your kids just over $15,000 per year as a tax deduction to the business while it is tax-free to the child, and you still control that money through structured business arrangements.
