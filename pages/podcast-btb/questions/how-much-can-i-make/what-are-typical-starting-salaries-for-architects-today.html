What are typical starting salaries for architects today?

September 2, 2026 How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights · Rob Dupont

← See the How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights fiche

Starting architects in Boston earn around $20 per hour while still in school, rising to approximately $25 per hour once they graduate. Entry-level compensation is notoriously low across the profession, but salary varies significantly by geography—you earn less in Texas than in major markets like Boston, New York, or Los Angeles. The payoff comes later: if you persist and eventually own your own firm, the financial picture transforms dramatically.

The early years of an architecture career demand patience and genuine passion for the work. Low starting pay is an industry standard, particularly for unlicensed graduates completing their apprenticeship hours toward full licensure. As one Boston-area architecture firm partner noted, the hourly wage reflects the training period—students and recent graduates are still building their expertise under supervision, which limits immediate earning power.

Geography plays a decisive role in compensation. Regional cost of living and market demand directly influence what firms can and will pay. Markets like Boston, New York, and Los Angeles command higher salaries due to competition for talent and higher project costs, while markets like Texas offer lower entry-level wages. This geographic disparity persists throughout an architect's early career before narrowing as experience and credentials accumulate.

Rob Dupont, whose career spans over 40 years and includes establishing his own practice, emphasizes the long-term trajectory. As he explains in the episode, the low initial salary is survivable only if you have genuine love for the profession—otherwise, the financial sacrifice early on will wear you down.

From apprentice wages to firm ownership

The shift in earning potential happens when architects transition from employees to business owners. Ownership of your own firm fundamentally changes the financial dynamic: instead of earning an hourly wage or modest annual salary, you capture project fees, build client relationships, and control your revenue stream. This progression from $20–25 per hour as a new graduate to potentially six-figure income as a principal is the real economic story of the profession.

When Rob Dupont established his own practice in the early 1990s during an economic downturn, he shifted from a salaried or hourly position into direct control of his earnings. Over decades of practice, building networks with builders, engineers, and contractors created consistent referral work—a foundation that would never have been possible on an entry-level wage. For anyone considering architecture, understanding this arc is crucial; the salary question isn't really about what you'll make in year one, but whether you can sustain yourself until you reach the partner or owner stage.

"If you're prepared to work and not make a lot of money at the beginning and you love what you do, then be an architect. If you don't love it, don't do it."

What role does networking play in an architect's career success?

The key to getting work is networking. Rob was fortunate to have people early on that saw potential in him and wanted to help him, starting with architect mentors and expanding into lasting relationships with builders and contractors.

How much time do architects spend on design versus regulatory and administrative tasks?

Rob says the vast bulk of time is spent in drawing and construction document drafting in the computer and construction document phase, because there are so many regulatory requirements, building codes, and compliance tasks to navigate.

What formal education and certification does an architect need to practice?

You need a degree in architecture, typically a five-year program. Rob attended Syracuse University from 1985 to 1990. After graduating, you must apprentice under a licensed architect before sitting for your licensing exams to become fully certified.

Key takeaways

  • Entry-level architects earn approximately $20–25 per hour in major markets like Boston, with lower rates in smaller or less expensive regions like Texas.
  • Starting salaries are notoriously low because new graduates are still completing apprenticeship requirements and gaining supervised experience before full licensure.
  • Geographic variation is significant: high-cost, high-demand markets (Boston, New York, Los Angeles) pay more than emerging or lower-cost regions.
  • The real financial payoff in architecture comes from building experience, networks, and eventually owning your own firm—requiring patience and genuine passion for the profession in the early years.
