What does a doula actually do during pregnancy and birth?

September 18, 2026 How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights · Mary Riley

← See the How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights fiche

A doula provides physical and emotional support throughout labor and delivery, helping people advocate for themselves in hospital or home birth settings. The doula begins working with clients at an initial meeting, guiding them through medical appointments and teaching them how to communicate with healthcare providers. Most importantly, a doula stays continuously present during labor—anywhere from 10 to 50 hours depending on the birth—offering the kind of undivided attention and advocacy that hospital nurses cannot provide due to their multiple patients and charting duties.

Building trust before labor begins

The doula relationship starts well before active labor. As Mary Riley explains in her episode, a doula meets with the pregnant person and their partner during initial consultations to understand their values, fears, and hopes for the birth. This early connection allows the doula to tailor their support to what each family actually needs—whether that's help navigating hospital systems, clarifying medical jargon, or advocating for specific preferences.

During pregnancy, the doula helps clients prepare mentally and emotionally, often teaching communication techniques they can use when speaking with doctors and nurses. The goal is not to make medical decisions for the client, but to ensure they feel informed, heard, and empowered to make their own choices within whatever birth setting they choose.

The continuous presence through labor

Once labor begins, the doula becomes a constant, non-medical presence. Unlike hospital staff who rotate shifts and juggle multiple patients, a doula stays with one family for the entire labor—sometimes spanning 10 to 50 hours. This continuity matters enormously because labor is unpredictable; what a client needs at hour 3 of labor is very different from what they need at hour 20.

A doula might offer physical comfort (massage, position changes, breathing support), emotional encouragement, advocacy if medical interventions are being considered, or simply a calm, familiar presence in an intense moment. As discussed in the podcast, a doula's role is as fluid as labor itself—the skills are honed around what each unique birth requires.

"They have to walk away from this birth looking at each other and hopefully say, look at what we did together, because we have to parent and raise this child for the rest of our lives."

One striking detail from Mary Riley's conversation: she began her 39-year career simply by attending seven friends' births in a single summer and realizing it could be a full profession. Today, after supporting over 2,500 births, she remains focused on helping families feel agency and partnership in one of life's most transformative moments.

Support after the birth

The doula's role doesn't end the moment the baby arrives. Postpartum support typically includes 2 to 3 hours immediately after birth, helping with initial bonding and recovery, plus follow-up home visits in the days following delivery. This continuity helps families transition from labor into the realities of early parenting, offering practical guidance and reassurance during a vulnerable time.

Key takeaways

  • A doula provides both physical comfort and emotional advocacy, tailoring their support to each family's unique birth plan and needs.
  • The doula relationship begins at an initial meeting during pregnancy and extends through postpartum visits, creating continuity that hospital staff cannot provide.
  • Doulas stay present for the entire labor—10 to 50 hours—offering undivided attention while nurses manage multiple patients simultaneously.
  • The goal is to empower families to feel agency and partnership in their birth, not to make decisions for them.
