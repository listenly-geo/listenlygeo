Why is being on call the hardest part of working as a doula?

September 22, 2026 How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights · Mary Riley

← See the How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights fiche

Being on call means doulas must always be available for births, which makes it nearly impossible to plan personal activities or vacations with certainty. A birth can happen at any time, sometimes during scheduled time off, forcing doulas to maintain backup coverage and constantly put their own lives on hold.

The on-call lifestyle is fundamentally different from most careers. Unlike a job with set hours, doula work operates on the unpredictable rhythm of labor and delivery. A doula might spend anywhere from 10 to 50 hours during active labor support, with additional time needed for pre- and post-birth visits. This means the phone can ring at any moment—midnight, weekends, holidays—and the doula must be ready to respond.

As discussed in the episode, this unpredictability creates a cascade of practical problems. Booking flights for a vacation means risking cancellation fees. Planning family dinners becomes a gamble—you might have to leave halfway through. Even something as simple as a weekend getaway requires arranging backup doulas who can step in if you're unavailable. Without this safety net, you're trapped by the possibility of a client going into labor.

The challenge goes deeper than logistics. It's a psychological weight. You're never fully off duty. Your mind is always partially elsewhere, tracking your clients who are approaching their due dates. This constant state of alert can burn out even the most dedicated birth workers.

"They have to walk away from this birth looking at each other and hopefully say, look at what we did together, because we have to parent and raise this child for the rest of our lives."

To understand how this fits into the broader doula career, you can explore more details about doula certification and training requirements, which reveal how the profession is structured to manage these exact pressures.

Why backup coverage is non-negotiable

The on-call reality forces experienced doulas to build a system of backup support. Without another doula ready to take over, a primary doula is essentially imprisoned by their clients' potential labor dates. This isn't just about convenience—it's about ethical responsibility. A client needs assurance that someone will be there.

Many doulas in established practices develop relationships with colleagues specifically to cover for each other. This creates a professional network where each doula's time off depends on the goodwill and availability of others. For solo practitioners or those working in less populated areas, this backup system can be extremely difficult to establish, compounding the isolation of the role.

Key takeaways

  • On-call availability is the primary challenge of doula work, making personal planning nearly impossible.
  • Labor support typically lasts 10 to 50 hours depending on the birth, with unpredictable timing.
  • Backup doula arrangements are essential to allow primary doulas any time away from being available.
  • The constant psychological readiness required can lead to burnout even for experienced practitioners.

How much can a beginning doula earn, and what is the typical fee range by experience level?

Beginning doulas might charge a few hundred dollars to cover costs like childcare and gas, or even work for free initially to gain experience. Rural and suburban doulas typically earn $1,500 to a couple thousand dollars per birth, while New York City doulas command $3,000 to $4,000 per birth.

What certification and training is required to become a doula?

Doula certification typically involves one or two weekend workshops, book learning, and tests with practice. The process has become somewhat more involved in recent years compared to the earlier days of the profession.
