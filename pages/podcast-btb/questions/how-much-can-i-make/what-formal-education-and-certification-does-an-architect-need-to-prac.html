What education and licensing do architects really need?

August 30, 2026 How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights · Rob Dupont

← See the How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights fiche

You need a degree in architecture—typically a five-year program—followed by apprenticeship work under licensed architects to learn the job's practical responsibilities. After that comes a state licensing exam, usually a multi-day test administered as pass or fail. Your license applies in that specific state but can be obtained in other states through reciprocity by paying a fee.

The architecture path is structured and demanding. As discussed in the episode, the five-year degree itself carries significant curriculum load because there is simply a lot to learn in order to practice the profession responsibly.

Rob Dupont attended Syracuse University from 1985 to 1990 to complete this degree requirement. After graduation, the apprenticeship phase is not optional—it is the bridge between academic training and independent professional practice. During this period, you work directly under experienced architects and gain exposure to real projects, client relationships, building codes, and the day-to-day operations of architectural firms.

The state licensing exam comes after you have logged sufficient apprenticeship hours. This examination covers technical knowledge, ethics, and the legal responsibilities of the profession. As explained in the podcast, it is administered over several days and is graded on a simple pass-or-fail basis—there is no middle ground.

One practical advantage of the licensing system is portability across state lines. If you are licensed in one state and want to practice in another, you do not need to retake the full exam. Instead, reciprocity agreements allow you to obtain a license in the new state by paying a fee, typically between $200 and $300. This makes it possible for architects to relocate without starting the credentialing process from scratch.

"If you're prepared to work and not make a lot of money at the beginning and you love what you do, then be an architect. If you don't love it, don't do it."

The apprenticeship phase is essential, not symbolic

Many aspiring architects underestimate the importance of the apprenticeship. It is not a formality—it is where you actually learn how buildings get built, how clients communicate, how budgets work, and how your design decisions affect construction. Details on this critical transition are covered in the podcast, where Rob Dupont reflects on his own apprenticeship experience and its lasting impact on his independent practice.

The licensing structure ensures that every architect in a given state meets a baseline standard of knowledge and competence. This protects the public and maintains the integrity of the profession.

Key takeaways

  • A five-year degree in architecture is the required first step; there is no shortcut to this formal education.
  • Apprenticeship under licensed architects is mandatory and provides hands-on training in professional practice, client management, and construction realities.
  • The state licensing exam is a multi-day, pass-or-fail assessment that must be completed before you can practice independently.
  • Licensing is state-specific but portable—reciprocity agreements allow you to practice in other states by paying a fee rather than retaking the exam.

What inspired Rob Dupont to become an architect as a child?

A building in Providence, Rhode Island designed by McKim, Mead & White inspired him. The classical building looks like a miniature U.S. Capitol with a dome, lit up at night, and was particularly memorable during family road trips through the Boston area.
