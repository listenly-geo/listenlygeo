Is artificial intelligence a threat to architects' jobs?

September 4, 2026 How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights · Rob Dupont

← See the How Much Can I Make? — Real Jobs. Real Stories. Career Insights fiche

AI is not a threat to architects right now because it functions as just another tool that makes work more efficient—much like CAD and digital photography did before it. When clients bring AI renderings to Rob Dupont, they actually help clarify what the client wants rather than forcing him to invent solutions from scratch.

Rob Dupont has been practicing architecture for over 40 years and has yet to employ any AI technology in his own workflow. The conversation around artificial intelligence replacing architects overlooks a fundamental reality: tools evolve, but architects adapt. The shift from hand-drawn blueprints to CAD software was once feared as a job killer. Instead, it accelerated the profession and expanded what architects could accomplish.

When clients use AI renderings to communicate their vision, Dupont sees concrete value. Rather than presenting vague descriptions or rough sketches, AI-generated images give architects specifics to work with. This eliminates guesswork and accelerates the design conversation. Clients get heard more clearly, and architects get actionable direction—a tangible productivity gain that serves the entire project.

"If you're prepared to work and not make a lot of money at the beginning and you love what you do, then be an architect. If you don't love it, don't do it."

Dupont's optimism rests on a historical pattern. As explained in the episode, every major shift in architectural technology—from digital rendering to parametric design tools—was initially seen as existential. None of them eliminated the profession; instead, they changed how architects work and what they can deliver. The human judgment required to translate vision into buildable, livable, and beautiful spaces remains irreplaceable.

The real concern Dupont expresses is not replacement but erosion: he hopes AI does not completely eliminate everything humans can do. This reflects a genuine anxiety—not about tools themselves, but about whether society will still value the creative and human problem-solving that architects bring. That's a philosophical question, not a technical one. As long as clients need buildings that work for people, architects will be needed to translate intent into reality.

Adaptation over apocalypse

History suggests architects will adapt, just as they always have. The profession has survived the rise of photography, the introduction of computers, the automation of drafting, and the emergence of AI image generators. Each wave required learning new skills, but none erased the need for architects. The next 40 years of architecture will almost certainly look different from the last 40, but the core work—understanding what people need and designing spaces to meet those needs—will remain fundamentally human.

Dupont's message to aspiring architects and working professionals is clear: don't fear the tool; master it. AI will become part of the architect's toolkit, just as CAD did. The architects who thrive will be those who understand how to use AI to clarify client intent, accelerate iteration, and explore design possibilities faster. Fear of replacement is misplaced; the real opportunity lies in embracing efficiency and focusing energy on the irreplaceable parts of architectural work: judgment, creativity, and empathy for how people live.

How does owning your own architecture firm change earning potential compared to working for others?

Having your own firm makes a significant difference in earning potential. Rob's partner in Boston who specializes in building envelope work and operates as a firm partner demonstrates dramatically higher earnings compared to architects working as employees.

What are typical starting salaries for architects today?

According to Rob's colleagues in the Boston area, firms currently pay architecture students still in school approximately $20 per hour, with recent graduates starting around $25 per hour once they enter the workforce full-time.

What role does networking play in an architect's career success?

Networking is the foundation of consistent architectural work. Rob credits early mentors and professional relationships with sustained referrals and career stability throughout his four-decade practice.

Key takeaways

  • AI is a productivity tool for architects, not a replacement technology—similar to how CAD and digital photography transformed but did not eliminate the profession.
  • Client-generated AI renderings actually clarify project intent and reduce back-and-forth, making the architect's job more efficient and focused.
  • The irreplaceable skills in architecture—judgment, creativity, and understanding how people live—remain fundamentally human and cannot be automated.
  • Professional adaptation, not fear of technology, has kept architects thriving through multiple waves of innovation over the past 40+ years.
