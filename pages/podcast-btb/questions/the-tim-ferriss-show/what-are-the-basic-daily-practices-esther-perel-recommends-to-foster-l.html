What are the basic daily practices Esther Perel recommends to foster longevity and emotional depth in a marriage?

August 28, 2026  ·  The Tim Ferriss Show

← See the full Tim Ferriss Show page

Esther Perel's answer is deceptively simple: the foundation of a lasting marriage is daily appreciation. Say thank you. Make compliments. Hold hands, kiss, send sweet notes, bring coffee to bed, buy flowers — small gestures that signal to your partner that they live inside you. She adds two deeper practices: write letters to tell the story of your couple, and actively encourage your partner to pursue what they love and become the best version of themselves.

Small gestures are not trivial — they are the architecture of closeness

Perel is explicit about one trap couples fall into: they stop doing the nice things they once did naturally, simply because they now share a life together. Familiarity becomes an excuse for neglect. Her correction is direct — the gestures don't become less important with time, they become more important.

The list she offers in this episode of The Tim Ferriss Show is deliberately concrete: holding hands, stroking, kissing, a handwritten note, flowers, breakfast prepared, coffee brought to bed. None of these require scheduling or a therapist. They require only the decision to keep doing them.

The logic behind the list matters as much as the list itself. Each gesture functions as a signal: "you are still present in my mind, even when we are not talking, even when we are tired, even when life is heavy." That signal, repeated daily, is what Perel describes as keeping the other person alive inside of you.

Letter writing and growth: the two practices that go deeper

Beyond the daily micro-gestures, Perel singles out letter writing as a practice of particular depth. A letter is not a text message. It is an act of reflection — a way to articulate what you feel, what you remember, what you value in the other person. She frames it as a way to tell the story of the couple, which is itself a form of intimacy that conversation alone rarely reaches.

This is a theme explored throughout The Tim Ferriss Show's episode #880, which assembles some of the sharpest relationship thinkers — from Perel to Brené Brown, Terry Real, and Dr. Sue Johnson — around the single question of how to stay close to those closest to you over time.

The third pillar is less obvious but equally central: encouraging your partner to pursue what they love and be the best version of themselves. Perel positions this not as a passive act of support, but as an active investment in the vitality of the relationship itself. A partner who is growing, engaged, and fulfilled brings that energy back into the couple.

"Behind every criticism, there's often a wish. Instead of asking them to correct it, you basically tell them that you can never do it well — just ask them to do it better next time, rather than 'would it kill you?'"

This quote captures the same underlying philosophy as her daily practices: small, intentional language choices — like replacing a chronic complaint with a specific request — reshape the emotional texture of a relationship far more than grand gestures or difficult conversations. The friction lives in the daily, and so does the repair.

For anyone looking to put these ideas into practice, the full conversation is available on The Tim Ferriss Show on Listenly, alongside contributions from Brené Brown on the 80/20 energy rule in partnerships, Sheila Heen from the Harvard Negotiation Project on humor and curiosity as tools, and Brad Feld on the monthly "life dinners" he and his wife have held consistently for over 20 years.

Key takeaways

  • Daily appreciation — thank-yous, compliments, physical affection, small gifts — is not optional background noise but the core infrastructure of a long marriage.
  • Letter writing creates a form of intimacy that ordinary conversation rarely reaches: it tells the story of the couple back to both partners.
  • Actively supporting your partner's personal growth and passions is itself a relationship practice, not separate from it.
  • The shift from criticism to wish-expression ("ask them to do it better" rather than "would it kill you") is a microtool with outsized daily impact — part of Perel's broader approach detailed in episode #880.
