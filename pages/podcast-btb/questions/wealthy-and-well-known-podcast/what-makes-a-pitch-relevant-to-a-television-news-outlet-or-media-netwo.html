What makes a pitch relevant to a television news outlet or media network?

September 25, 2026 Wealthy and Well-Known Podcast · Jamie Maglietta

← See the Wealthy and Well-Known Podcast fiche

Your pitch succeeds when it speaks to two simultaneous needs: the news cycle in that moment and the network's immediate programming strategy. The moment a producer is lining a show—whether with a holiday-themed segment or a breaking news angle—your idea must solve a real problem they're facing right now, not next month.

Think about the rhythm of television. If a show is planning a cooking segment around Memorial Day, a cookbook author pitching a barbecue technique becomes instantly relevant. That same pitch in January lands in the trash. As Jamie Maglietta explains in the Wealthy and Well-Known Podcast, success requires understanding where your audience sits in that moment and how you can provide genuine value to the network's viewers.

The second dimension is audience awareness. You're not pitching to make yourself famous—you're pitching to solve the network's problem of serving their viewers. When you can articulate how your expertise directly addresses what that audience needs right now, producers stop seeing you as another pitch and start seeing you as a solution.

"I have so many examples of people that are controlling their brand in a way that's so strategic that they are not chasing press—press is coming to them."

This distinction matters because it flips the entire dynamic of pitching. Most people chase media coverage as if they're the story. Strategic pitchers position themselves as the answer to a genuine need the network has right now. The difference between being ignored and being actively sought after often comes down to this single reframing.

One more layer: preparation allows you to be precise. When you know exactly which networks serve your audience, when you understand their current programming gaps, and when you can articulate your value in the language they use, you eliminate the friction that causes pitches to fail. As discussed in this podcast episode, the networks that see the most bookable talent are those who've clearly done their homework.

Key takeaways

  • Relevance requires alignment on two fronts: where the network is in its production calendar and what the current news cycle demands.
  • Your pitch must solve a real problem for the network's audience right now, not offer generic expertise that could work anytime.
  • Strategic positioning means the network comes to you because you've proven you understand their needs—not chasing coverage as if you're the story.
  • Preparation creates precision, which eliminates the friction that causes most pitches to be ignored or deleted.
