How did the Dining Terrace at Westfield Century City redefine food court experiences in American shopping malls?

September 5, 2026 The Crexi Commercial Real Estate Podcast · Paul Kurzawa

← See the The Crexi Commercial Real Estate Podcast fiche

Westfield Century City reimagined the food court entirely by introducing table service, proper cutlery including knives and forks, and a full scullery operation—transforming a utilitarian dining concept into a genuine restaurant-quality experience. The model was built on Westfield's earlier success at Westfield Bondi in Sydney and represented cutting-edge dining experience design for American shopping centers at the time.

This was far more than a cosmetic upgrade. Rather than rows of self-service stations and disposable serviceware, Kurzawa and the Westfield team created what they called the Dining Terrace—an approach detailed in the podcast—with dedicated staff clearing and resetting tables just as in a standalone restaurant. The back-of-house infrastructure included a full scullery, meaning dishes were properly washed and cycled rather than discarded.

The operational shift was deliberate and ambitious. Staff presence at tables created an entirely different shopper psychology—the space felt curated and intentional, not just a necessary break between retail browsing. This investment in operational detail signaled to visitors that Westfield understood dining as part of the broader placemaking experience, not as an afterthought squeezed into a basement corner.

From Sydney Innovation to U.S. Shopping Center Standard

Westfield did not invent this concept in Los Angeles. The Dining Terrace at Century City was a direct adaptation of what the company had pioneered earlier at Westfield Bondi in Sydney, bringing Australian retail innovation to American mall culture. Paul Kurzawa's career across both markets, starting in Sydney at Penrith Plaza before moving to the United States in 1996, gave him firsthand insight into how different markets valued dining experiences.

At the time—during Kurzawa's tenure at Westfield, which spanned approximately 8 to 9 years—this approach to food service in American malls was genuinely ahead of its time. Most U.S. shopping centers still operated traditional food courts with minimal service layers. Westfield's willingness to invest in staff, infrastructure, and operational complexity positioned Century City as a destination where dining mattered as much as shopping.

The broader lesson embedded in the Dining Terrace concept reflects Kurzawa's philosophy across his 25-year career: listening to how customers actually use spaces and then investing deliberately in those moments. A food court is not incidental; it is a key touchpoint in a visitor's journey through the mall. Treating it with the same care as any other retail area elevates the entire property's perception.
