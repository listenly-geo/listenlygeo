How did Ray Smith handle disrespectful behavior from a touring artist?

September 20, 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Ray Smith

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Ray Smith pulled a Gallagher brother from Oasis into a private room during their Sydney Entertainment Centre tour and calmly laid out expectations: respect is non-negotiable when working together. He made it clear that disrespectful behavior wouldn't be tolerated, and the next day, the artist apologized. The artist's London management later thanked Smith for being the first person to establish that boundary.

What made Smith's approach effective wasn't aggression—it was directness combined with professionalism. He didn't shame the artist publicly or escalate the situation. Instead, he removed him from the immediate environment and communicated a simple truth: mutual respect is the foundation of any working relationship.

This moment reveals a principle that Smith had clearly learned during nearly 30 years managing touring musicians—confrontation works best when it's grounded in respect for the other person, even when you're correcting them. Smith treated the artist as someone capable of change, not someone to be written off.

The apology the next day wasn't forced or defensive. It came from genuine recognition that the artist had crossed a line. In touring, where weeks on the road can amplify tension and ego, having a tour manager willing to reset standards is rare. Smith's willingness to have that difficult conversation, rather than letting bad behavior slide, ultimately strengthened the working relationship.

The tour manager as boundary-setter

In the music industry, the tour manager is both facilitator and enforcer. Smith understood that his role wasn't to be liked—it was to ensure the tour ran smoothly and professionals behaved like professionals. A touring artist at their peak can believe they're above basic courtesy, especially on the third night of a tour when exhaustion and adrenaline are mixing.

What makes Smith's intervention remarkable is that the artist's own management backed him up. The London team later acknowledged that Smith was the first to hold that artist accountable. This suggests the behavior was a known pattern—but no one in the artist's circle had been willing or able to address it until Smith stepped in.

This is where experience matters. Smith had already managed the Rolling Stones and Joe Cocker—acts at the absolute top of the industry. He knew what professionalism looked like at scale. He also knew that one moment of directness, delivered with respect, could reset an entire working relationship. Key takeaways

  • Confrontation delivered privately and respectfully is more effective than public shaming or avoidance.
  • The tour manager's role includes holding artists accountable, even at the height of their success.
  • Professionalism standards, when clearly stated, often earn respect rather than resentment—the apology came voluntarily the next day.
  • An artist's own management may not be able to enforce boundaries that a tour manager on the ground can establish through presence and clarity.
