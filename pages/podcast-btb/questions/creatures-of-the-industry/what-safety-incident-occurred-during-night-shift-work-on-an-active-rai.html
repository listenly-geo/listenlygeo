What safety incident occurred during night shift work on an active railway bridge construction?

23 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Ray Smith

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

During the Long Walk railway bridge project, overhead power lines that should have been switched off remained live while workers in scissor lifts operated between them at night. The union health and safety crew intervened just in time, preventing what could have been a catastrophic accident.

The incident unfolded on what Ray Smith describes as a hairy night when the operational logistics broke down completely. Workers were positioned in scissor lifts—elevated work platforms requiring precise positioning—on an active railway bridge where the infrastructure overhead carried live electrical current. The danger was not theoretical: a single contact with energized lines would have been fatal.

What made this near-miss particularly grave was the communication failure at the core of it. The power lines were supposed to be de-energized as a safety precaution before night shift work began, a standard protocol on any active railway construction site. The fact that they remained live—with workers already in position and beginning operations—meant that nobody's safety systems had functioned. As Smith explains in the episode, it took the union health and safety representatives being on-site and vigilant to catch the error before tragedy struck.

The broader lesson embedded in this incident is straightforward: union presence and active health and safety monitoring saved lives. On a night when the formal safety checklist had failed, when the electrical contractor or rail operator had not properly communicated the de-energization, and when management procedures had not caught the gap, it was the union representatives—workers embedded on the site—who conducted a final, critical safety audit.

This is not an abstract safety philosophy. In the full episode, Smith recounts the incident as a moment when the informal safety culture of the construction site—the willingness of experienced workers to speak up and stop work—proved more reliable than any signed-off checklist. It was the kind of catastrophe that would never have appeared in accident statistics because it was averted before it happened.

If you want to understand how such a gap could occur on a major infrastructure project, Smith's account explores the coordination pressures and human factors that create these blind spots in otherwise professional operations.
