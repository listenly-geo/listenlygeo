Why do oil and gas companies keep safety plans locked away on shelves?

5 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Terry Riggs

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Major oil and gas companies like Woodside and Esso maintain extensive documentation claiming "world's best safety practice" that remains unused and unopened in filing folders. When incidents occur, these companies conduct investigations that target the least powerful workers as scapegoats while the safety documentation never leaves the shelf—a systemic failure to implement the safety systems they publicly promote.

This contradiction sits at the heart of corporate safety culture in heavy industry. According to Terry Riggs, a scaffolder and health and safety representative with decades of experience across major Australian industrial projects, the gap between documented safety protocols and actual workplace practice creates a dangerous two-tier accountability system.

Documentation as a liability shield, not a safety tool

The real purpose of these safety folders, as described in the episode, appears to be legal protection rather than worker protection. Companies can point to their comprehensive safety documentation in court or regulatory proceedings, even as workers on the ground operate without access to or awareness of these protocols.

When incidents trigger "root and branch investigations," the focus shifts away from systemic failures in safety management and toward individual workers—typically those with the least power to defend themselves. Riggs witnessed junior workers subjected to intensive scrutiny, questioned for weeks, and ultimately dismissed, while the safety systems themselves escaped examination.

The irony is stark: the same documentation that companies tout as evidence of their commitment to "world-class safety" becomes invisible the moment a worker is injured or killed. It sits on shelves, as Riggs puts it bluntly, and "never come off the fucking shelf." This reveals a structural failure where safety management is decoupled from actual safety practice—a gap that transfers risk from the company's systems to the individual worker.

"The majority of a construction worker's wage isn't his hourly rate. It's his overtime and other conditions. That's what I missed."

Key takeaways

  • Safety documentation at major companies serves as legal cover rather than operational guidance for workers.
  • Incident investigations typically blame individual workers rather than examining failed safety systems.
  • The gap between documented "best practice" and actual workplace implementation creates systemic risk.
  • Junior workers absorb accountability for failures in safety management structures.
