How did custom and practice keep scaffolders safe before regulations?

2026-09-08 Creatures of the Industry · Terry Riggs

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Before written safety regulations existed, experienced scaffolders passed knowledge directly to younger workers through hands-on mentorship, making custom and practice more effective than formal rules. A skilled lead hand would watch over newcomers carefully, teaching them the trade and ensuring they understood the work before moving them into dangerous positions high in the air.

In the era before scaffolding work was heavily regulated, the construction site itself became a school. As described in Creatures of the Industry, the relationship between experienced and apprentice scaffolders was built on observation, correction, and earned trust—not compliance with written procedures.

The lead hand as safety keeper

The most critical figure in this informal system was the lead hand, whose watchfulness kept younger workers alive. This person held authority not through a title but through demonstrated competence and years of working safely on complex projects. They knew every hazard before it became dangerous.

A lead hand would train newcomers by placing them in progressively more challenging positions—never throwing an untested worker into work at height until they had proven themselves capable. This gradual exposure ensured that by the time someone was working 70 metres in the air, they had internalized the rhythms and risks of the job through months of supervised practice.

Belonging to a gang meant knowing the code

Custom and practice was also about integration into a work community with unwritten but absolute standards. A scaffolder didn't just learn techniques; they learned what it meant to be part of a gang. This sense of belonging created accountability—letting down the gang or breaking an unspoken rule carried social weight that often exceeded any formal penalty.

As discussed in the episode, the question "If you can't get on with people, you're no good" captured this reality. Technical skill alone was insufficient; trustworthiness, reliability, and willingness to look out for your workmates were equally valuable. The gang would remove someone who didn't fit, regardless of their scaffolding ability, because working at height with unreliable people was fatal.

"If you can't get on with people, you're no good. Doesn't matter if you're a super scaffolder. We'll just get rid of you and get two other people."

The strength of this system lay in its immediacy. If someone made a mistake, the lead hand saw it in real time and corrected it on the spot—not in a formal incident report weeks later. Mistakes that could kill you were addressed within seconds.
