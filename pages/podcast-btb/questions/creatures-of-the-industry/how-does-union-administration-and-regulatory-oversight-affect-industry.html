Does union administration really shift industry culture?

15 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Ray Smith

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

No, changing administrations won't shift industry culture on its own. Government overreach causes far more harm to workers than administrative shifts—Ray Smith compares the current era to the ABCC period, where construction workers had fewer rights than convicted murderers and rapists.

Ray Smith's perspective challenges the notion that simply rotating leadership or adjusting regulatory frameworks will meaningfully protect workers or reshape how the industry operates. The real damage, he argues, comes from government bodies that prioritize monuments to their own legacy over accountability for the conditions workers face on site.

Government agencies, Smith contends, pursue agendas that benefit the state rather than the workforce. They seek visible projects to justify their tenure but accept no responsibility for workplace deaths, injuries, or exploitation that occurs during execution. This structural indifference places the burden of member protection entirely on unions themselves.

In the episode, Smith explores the specific parallels between the ABCC era and modern regulatory overreach, drawing on his lived experience as both a worker and a union representative. His argument rests on a simple premise: **unions must be the primary safeguard for members** because governments will never take genuine responsibility for worker safety or dignity.

The implication is stark. Industry culture shifts only when union power remains strong and vigilant—not when governments change personnel or issue new policies. Protecting workers requires constant defense against both negligent employers and government bodies that view construction sites as mere backdrops for political achievement rather than spaces where human lives are at stake.

What are the most memorable aspects of a career spanning multiple construction disciplines and roles?

The fond memories are just the mateship within construction, the characters I've met along the way. The highlight was definitely going from job to job, meeting good people and developing lasting friendships across trades.

What role did the monthly scaffold meetings at Trades Hall play in maintaining industry standards and knowledge sharing?

The scaffold meetings at Trades Hall, chaired by Normie Wallace, created a forum for direct knowledge transfer among workers, allowing standards to be set and maintained through peer discussion and collective learning in a collaborative setting.

How essential was interpersonal cooperation and gang cohesion to effective scaffolding work?

Scaffolding is not an individual pursuit. Experienced workers emphasize that if you can't get on with people, you're no good at the work, no matter how technically skilled you are—cohesion and trust are non-negotiable.
