How did a music industry professional transition into construction labouring in 2004?

21 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Ray Smith

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Ray Smith simply rang Steve Longy, a contact in the construction industry, and asked for a job as a labourer after he had finished his career in music. Longy agreed, and in January 2004, Smith's first job was at Coot Island doing the old redevelopment for a company called Ruleway, initially intending to stay just 12 months.

A Clean Break from Music

Smith's transition was straightforward but decisive. He had reached a point where he needed to step away from the music industry entirely. Rather than pursuing another role within the same field, he sought something completely different—physical labour that would offer a genuine break from what he had been doing.

His friendship with Steve Longy made the pivot possible. When Smith explained he was willing to work as a labourer and that he only needed about 12 months away, Longy accepted the proposal without hesitation. The informal nature of the arrangement—a verbal agreement with a mate he trusted—set the tone for what would become a permanent shift in his career. There was no formal recruitment process, no industry experience required; just a willingness to do the work.

As described in the episode, Smith arrived on site at Coot Island in the summer of 2004, ready to take on his first construction role as a TA (trade assistant) with Ruleway.

From the Music Industry to the Building Site

The shift from touring and managing high-profile artists to labouring on a construction site represented a dramatic change in environment. Where Smith had once worked in the fast-paced, high-stakes world of music promotion and artist management, he now found himself on a building site, exposed to summer heat and winter cold, working alongside tradespeople and other labourers.

What Smith discovered on his first day proved transformative. The camaraderie and mateship on the construction site exceeded his expectations, offering a completely different social dynamic from the music industry. As he would later reflect on his first weeks at Coot Island, the culture of collective effort and mutual respect among the workers immediately appealed to him, making what was supposed to be a temporary escape into something far more meaningful.

"I loved the camaraderie and the mateship and just the fun these blokes were having on site. The jokes were endless."

Smith's twelve-month break never ended. What began as a temporary escape evolved into a permanent career, driven by the unexpected sense of belonging he found on the building site. The twelve-month plan gave way to decades of construction work, union organising, and a deep commitment to the industry and the workers within it.
