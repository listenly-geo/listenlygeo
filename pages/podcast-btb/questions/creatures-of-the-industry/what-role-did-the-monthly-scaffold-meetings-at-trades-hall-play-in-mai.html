How did Trades Hall scaffold meetings shape industry standards and learning?

13 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Terry Riggs

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

The monthly scaffold meetings at Trades Hall, chaired by Normie Wallace, functioned as a collaborative learning forum built on peer accountability and lived experience. Held in the round with workers seated on wooden benches facing each other, these gatherings became a place to learn custom and practice through debate and discussion, with Wallace ensuring rank-and-file members held each other to standards based on actual hands-on knowledge.

A semicircle of accountability

The physical setup of these meetings reflected their purpose: workers arranged in a semicircle created a family-gathering atmosphere where no one person stood above the rest. This horizontal layout broke down hierarchy and invited equal participation. Everyone could see everyone else, making it harder to hide inaccuracy or dismiss concerns as coming from an outsider.

Normie Wallace's role as chair was not to lecture but to enforce honesty. As discussed in Creatures of the Industry, Wallace was unrelenting at pulling people up when they misrepresented facts or mentioned someone doing something wrong. He did not allow talk to drift into gossip or rumor; instead, he grounded every discussion in verifiable experience and actual practice.

Learning through debate, not decree

These meetings were not training sessions run by management or external experts—they were spaces for workers to learn custom and practice through peer-to-peer debate. A scaffolder sharing a method had to defend it against scrutiny. Someone describing an accident or near-miss faced questions about their own role and judgment.

This approach ensured that industry standards were not just written rules handed down from above. Instead, they emerged from collective wisdom. The meetings reinforced what worked on real job sites and what didn't, as Terry Riggs explains in this episode, drawing on his decades of experience in scaffolding from Barry Beach to the Shell Refinery.

"If you can't get on with people, you're no good. Doesn't matter if you're a super scaffolder. We'll just get rid of you and get two other people."

This quote, shared by Riggs, captures the culture embedded in those Trades Hall meetings: technical skill alone was never enough. A scaffolder had to prove they could work within a team, respect collective decisions, and accept accountability. The monthly gatherings were where that standard was tested, reinforced, and passed on to the next generation of workers joining the industry.

The podcast also explores how scaffold construction methods evolved and the safety risks that emerged when shortcuts were taken, providing deeper context into why these standards mattered so much to the scaffolding trade.

Key takeaways

  • Trades Hall meetings were held in a semicircle on wooden benches, creating a family-gathering atmosphere and horizontal structure that invited equal participation.
  • Normie Wallace, the chair, enforced accountability by challenging speakers who misrepresented facts or mentioned wrongdoing, grounding debate in actual experience.
  • These gatherings functioned as peer-to-peer learning forums where custom and practice were debated and refined by rank-and-file workers, not imposed from above.
  • The meetings reinforced a cultural standard: technical skill was inseparable from interpersonal cooperation and willingness to be held accountable by fellow workers.

How essential was interpersonal cooperation and gang cohesion to effective scaffolding work?

Scaffolding is not an individual pursuit. One experienced scaffolder told Terry that if you can't get on with people, you're no good, no matter how skilled you are—a principle reinforced at every Trades Hall meeting.

What structural failures occur when scaffold standards are not staggered properly?

When standards are not staggered and joins all align on one level, the scaffold can fail catastrophically. Terry witnessed a collapsed demolition scaffold that reinforced why these technical standards were non-negotiable.

What safety risks emerged from scaffold construction methods using boom lifts instead of conventional hanging methods?

When a scaffold was built using a boom lift rather than conventional hanging construction, fittings were not all done up properly, creating serious safety hazards that would have been caught and debated at Trades Hall.
