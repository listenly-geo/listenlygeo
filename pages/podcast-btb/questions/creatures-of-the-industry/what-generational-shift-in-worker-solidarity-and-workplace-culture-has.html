How has worker solidarity shifted in Melbourne construction over 20 years?

6 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Terry Riggs

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Union membership has shifted from a foundation of shared values and workplace comradeship to a purely transactional relationship driven by money and hourly rates. On major projects like the Metro Tunnel, workers no longer experience the meaningful interaction with colleagues that once defined construction culture. As Riggs observes, "the brothers have just had it too good for too long and they've lost perspective"—union solidarity no longer binds workers through adversity, but rather fragments them into individual wage-seekers.

From comradeship to cash transactions

The transformation reflects a fundamental rupture in how construction workers relate to their labour and to each other. Where earlier generations understood union membership as a collective commitment rooted in shared struggle and mutual protection, current workers treat it as a contractual arrangement focused on extracting maximum hourly compensation.

This shift extends beyond attitude to material working conditions. As Terry Riggs explains in the podcast, the majority of a construction worker's wage is not the hourly rate itself but the overtime and conditions stacked on top—a reality that has reshaped how workers calculate value and view their union membership.

Loss of adversity as social glue

Earlier periods of construction work were marked by hazardous conditions that forged genuine workplace bonds. Meaningful interaction with workmates through shared hardship created the foundation of construction culture. These bonds were not sentimental—they were survival mechanisms born from industry-wide dangers, from the scaffolding heights to the refinery heat.

The contemporary construction worker, particularly on high-profile projects like the Metro Tunnel, no longer experiences this crucible. Without that shared adversity, the social fabric that once held workers together has unravelled. The result is what Riggs terms the absence of "solidarity and no comradeship"—a workplace stripped of the collective identity that once transcended individual wages.

"The brothers have just had it too good for too long and they've lost perspective."

What were the working conditions and labour practices on a 2,000-person industrial project in Port Hedland during the late 1990s?

The Port Hedland project was highly casualized and de-unionized with no union solidarity whatsoever. Workers were paid at half a dozen different rates, fragmenting the collective workforce and undermining traditional union protections.
