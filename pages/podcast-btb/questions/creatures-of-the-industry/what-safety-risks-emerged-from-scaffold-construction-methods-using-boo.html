What safety risks emerged from scaffold construction methods using boom lifts instead of conventional hanging methods?

10 September 2026 Creatures of the Industry · Terry Riggs

← See the Creatures of the Industry fiche

Boom lifts introduced a critical construction flaw: not all fittings were properly completed during assembly. When a scaffold was built using a boom lift rather than conventional hanging methods, the transom fitting could go unfinished while the ledger fitting was secured, leaving tubes vulnerable to collapse.

Terry Riggs witnessed this danger firsthand in Port Hedland around 1998. At a 70-metre tower, a tube dropped because while the ledger fitting had been tightened, the transom fitting remained incomplete. The tube fell with terrifying speed—saved from catastrophe only by a vessel's protrusion that broke its fall. Without that chance obstruction, Riggs and his off-sider would have come off the scaffold entirely.

This wasn't an isolated mishap. The shift from conventional hanging scaffold methods to boom lift assembly fundamentally changed how work was sequenced on site. In hanging scaffold construction, workers had time and physical access to verify every fitting was properly torqued before moving to the next stage. Boom lifts accelerated the process but introduced gaps in the workflow where supervisors might assume fittings were complete without verification.

As discussed at length in this podcast episode, the real danger lay in the assumption. A ledger fitting done up could mask an incomplete transom fitting on the opposite side of the joint, creating an asymmetric load condition that destabilized the entire vertical support.

"If you can't get on with people, you're no good. Doesn't matter if you're a super scaffolder. We'll just get rid of you and get two other people."

The deeper issue revealed in Riggs' account is that boom lift convenience removed the built-in verification steps that older methods enforced. In traditional hanging scaffold work, the physical labour of manual assembly meant multiple workers had to touch and check each joint. Boom lifts compressed that labour—and accidentally compressed the safety checks along with it.

Incomplete fittings created asymmetric load failure

The fittings on a scaffold joint work in pairs: the ledger connects the scaffold frame horizontally to the building or structure, and the transom supports the platform perpendicular to it. A completed ledger with an incomplete transom creates a one-sided anchor point, making the structure vulnerable to twisting and pulling forces it wasn't designed to resist.

At 70 metres, even a partially supported tube becomes a falling object with fatal kinetic energy. The margin for error at that height is zero. What makes this failure mode particularly dangerous is its invisibility—a worker standing on the platform might see a fitting that looks secured without any way to verify the joint on the far side is also torqued. The boom lift method removed the redundant human verification that custom and practice had built into the older system.

What were the physical toll and occupational health impacts on contract scaffolders carrying heavy loads?

Most scaffolders wore out their knees or backs from carrying heavy, concentrated loads, typically on one shoulder without alternating much. Decades of one-sided strain left permanent damage across the workforce.

How did custom and practice function as safety and skill transmission in pre-regulation scaffolding work?

Custom and practice was the act in circumstances where there was no act, and it was actually more powerful than formal regulations. Experienced scaffolders embedded safety knowledge into daily work through observation and hands-on guidance.

What was the training pathway for scaffolders in Australia during the 1970s and 1980s?

In the 1970s, scaffolders obtained a permit valid for at least 12 months to 2 years, during which they recorded everything they'd done and learned, then presented their logbook to secure permanent qualifications through formal assessment.
