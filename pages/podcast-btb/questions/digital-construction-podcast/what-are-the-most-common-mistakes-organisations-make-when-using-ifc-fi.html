What are the most common mistakes organisations make when using IFC files, and how should they be used correctly?

The most common mistake is straightforward: designers export an IFC file with no specific instructions, producing a massive, unmanageable file that contains every attribute and geometry element — whether the receiving party needs it or not. The correct approach is for clients to specify exactly which parameters and level of detail are required before the export is even configured, so that the resulting file serves a defined, practical purpose.

According to Eric Bugeja, speaking on the Digital Construction Podcast, the frustration that surrounds IFC in the industry largely comes down to a lack of education — on both sides of the exchange. Neither the geometry limitations nor the data limitations of IFC are well understood by those requesting or those producing the files. The result is a format that gets blamed for problems that are actually caused by how it is used, not by what it is capable of.

What is an IFC file? IFC (Industry Foundation Classes) is an open, ISO-standardised file format developed by buildingSMART International to enable the exchange of building and infrastructure data across different software platforms. Unlike proprietary formats, IFC files are designed to remain accessible for 30 years or more without requiring a specific software version — making them a foundational tool for long-term asset management and interoperability.

The solution is not technical — it is organisational. Before an IFC export is produced, the client or receiving party should define exactly what attributes are needed, how the geometry should be structured, and what the file is intended to do. This is precisely the role that IDS (Information Delivery Specification) was designed to fill: it allows clients to formally specify information requirements so that an IFC export can be validated against them. Without that clarity upstream, even a technically correct IFC export becomes unusable in practice.

"AI systems are only as good as the data that they are trained on. If our information environments are fragmented and inconsistent and ungoverned, then AI doesn't solve that problem. It actually scales it."

— Eric Bugeja, buildingSMART Australasia & buildingSMART International

This observation about AI applies equally to IFC. A poorly scoped export that floods a downstream team with irrelevant geometry and unstructured attributes does not become useful just because it is in an open format. The discipline of specifying information requirements — knowing what you need and communicating it clearly — is what separates a functional IFC workflow from a frustrating one.

What are the risks of vendor lock-in for engineering firms and asset owners, and how do open standards help?

If one or two software vendors become dominant and an organisation's data is locked into their proprietary format, that organisation faces serious risk if those vendors change their terms, discontinue a product, or raise prices. Open standards such as IFC provide a vendor-neutral format so that data remains accessible regardless of which software is used.

What is buildingSMART International and what does it actually do?

buildingSMART International is a global industry association that develops open standards — such as IFC, IDS, and BCF — enabling information to be shared across software platforms and throughout a building or infrastructure asset's entire lifecycle, without dependence on any single vendor.
