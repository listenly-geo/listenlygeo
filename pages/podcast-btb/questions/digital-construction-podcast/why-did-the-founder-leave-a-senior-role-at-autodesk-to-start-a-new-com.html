Why did the founder leave a senior role at Autodesk to start a new company?

September 17, 2026 Digital Construction Podcast · Dominique Pouliquen

← See the Digital Construction Podcast fiche

While leading ReCap at Autodesk, Pouliquen identified a fundamental problem: the entire workflow was stuck on the desktop with no way to share massive point cloud data via the cloud. When he connected with PhDs from a French lab who had developed compression technology to solve this exact problem, Autodesk wasn't interested in acquiring it. Rather than shelve the solution, he and the three co-founders decided to build the platform independently, and Pouliquen left Autodesk around 2017 to invest his own capital and launch Cintoo.

Desktop constraints and a missing piece

At Autodesk, Pouliquen had overseen the ReCap team in San Francisco, managing large-scale point cloud workflows across Revit, AutoCAD, and C3D. The platform worked—but it was constrained. Every major operation required desktop-level computing power, and sharing massive datasets remained cumbersome. Point clouds, which consist of millions of individual 3D coordinates, were difficult to move, collaborate on, or access from anywhere.

The practical challenge was acute: how do you enable teams on a construction site or across an office to view, share, and annotate high-resolution point cloud data without requiring specialized hardware or lengthy downloads? As Pouliquen explains in the episode, Autodesk's ecosystem was built for desktop workflows, not cloud-first collaboration.

The technology Autodesk declined

The turning point came when Pouliquen met PhDs from a French research lab who had cracked a critical piece of the puzzle: compression technology that could turn raw point clouds into 3D mesh surfaces, drastically reducing file size while preserving fidelity. This wasn't just a compression trick—it was a fundamental data transformation. By converting unstructured point clouds (which you can see through) into continuous 3D surfaces (which behave like real-world geometry), the data became both more interpretable and far smaller.

Pouliquen brought the technology to Autodesk's leadership, but the company declined to acquire it or integrate it into ReCap. The reasons were likely strategic—Autodesk was focused on its existing desktop-centric products and enterprise licensing model. Rather than accept that decision, Pouliquen and his co-founders chose to pursue the opportunity independently.

"Everything takes longer than expected, and usually it's three times longer than what you think."

The founding happened around 2017, and Pouliquen invested his own money as a business angel to get Cintoo off the ground. His willingness to take that personal financial risk underscored his confidence in the core technology and the market need he'd observed firsthand at Autodesk.

What makes this transition instructive is that it wasn't born from frustration alone—it was grounded in a specific, unsolved problem and access to proven technology. Pouliquen had the industry knowledge, the technical validation, and the co-founder partnership to execute. The decision to leave a senior position at a Fortune 500 company was calculated rather than impulsive.

Today, Cintoo operates at a very different scale, with over 110,000 active users relying on its platform to stream high-resolution 3D meshes in a web browser—a capability that was not feasible when Pouliquen was at Autodesk. The company has validated the market demand that Autodesk's leadership had not yet recognized.

The technology converts huge point clouds into 3D meshes or 3D surfaces, which solves the problem that point clouds are like 3D dots in space where you see through them. By transforming point clouds into continuous surfaces, the data becomes more interpretable and highly compressible—all while preserving the resolution of the original scan.
