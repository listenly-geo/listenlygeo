What false vaccine claims has Kennedy made as Health Secretary?

September 22, 2026 Up First from NPR

← See the Up First from NPR fiche

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. claimed the MMR vaccine contains fetal tissue, a claim that is factually incorrect. Additionally, Democratic members of Congress accused Kennedy of lying during his confirmation hearing about a 2019 trip to Samoa, claiming it had nothing to do with vaccines when new documents suggest otherwise.

The vaccine ingredient claim

Kennedy's assertion about fetal tissue in the measles vaccine contradicts established medical fact. The MMR vaccine has been extensively studied and verified to be free of fetal tissue. This claim circulates widely in anti-vaccination circles but lacks any scientific support.

As Up First from NPR covered in this episode, Kennedy's statements on vaccines have come under increasing scrutiny, particularly as measles cases and deaths have resurged in the United States.

The Samoa testimony discrepancy

During his confirmation hearing, Kennedy denied that his 2019 trip to Samoa was related to vaccines. However, congressional Democrats produced new documents suggesting the trip had significant connections to vaccine-related activities, contradicting his sworn testimony.

The Samoa visit occurred during a measles outbreak on the island, raising questions about Kennedy's consistency in addressing vaccination matters. This discrepancy between his testimony and the documentary evidence forms part of the broader scrutiny his tenure has faced, as detailed in the full podcast episode.

What was Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s response to the Pennsylvania measles deaths?

Secretary Kennedy questioned whether the deaths were real, saying he was trying to figure out who those deaths were and whether they actually happened.

What is the current measles situation in Pennsylvania and what deaths have been reported?

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro announced two deaths linked to measles, the first measles deaths in 2026 and the first in Pennsylvania in 35 years.

Are modern El Niños stronger than historical ones, and what causes this difference?

Modern El Niños are significantly stronger than historical ones, with climate scientist Julia Cole's research showing they are about 36 percent stronger than pre-industrial El Niños.
