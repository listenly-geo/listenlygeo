Trump says the US has a "steel wall" around the Strait of Hormuz — but does the reality match the rhetoric?

President Trump told reporters that the United States Navy holds full control of the Strait of Hormuz, describing the US naval presence as an "infallible blockade" and a "steel wall" through which only ships with permission can pass. NPR White House correspondent Franco Ordoñez pushed back sharply on that framing, noting that very few ships are actually moving through the Strait and characterizing Trump's claims as not tethered to reality.

According to Ordoñez, reporting for Up First from NPR, the bombastic language reflects something more political than operational: a president determined to project strength at a moment when the standoff with Iran is producing no clear wins. With the Strait of Hormuz effectively paralyzed — very little commercial or other traffic moving in either direction — Trump's insistence on framing this as a US victory reads as an attempt to avoid any perception that Iran is dictating terms.

The broader context deepens that reading. Iran's demand for war reparations prompted Trump to counter with his own sweeping demand — compensation for damages allegedly inflicted on the US over fifty years, invoking incidents including the USS Cole attack. Yet with no ships freely transiting the Strait, the economic and geopolitical cost of the stalemate continues to mount, and the gap between Trump's declared "steel wall" and the chaotic standoff on the ground grows harder to ignore.

"It looks to me like the Iranian regime wants to turn President Trump into Jimmy Carter. It wants to impose the kind of humiliation on President Trump that they imposed on President Carter. And I think they're pretty much succeeding at that." — Analyst cited on Up First from NPR, August 11, 2026
