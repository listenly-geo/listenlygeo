What is castoreum and how was it historically used?

August 29, 2026 Up First from NPR · Lauren Frayer

← See the Up First from NPR fiche

Castoreum is an anal secretion produced by beavers, historically used as a scent marker similar to how dogs mark territory. Medieval perfumers valued it for its vanilla-like aroma, while Romans consumed it as a form of aspirin, believing it held powerful medicinal properties. The intense demand for castoreum, combined with hunting pressure for beaver fur and meat, drove the species to extinction in Britain by the early twentieth century.

This remarkable substance emerged as one of the most sought-after commodities in pre-modern Europe, revealing how deeply human civilization became entangled with beaver biology long before modern conservation efforts began. The practice illustrates how historical demand for animal products can reshape entire species' survival across continents.

As reported in Up First from NPR, the story of castoreum is inseparable from Britain's broader relationship with beavers — a relationship that ended in complete elimination before recent decades brought the species back.

Medieval perfumery and the vanilla-scented treasure

The demand for castoreum in medieval times stemmed from a single prized quality: its distinctive vanilla-like scent. Perfumers across Europe incorporated the secretion into luxury fragrances destined for nobility and the wealthy, making it a marker of status and refinement.

This olfactory appeal created a sustained economic incentive to hunt beavers, not just for their famously dense fur, but specifically for this specialized gland. The value placed on castoreum demonstrates how pre-industrial markets could drive species toward extinction based on a single biological feature, a dynamic documented extensively in this episode.

Roman medicine and castoreum as aspirin

Ancient Romans took castoreum consumption even further, treating it as a medicinal compound with analgesic properties. They believed the secretion could relieve pain and illness, essentially serving as an early form of aspirin centuries before acetylsalicylic acid was synthesized.

Whether the Romans understood the biological mechanisms behind castoreum's effects remains unclear, but the practice demonstrates how animal-derived remedies became embedded in medical tradition. This historical use underscores a pattern explored in the podcast: humans have long extracted value from beavers in ways that threatened their survival.

The path to extinction in Britain

The combined pressures of castoreum harvesting, fur trapping, and meat hunting compressed into a devastating timeline: beavers, once widespread across Britain, vanished entirely by the early twentieth century. This extinction reflected centuries of unrelenting demand for every usable part of the animal.

The disappearance was so complete that when conservation efforts began in the twenty-first century, Britain had to import beavers from Norway to restart the population — a restoration documented in detail across the reporting shared here. Today's reintroduction represents a reversal of a historical trajectory driven partly by the medieval appetite for castoreum.

Castoreum: A yellowish secretion produced by the castor sacs of beavers, located near the anus. Beavers use castoreum naturally to mark territory and communicate with other beavers. Historically harvested for use in medieval perfumes and Roman medicine, the secretion possesses a vanilla-like scent and was believed to have analgesic properties.

Key takeaways

  • Castoreum, a beaver anal secretion, was prized in medieval Europe for its vanilla-like aroma and incorporated into luxury perfumes for nobility.
  • Romans treated castoreum as a medicinal compound with pain-relief properties, using it as an early equivalent to aspirin.
  • Demand for castoreum, combined with hunting for fur and meat, contributed significantly to beavers' extinction in Britain by the early twentieth century.
  • The historical overexploitation of beavers highlights how single-product demand can drive species to extinction across entire regions.

Why do beavers build dams even in Britain where their natural predators no longer exist?

Beavers build dams instinctively to raise water levels so they can hide underwater from predators such as wolves and bears. Even though these natural predators no longer exist in Britain, the instinct remains deeply encoded in beaver behavior.

How are beavers being used to fight wildfires in the United States?

Beavers have been released into fire-damaged areas of Washington state following wildfires across the American West. Research has found that areas with beaver wetlands retain more moisture and are better protected against future fire spread.

What are 'beaver bombers' and why are they causing controversy in Scotland?

Beaver bombers are renegade wildlife enthusiasts who illegally release unlicensed beavers into areas — particularly large farming zones in Scotland — where government reintroduction programs have not authorized their presence.
