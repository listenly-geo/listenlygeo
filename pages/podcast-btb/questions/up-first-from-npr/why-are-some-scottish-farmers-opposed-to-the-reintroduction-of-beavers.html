Why are some Scottish farmers opposed to the reintroduction of beavers?

August 22, 2026 Up First from NPR · Lauren Frayer

← Voir la fiche Up First from NPR

Scottish farmers argue that beavers dam irrigation channels, flood food crops, collapse riverbanks, and fell centuries-old trees. On top of that, Scottish law makes it illegal to disassemble a beaver dam on private property if it has been in place for two weeks or more — leaving farmers with no practical recourse over what happens on their own land.

Kate Maitland and the National Farmers Union: concrete damage, not abstract concern

Kate Maitland, representing the National Farmers Union in Scotland, is among the most prominent voices laying out the agricultural case against beaver reintroduction. The concerns she articulates are not theoretical. Beavers build dams instinctively and prolifically — and those dams can redirect or block the irrigation channels that farmers depend on to manage water across their fields.

The consequences include flooded food crops and destabilized riverbanks. Beavers also fell trees, including specimens that have stood for centuries. These are real, recurring losses that hit working farms directly, as Lauren Frayer documents in this episode of Up First from NPR.

A law that strips farmers of agency on their own property

The legal dimension sharpens the conflict considerably. Under Scottish law, it is illegal to disassemble a beaver dam on private property once it has been in place for two weeks or more. Two weeks is not a long time. A dam built over a single weekend can, within a fortnight, become legally untouchable.

This means a farmer who discovers a dam blocking a drainage channel cannot simply remove it without risking a legal penalty — even on land they own. The rule was designed to protect a newly reintroduced species, but its effect is to transfer control of private agricultural land to a wild animal and the state. This is the friction explored in depth in the episode, which frames the beaver reintroduction not as a unanimously celebrated conservation success, but as a genuinely contested policy.

"The beavers can do it, probably a fraction of the cost, probably better than we can, certainly more sustainably for the environment."

Frayer's reporting gives weight to both sides. The same animals celebrated for solving a chronic flooding problem at a London Underground station — at a fraction of the cost of conventional engineering — are, in agricultural Scotland, a source of legal helplessness and economic damage. As she makes clear in this edition of Up First, the reintroduction of beavers is a policy with real winners and real losers.

Britain's beaver reintroduction program has its legal roots in a 1992 EU directive requiring member states to investigate species driven to extinction by human activity and explore their return. Beavers were hunted to extinction in Britain in the early 20th century, at a point when only around 1,200 remained across all of Europe and northern Asia. The 2009 arrival of the first two Norwegian beavers in Napdale Forest marked the beginning of a reintroduction experiment that has since spread — and generated exactly the kind of land-use conflict Frayer investigates, a tension documented fully in Up First from NPR.

What other wildlife species have returned to areas where beavers have been reintroduced in Britain?

At the Ealing Beaver Project in West London, the return of beavers triggered a cascade of biodiversity recovery. Within weeks, freshwater shrimp arrived — and the site has since recorded 8 new bird species and 2 new bat species, with bat count rising from 4 to 6 species.

How cost-effective are beavers compared to traditional flood engineering solutions in Britain?

Municipal authorities had budgeted hundreds of thousands, if not over a million pounds, for conventional engineering works — large diggers, earthworks, and infrastructure. Beavers delivered comparable flood mitigation at a fraction of that cost, more sustainably and without heavy machinery.

How did beavers stop the flooding at a London Underground tube station?

Wildlife conservationist Elliot Newton and veterinarian Sean McCormick relocated beavers into a 20-acre park adjacent to the tube station — a former golf course — which the beavers transformed into a water-retaining wetland within weeks of their arrival, ending years of recurrent flooding.

Key takeaways

  • Beavers can dam irrigation channels, flood crops, collapse riverbanks, and fell ancient trees — causing direct, recurring economic losses for Scottish farmers.
  • Scottish law prohibits removing a beaver dam from private property once it has been in place for two weeks, stripping landowners of practical control over their own land.
  • Kate Maitland of the National Farmers Union Scotland is a central voice articulating the agricultural opposition to reintroduction.
  • The conflict sits within a broader tension: the same EU-driven reintroduction program that benefits urban flood management creates legally enforced burdens for rural farming communities.
