How did Robert F. Kennedy Jr. respond to Pennsylvania's measles deaths?

September 21, 2026 Up First from NPR

← See the Up First from NPR fiche

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. questioned whether the measles deaths in Pennsylvania actually happened, stating the administration was trying to verify the deaths' reality. He declined to offer condolences to families, did not urge unvaccinated people in affected areas to get vaccinated, and made a false claim that the MMR vaccine contains fetal tissue.

Kennedy's response stood in sharp contrast to the gravity of the situation. As reported in the episode, Pennsylvania had experienced its first measles deaths in 35 years—a significant public health milestone that typically prompts swift official action and clear guidance to vulnerable populations.

Instead of providing reassurance or public health recommendations, Kennedy's skepticism about the deaths themselves created confusion. His suggestion that the vaccine contains fetal tissue—a claim contradicted by medical and pharmaceutical evidence—added a misleading layer to an already delicate public health crisis.

The most striking element of his response was the absence of standard public health communication. No guidance to unvaccinated individuals in Pennsylvania's affected areas. No outreach to the families who had lost loved ones. The full episode details how this response contrasted sharply with how measles outbreaks are typically handled by health officials.

Questioning the deaths rather than addressing prevention

Kennedy's initial statement—"We're trying to figure out who those deaths were and whether they actually happened, and we can't figure that out yet"—raised immediate concerns. The deaths had been publicly announced by Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, making the Health Secretary's doubt appear to undermine official state records rather than engage with the crisis.

This approach represented a departure from conventional public health protocol, which typically prioritizes rapid response, community education, and vaccination campaigns when outbreaks occur. Instead of these measures, the response focused on verification delays and vaccine misinformation.
