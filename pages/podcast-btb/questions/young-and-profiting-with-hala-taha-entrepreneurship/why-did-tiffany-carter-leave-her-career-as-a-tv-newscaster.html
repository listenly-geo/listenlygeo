Why did Tiffany Carter walk away from her TV newscaster career?

Tiffany Carter originally became a TV newscaster because she wanted to state facts and be heard — but once the visibility of the role put her in front of the public, it triggered deep, unresolved trauma she had never addressed. When people began recognizing her in public and she had to cover disturbing stories such as gang rapes, she became consumed by the feeling that everyone could see through her facade and that she was a fraud. She ultimately quit, and went from appearing on television to picking up dog waste on Ventura Boulevard in Los Angeles, before entering an outpatient trauma healing program.

Carter's story is not simply about a career change — it is about what happens when external success collides head-on with internal wounds that have never been treated. The visibility that comes with being on camera every day forced her to confront feelings of worthlessness she had been suppressing for years. Rather than continue performing a version of herself she did not believe in, she chose to stop altogether. That descent — from TV anchor to picking up dog waste on a Los Angeles street — reflects just how completely her sense of self had collapsed beneath the pressure of being seen.

Carter later shared this chapter of her life on Young and Profiting with Hala Taha, framing it as one of the pivotal moments that set her on a path toward genuine healing — and eventually, toward building the business career she is known for today.

"Without self-worth, you sell your soul and you start doing things or you avoid doing things that would get you to where you want to go so much faster."

— Tiffany Carter, Young and Profiting with Hala Taha, Episode 411

About Tiffany Carter

TC
Tiffany Carter
Entrepreneur · Business Coach

Tiffany Carter's trajectory is one of the most striking in modern entrepreneurship. She became a millionaire by age 30, building her wealth through a top-performing career in pharmaceutical sales — where she was consistently the number one salesperson and eventually a sales trainer. Within approximately three months of reaching that financial milestone, she lost nearly everything after a financial advisor defrauded her by investing her money in fake gold mines. Before her rise in sales, Carter worked as a TV newscaster — a role that, rather than fulfilling her, exposed the depth of trauma she had been carrying since childhood. She has been diagnosed with complex PTSD stemming from abuse she experienced between the ages of 11 and 21, and she underwent an outpatient trauma healing program after hitting rock bottom. Around 18 years ago, after repeatedly encountering a glass ceiling in the corporate world, she launched her own digital marketing business — a move that came with its own brutal lessons, including pitching 50 companies and landing only 2 clients. Carter speaks with authority on the intersection of trauma, self-worth, and business performance precisely because she has lived every dimension of that relationship firsthand.

What is Tiffany Carter's number one piece of advice for selling without being manipulative?
Carter's top sales tip is to stop talking too much. She explains that people over-talk when they are nervous, eager, or excited — and that the seller needs to step back and listen instead of filling every silence with words.
Why did Tiffany Carter fail to close deals when she first pitched her digital marketing agency?
Carter pitched 50 companies when she launched her digital marketing business and landed only 2 clients. She attributes this failure to being visibly desperate and lacking the self-worth needed to show up confidently in sales conversations.
How does childhood trauma and abuse affect entrepreneurial performance and financial success?
Carter describes being raised in a highly abusive environment from ages 11 to 21, which left her with no self-worth despite an ability to mask it outwardly — and that unaddressed trauma quietly sabotaged her business decisions and financial outcomes for years.
