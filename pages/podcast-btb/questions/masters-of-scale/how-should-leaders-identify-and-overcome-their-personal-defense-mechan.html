What defense mechanisms do leaders use when facing uncertainty?

September 18, 2026 Masters of Scale · Brené Brown

← See the Masters of Scale fiche

Leaders default to self-protective behaviors—what Brené Brown calls "armor"—when facing uncertainty and fear. Common patterns include perfectionism, micromanagement, and over-decisiveness. The path forward is developing honest self-awareness about your own go-to defense mechanisms, rather than asking direct reports to identify them for you.

Uncertainty and risk trigger fear in every leader, regardless of experience level. In this conversation with Reid Hoffman at the Masters of Scale Summit, Brené Brown explains that fear itself is not the barrier to courageous leadership—armor is. Armor is the immediate protective reflex we reach for when we sense vulnerability, uncertainty, or exposure.

Each leader develops their own signature armor based on their temperament, background, and early experiences. Some leaders tighten control through micromanagement to feel safe. Others pursue perfectionism, believing flawlessness will prevent failure or criticism. Still others become over-decisive, rushing to conclusions to avoid sitting with ambiguity. None of these patterns is inherently wrong—they all work, in the short term.

The problem emerges when armor becomes automatic. As Brown details in the episode, leaders who cannot name and understand their own defense mechanisms end up broadcasting them to their teams—creating culture, shaping decisions, and limiting psychological safety without ever acknowledging what's driving the behavior.

The self-awareness gap: Why leaders can't ask their teams

Many leaders instinctively turn to 360-degree feedback or direct reports to understand their blind spots. But this approach fails when it comes to armor. Power differentials prevent honest answers from people who report to you. A direct report knows that naming their boss's anxiety-driven perfectionism or controlling tendencies carries career risk.

This is where courageous leadership begins: the willingness to examine your own armor without outsourcing that work. Brown emphasizes that if you lack the insight or courage to think honestly about how you self-protect when afraid, the legitimacy of leading people should be questioned. Who you are is how you lead.

Self-awareness here means recognizing the moment you reach for armor. It means noticing when anxiety, uncertainty, or fear triggers your go-to protective move—and asking yourself what you're afraid of in that moment. This is the foundational work that makes courageous leadership possible.

"Courage is teachable, measurable, and observable. It's a skill set that's made up of four things."

The deeper reason this matters is simple: your team will default to the same armor you model. If you lead through perfectionism, your organization learns to hide mistakes. If you lead through over-control, your people stop thinking independently. If you lead through over-decisiveness, psychological safety evaporates. The armor you reach for becomes the culture you build.

The antidote is vulnerability—not as weakness, but as clarity. When you acknowledge your own fear, name the armor you reach for, and show your team that you're working to stay in integrity despite that fear, you create permission for them to do the same. That's the difference between a leader who manages compliance and one who enables genuine courage in others.

What are the core components that make up courageous leadership as a teachable skill?

Courage is teachable, measurable, and observable—made up of four key components including clarity about your values, the ability to stay in integrity and vulnerability, and the willingness to not reach for armor when facing uncertainty, risk, and exposure.

Key takeaways

  • Armor—perfectionism, micromanagement, over-decisiveness—is what stops leaders, not fear itself.
  • Each leader has a signature defensive pattern shaped by their background and early experiences.
  • Power differentials make it impossible to ask direct reports to identify your armor for you.
  • Self-awareness about how you self-protect is non-negotiable for courageous leadership.
  • The armor you model becomes the culture you build; your team will mirror your protective patterns.
