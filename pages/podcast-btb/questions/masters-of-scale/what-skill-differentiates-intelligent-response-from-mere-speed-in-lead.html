What skill differentiates intelligent response from mere speed in leadership during times of disruption?

September 22, 2026 Masters of Scale · Brené Brown

← See the Masters of Scale fiche

Leaders must develop anticipatory, temporal, and situational awareness—the ability to read what they cannot see, similar to a quarterback's pocket presence. This skill allows leaders to appear to slow down the game without creating more time. The real distinction is pursuing strategic shit done (GSSD) over mere getting shit done (GSD)—action aligned with impact, not just movement.

The challenge facing modern organizations is the confusion between productivity urgency and strategic urgency. Many leaders confuse speed with effectiveness, pushing their teams into constant motion without asking whether that motion serves the organization's long-term direction.

As Brené Brown discusses in this episode, the mental model that separates excellent leaders from those merely keeping pace involves understanding when to take risks and when to take strategic risks—decisions grounded in values and long-term positioning rather than reactive firefighting.

Anticipatory awareness replaces reaction

The quarterback metaphor holds real power here: experienced players develop the ability to sense defenders they cannot yet see, anticipating the pocket's collapse before it happens. This same skill applies to leadership in disruptive environments. Leaders who cultivate temporal awareness can sense market shifts, organizational vulnerabilities, and opportunity windows before they become obvious to competitors or become crises.

This is not about having perfect foresight. It is about developing the pattern recognition that comes from deep situational awareness—understanding your organization's strengths, blind spots, and the ecosystem around it well enough to act before being forced to act.

"Courage is teachable, measurable, and observable. It's a skill set that's made up of four things."

The framework Brown describes—courage as a teachable skill—underpins this broader shift from speed to strategic action. The full conversation explores how leaders build this framework through vulnerability and clear values alignment, creating organizations where action and impact move together rather than pulling apart.

Strategic risk-taking demands a different mindset

Risk-taking and strategic risk-taking are not the same. The former can look like courage but often masks anxiety or pressure to produce results. The latter requires knowing your organization's core values, understanding which risks align with long-term vision, and choosing action only when both preparation and purpose align.

Leaders operating from this foundation ask different questions before moving: not "How fast can we respond?" but "What does the right move look like here, and do we have the clarity to execute it?" The Masters of Scale episode dives deeper into how leading with discipline, accountability, and humility creates conditions where this kind of decision-making becomes possible, even under pressure.

The organizations that survive disruption are not necessarily the fastest. They are the ones where leaders see clearly enough to act strategically, where speed serves direction rather than replacing it.

Key takeaways

  • Anticipatory awareness—the ability to read situations before they crystallize—is a learnable skill, not innate talent.
  • Strategic urgency differs from productivity urgency; the former requires alignment with values and long-term positioning.
  • Leaders who slow the game through situational awareness outperform those who simply move faster.
  • Strategic risk-taking requires discipline and clarity about organizational values, not just courage to act.
