Why does rebranding a humanitarian organization in conflict zones require a phased country-by-country approach?

September 25, 2026 Masters of Scale · Jada McKenna

← See the Masters of Scale fiche

Community recognition is a safety mechanism. In conflict zones and remote areas where organizations operate without armed guards, the local population's trust in the established brand—including its colors and identity—is the sole barrier between staff safety and vulnerability. A phased transition allows communities time to connect the new brand with the same trusted organization.

When Prosper Global (formerly Mercy Corps) undertook its rebrand, the organization faced a reality unique to humanitarian work: 90 percent of staff live and work in the communities they serve. These teams depend entirely on community relationships, not security infrastructure. A sudden brand shift across all 30+ countries of operation risked breaking those fragile bonds of recognition and trust.

Government officials in contested governance situations added another layer of complexity. As Jada McKenna explains in the episode, authorities needed time to adjust to the new organizational identity and reestablish formal relationships with the renamed entity. In places where state capacity is fragmented or challenged, this bureaucratic recalibration cannot be rushed.

"We cannot settle for survival. We can't respond to choosing between today's crises and helping communities prepare for tomorrow."

The phased approach also reflects operational realities on the ground. In regions like Syria, where 14-year conflict has been followed by large-scale population returns, rebranding must be calibrated to local readiness. Staff working in Aleppo or Idlib cannot simply switch signage overnight—the organization's presence and legitimacy depend on sustained local recognition.

Protecting operational safety while modernizing organizational identity is a tension unique to humanitarian organizations operating without institutional security. The full conversation explores how McKenna navigated this balance across Nepal, Syria, Sudan, and Ukraine, each with distinct governance and security conditions that shaped the rebrand timeline.

Key takeaways

  • In conflict zones, staff safety depends entirely on community trust and brand recognition—not armed security—making sudden rebranding a safety risk.
  • A phased country-by-country transition allows communities and government officials time to reconnect the new brand with the established, trusted organization.
  • Operating in over 30 countries means rebrand complexity multiplies across different governance contexts, conflict states, and local relationships.
  • Humanitarian organizations cannot separate organizational change from operational risk—strategic initiatives must account for ground-level safety implications.

Leaders today must develop anticipatory awareness, temporal awareness, and situational awareness—the ability to read what they cannot see, similar to strategic foresight in crisis response.
