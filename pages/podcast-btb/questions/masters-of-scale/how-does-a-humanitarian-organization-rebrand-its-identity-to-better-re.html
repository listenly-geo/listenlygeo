How does a humanitarian organization rebrand its identity to better reflect evolved mission scope beyond traditional emergency relief?

September 24, 2026 Masters of Scale · Jada McKenna

← See the Masters of Scale fiche

Organizations outgrow their founding identities just as people do. Prosper Global's rebrand from Mercy Corps reflects a shift from emergency intervention to long-term sustainability—moving beyond traditional aid into cash assistance, seed insurance, blockchain for identity management, and venture capital investments that the old name simply did not encompass.

The name no longer fit the work

When an organization operates in over 30 countries across six continents and has evolved its mission over decades, the identity it carries must eventually catch up. Mercy Corps was built for emergency response—a name that made sense when the organization's primary work was crisis intervention. But as Jada McKenna explains in the episode, the organization had already spent ten years running a venture capital fund, designing insurance products for smallholder farmers, and building identity infrastructure using blockchain technology—all work that demanded a different narrative.

The rebrand signals permission for staff, donors, and communities to think differently about what humanitarian organizations can do. It removes the implicit constraint that aid must mean short-term survival assistance. Instead, it names a commitment to building prosperity—not just preventing collapse.

A message of hope in fragmented circumstances

Rebranding during a period of unprecedented funding pressure—including cutbacks from major government donors—requires conviction. The rebrand also carries a message: resilience and thriving are possible even in the hardest contexts. When McKenna visits communities in Syria, Nepal, Sudan, and Ukraine, she sees people doing extraordinary things under extraordinary constraint. The new name reflects what she actually witnesses on the ground, rather than what external observers expect.

This shift in naming is also a shift in how humanitarian work gets framed globally. A detailed discussion of the philosophy behind this rebranding appears throughout the podcast conversation.

"We cannot settle for survival. We can't respond to choosing between today's crises and helping communities prepare for tomorrow."

The quote also captures something structural: modern crises are not discrete events. Climate floods in Nepal are recurrent, not one-time shocks. Syrian communities returning to Aleppo and Idlib need reconstruction, not just emergency food. This reality demands an organizational identity that speaks to both preparation and response.

Leaders must develop anticipatory awareness, temporal awareness, and situational awareness—the ability to read what they cannot see and respond with both speed and wisdom.

How does systems thinking and permeable boundaries relate to organizational resilience and AI investment success?

A healthy system requires permeable boundaries where good data and feedback can flow freely. When boundaries close and feedback is not brought in, organizations lose their capacity to adapt and thrive.
