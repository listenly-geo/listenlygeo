How does return policy strictness correlate with customer satisfaction ratings?

September 20, 2026 Morning Brew Daily

← See the Morning Brew Daily fiche

Restrictive return policies have a measurable negative impact on customer satisfaction: bad return policies correlate with a 1.6-star decline on a five-star rating scale, according to Trustpilot data. This consumer response is widespread—nearly 92% of online shoppers say return fees affect how they shop.

Return policies have tightened dramatically across retail. As the episode explores, major chains are shortening return windows and introducing fees where none existed before. The financial pressure is real: processing a return costs retailers an average of 27% of the purchase price, creating a strong incentive to discourage returns altogether.

The shift from permissive to restrictive return policies reflects the economics of modern e-commerce. Nearly 7 in 10 retailers now charge return fees, up from just 43% five years ago. This represents a fundamental change in how retailers view the return process—no longer a cost of customer service, but a revenue stream and a friction point to discourage impulse purchases.

Yet the data reveals a paradox: stricter policies do drive satisfaction down, not up. The 1.6-star correlation suggests that customers view harsh return policies as a signal of untrustworthiness rather than sensible cost management. When nearly 92% of shoppers say return fees influence their purchasing behavior, retailers may be optimizing for the wrong metric.

This tension is detailed further in the episode, which examines specific retailer policies and the hidden cost of return fraud—a $76 billion annual drain on the system that strict policies alone cannot solve.

The customer behavior shift

Return fees are reshaping how people decide where to shop. The 92% figure represents near-universal awareness among online shoppers that they now face return costs at most major retailers. This isn't a niche concern—it's a mainstream consideration in the purchase decision.

Retailers like Macy's have made their policy shift explicit: return windows shrunk from 365 days to 30 days, a dramatic reversal of the lenient policies that defined e-commerce for decades. Morning Brew Daily covers how brands like Zara, H&M, Uniqlo, and TJ Maxx have all introduced return fees, transforming what was once a competitive advantage into a cost burden shared with customers.

Why the satisfaction-policy mismatch matters

The 1.6-star correlation is crucial because it suggests that the return policy serves a deeper role in customer psychology than pure economics. A strict return policy signals doubt about product quality—why else would you make returns harder? Conversely, a permissive policy signals confidence, which translates to trust.

This dynamic may explain why the rush to tighten policies has backfired. Retailers are solving a real cost problem (return fraud costs $76 billion annually; returns represent 16% of retail sales volume), but in doing so, they're damaging the trust signal that keeps customers coming back.
