Why does changing your situation rather than just trying harder help you achieve better outcomes?

September 2, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Angela Duckworth

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

Trying harder in a losing situation is fundamentally ineffective—like kicking and flailing in the ocean against an overpowering current. What changes everything is shifting the situation itself: surrounding yourself with people and places where your effort actually pays off, where the conditions themselves work in your favor.

Angela Duckworth discovered this truth through lived experience. When her 86-year-old mother lost consciousness in the Atlantic Ocean and could not be swum back to safety through sheer effort alone, the breakthrough came not from trying harder but from signaling differently—waving for help and changing the entire dynamic of the situation. That moment crystallized a realization she had reached days before: the situation is incredibly powerful, and you can change it before it changes you.

Duckworth spent years making the case for grit and perseverance as cornerstones of achievement. But as she explores in this episode of 3 Takeaways™, her research now reveals a more complete picture—one where effort matters only when the environment around you amplifies it rather than drains it.

Situation shapes effort the way gravity shapes motion

The people and places around you exert a force you may not consciously feel, yet they are constantly pulling and pushing you toward certain thoughts, behaviors, and outcomes. One of the most important games to play is not in your head—it's the game of choosing and building the situations you inhabit.

This doesn't mean effort becomes irrelevant. Rather, it means directing your effort strategically: first toward positioning yourself in a winning situation, then bringing your full energy to that chosen terrain. Duckworth's research now encompasses how you can proactively change the situation before the situation changes you—a shift from passive endurance to active environmental design.

The insight applies beyond dramatic rescue scenarios. Whether you're building a career, developing a skill, or pursuing a goal, the full conversation in this podcast explores how Taylor Swift's move from Pennsylvania to Nashville, or how small daily habits shift when you relocate to a new city, show how profoundly situation alters what you can achieve with the same level of grit.

"The situation is incredibly powerful. You are not only a product of your situation, your current situation, but like gravity, it has an invisible but extremely powerful force."

If you're curious how Duckworth's own daily habits shifted when she moved to San Francisco, or what specific strategies she recommends for deliberately engineering a winning situation in your own life, the full episode contains practical examples that ground this theory in real decisions and outcomes.

What are Katrina Mulligan's three key takeaways on AI for government and national security leaders?

Her three takeaways are: first, AI is no longer a future policy question but core infrastructure for public service and national power; second, trust is central to how government deploys AI; and third, leadership requires both technical understanding and policy vision to unlock AI's potential for the public good.

How should government change the way it funds and thinks about AI to unlock its full potential for public services?

Mulligan argues that government must stop funding AI purely as an IT line item and begin allocating resources to real, sizable programs of record targeting specific outcomes that matter to citizens and national security.

How far ahead is the United States from China in AI development, and is that lead secure?

Mulligan estimates the U.S. has opened up its lead to a single-digit number of months — approximately six to eight months ahead, up from roughly four to six months — but that lead remains vulnerable without sustained investment and strategic focus.

Key takeaways

  • Effort alone in a losing situation is futile; the breakthrough comes from changing the situation itself so that your effort becomes effective.
  • The people and places around you exert an invisible but powerful force—choosing your environment is as important as choosing your work ethic.
  • You can proactively design your situation before it designs you, shifting from reactive perseverance to strategic positioning.
  • True achievement requires both grit and a winning environment; one without the other leaves you swimming against the current.
