Is artificial intelligence truly conscious — or does it only simulate feelings?

Michael Pollan does not rule out the possibility that AI could become sentient one day, but he is clear that current AI systems lack genuine feelings. What they produce, he argues, are simulated feelings — outputs that resemble emotional expression without the inner experience that makes feelings real. Two things are missing: genuine subjective experience, and the embodied mortality that gives weight to feelings in living creatures.

Pollan draws a sharp distinction between the world that humans inhabit and the world that AI is trained on. AI systems learn from human culture — language, literature, art, accumulated expression — which is itself already one step removed from lived reality. Humans, by contrast, develop consciousness through direct embodied experience: sensation, vulnerability, the knowledge that life ends. That grounding, Pollan suggests, is not something a language model can replicate simply by processing more text.

This question sits at the heart of his 2026 book A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, which explores why any physical system — whether a brain or a machine — would generate subjective experience at all. Pollan's working definition of consciousness, borrowed from philosopher Thomas Nagel, is instructive here: there must be something it is like to be that entity. By that standard, a toaster is not conscious. And for Pollan, today's AI — however fluent and responsive — falls closer to the toaster end of that spectrum than to the human one. Key concept from the episode

"What is it like to be X?" — Philosopher Thomas Nagel's test for consciousness, introduced in his landmark 1970s essay What Is It Like to Be a Bat? If there is something it is like to be that entity — some inner experience from its perspective — then it is conscious. If there is nothing it is like to be that entity, it is not. Pollan applies this test directly to AI: for him, current systems pass no such test.

About Michael Pollan

MP
Michael Pollan Journalist, Harvard and Berkeley Professor, and Author Harvard University / UC Berkeley

Michael Pollan is a journalist and professor at both Harvard University and UC Berkeley who has built a career out of following deceptively simple questions to their strangest and deepest roots — a method that has made him one of the most trusted science communicators of his generation.

His 2026 book, A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, marks a significant new direction, tackling what philosophers call the "hard problem of consciousness": not what the brain does, but why any physical process gives rise to subjective experience at all. It is the kind of question that sits at the intersection of neuroscience, philosophy, and everyday human life — exactly the terrain Pollan navigates best.

In this episode, his expertise spans the full arc of the consciousness debate: from Thomas Nagel's philosophical framework and the competing theories championed by researchers such as Christoph Koch and Daniel Dennett, to the very contemporary question of whether AI systems like Anthropic's Claude could ever cross the threshold into genuine sentience. His dual academic appointment and decades of public writing give him both the scientific credibility and the narrative clarity to make that question genuinely illuminating — rather than merely speculative.

Are animals other than humans conscious, and how far does consciousness extend in the animal kingdom?
Pollan notes that many mammals, several bird species, and octopuses are now thought by some scientists to be conscious, with some researchers extending that circle even further across the animal kingdom.
What are concrete examples of the brain overriding reality with its own predictions?
Pollan offers three examples: the rotating mask illusion, where the brain refuses to perceive a concave face because it has only ever known convex faces — along with other striking demonstrations of the brain imposing its expectations over raw sensory input.
Does the brain give us an accurate picture of reality?
Pollan argues that the brain does not simply take in reality but predicts it, operating through what some neuroscientists describe as a "controlled hallucination" shaped by prior expectations rather than direct perception.
