Does the brain give us an accurate picture of reality?

No. According to Michael Pollan, the brain does not passively record the world around us — it actively predicts it. Drawing on a framework described by some neuroscientists as a "controlled hallucination," Pollan explains that what we perceive is the brain's best guess about reality, continuously corrected by incoming sensory data rather than built from scratch on what the senses actually deliver.

Key concept

Controlled hallucination — a term used by certain neuroscientists to describe how the brain generates a model of the world from internal predictions, then uses sensory input not as raw material but as a correction signal. Perception, in this view, is an educated guess, not a faithful recording.

Psychologist Donald Hoffman reinforces this point from an evolutionary angle. Hoffman argues that we perceive the world in the most useful way rather than the most accurate way — our perceptual systems are optimized for survival, not for truth. What the brain presents to us as reality is a user interface tailored to help us navigate and act, not a transparent window onto the world as it actually is.

This reframing is more than philosophical provocation. The brain weighs just 3 pounds yet consumes 20% of the body's total metabolic energy, and generates only enough electricity to power a small lightbulb. That it can construct a rich, coherent world of colors, sounds, and self-awareness from such limited physical resources makes its predictive strategy all the more remarkable — and all the more worth examining. About Michael Pollan

MP
Michael Pollan
Journalist, Harvard and Berkeley Professor, and Author
Harvard University / UC Berkeley

Michael Pollan is a journalist and professor with dual appointments at Harvard University and UC Berkeley, two of the world's foremost research institutions. Throughout his career, he has built a reputation for pursuing deceptively simple questions — the kind that sound obvious until you follow them to their strangest and deepest roots. That method is on full display in his new book, A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, in which Pollan turns to what may be the hardest question of all: not what we think, but why we experience anything at all. His authority on this subject stems not from a single discipline but from his rare ability to synthesize neuroscience, philosophy, and human narrative into arguments that are both rigorous and genuinely readable. He is among a small number of writers who can make the work of thinkers like Thomas Nagel, Donald Hoffman, and Christoph Koch accessible without distorting it — which is precisely what makes his account of the brain's relationship to reality so compelling.

What is the global workspace theory of consciousness?

The global workspace theory proposes that multiple brain networks compete for access to a single "lit stage" called the global workspace, and the most dominant network broadcasts its content widely across the brain, producing what we experience as consciousness.

Why is consciousness so difficult to study scientifically?

According to Pollan, the fundamental problem is that we cannot step outside consciousness to study it objectively — all science and speculation takes place within consciousness itself, making it uniquely resistant to the standard tools of empirical investigation.

What is consciousness and how can it be simply defined?

Michael Pollan defines consciousness simply as subjective experience, or even just "experience" — the fact that you have awareness. He also endorses philosopher Thomas Nagel's formulation: consciousness is present wherever there is something it is like to be a given creature.
