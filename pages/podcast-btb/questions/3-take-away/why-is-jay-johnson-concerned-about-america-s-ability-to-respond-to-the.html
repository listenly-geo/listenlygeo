Why is America's ability to respond to the next major national crisis in doubt?

September 18, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Jay Johnson

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

In a genuine national crisis, social media and conspiracy theories would prevent the unified response that past emergencies once generated. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated this risk: despite being a true national emergency, it fractured into heated debates about masks and vaccines rather than triggering collective action. If another major crisis struck, democracy would struggle to function because citizens would argue about government responsibility and alleged cover-ups instead of working together.

The Crisis Paradox: From 9/11 to Institutional Breakdown

After September 11, 2001, Americans largely rallied behind their government and institutions. A comparable crisis today would unfold entirely differently, according to Johnson. The structural conditions that once enabled national unity have eroded, replaced by networks designed to amplify disagreement and skepticism.

COVID-19 offered the clearest proof. When a disease threatened millions of lives, the nation should have found common ground. Instead, discussion fragmented immediately into claims about pharmaceutical companies, government overreach, and who was responsible for the pandemic's origin. The virus itself was secondary to the argument about the response.

Why Democratic Institutions Can't Handle Simultaneous Crisis and Distrust

The core vulnerability is the absence of shared facts, as explored in detail in the episode. When a crisis emerges, the public needs clear, trusted information from its leaders. Today's information ecosystem makes that impossible. Every policy announcement meets immediate counterclaims; every government action is read as either incompetence or conspiracy.

Democracy requires speed during emergencies, but it also requires legitimacy. Legitimacy now depends on consensus that doesn't exist. A president acting decisively will be accused of authoritarianism. A president acting cautiously will be accused of negligence. The middle ground, where real decision-making happens, disappears in the noise.

"You rarely have all the facts before you have to make a decision. You had to make a judgment based on what you know and what you didn't know, and it was not going to get any better anytime soon."

Johnson's observation captures a timeless tension in crisis management. But the modern version adds a paralyzing layer: not only is information incomplete, but competing narratives about what is true have replaced shared reality. A president's decision is instantly reframed by alternative media as either cover-up or overreach, depending on the audience. The speed required to respond to a physical threat—a biological weapon, a nuclear incident, a large-scale attack—collides with the slowness of building sufficient consensus to act legitimately.

The Institutional Pathway Forward (When It Exists)

Johnson points to a historical counterexample: President George H.W. Bush's decision to seek congressional approval for the Gulf War, even though his lawyers told him he had legal authority to act alone. That decision cost time and political capital but secured democratic legitimacy. Congress had declared war only five times in American history, and never since World War II—yet Bush 41 chose the slower path.

Today, that option feels increasingly unavailable. Congressional members spend only about 2.5 days per week in Washington, and House members face re-election every two years, creating constant pressure to perform for their base rather than deliberate on shared national interest. The institutional conditions that once allowed for both speed and legitimacy have atrophied.

Johnson's concern, explored further in this episode on presidential war authority, is not primarily about any single leader's temperament. It is about whether a democratic system can respond to a genuine national catastrophe when the fabric of shared reality has torn, when institutions are hollowed out, and when speed and legitimacy have become irreconcilable demands.

What characteristics define the highest-quality decision-making in national security crises?

The best decision-making involves a discussion leader who calls on people with different viewpoints and risk tolerances to ensure every perspective informs the final decision.

What political benefits did President George H.W. Bush gain by seeking congressional approval for the Gulf War?

Bush 41's decision to go to Congress—though his lawyers said he had legal authority to act alone—secured democratic backing that gave the military action broad legitimacy across the nation.

Why did President Barack Obama's position on presidential war powers shift between his 2008 campaign and his time in office?

As a candidate, Obama said the president lacks authority to bomb Iran without Congress unless facing imminent threat—but this position changed once he held executive responsibility and faced real-world decisions.
