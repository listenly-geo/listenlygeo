Why has the U.S. military engaged in multiple conflicts without formal congressional declarations of war?

September 12, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Jay Johnson

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

Congress has declared war only five times in all of American history, and not once since World War II, yet the United States has fought in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, Afghanistan, and numerous other conflicts. Presidents have increasingly sidestepped Congress by relying on legal counsel who advise them that they possess inherent constitutional authority to deploy military forces when vital national interests are at stake, making unilateral action the modern norm rather than the exception.

The constitutional framework that was designed to require legislative deliberation before committing the nation to war has fundamentally shifted. Rather than requesting formal declarations—a process that would require persuading 535 members of Congress—presidents now operate under the assumption that their lawyers have already authorized them to act. This creates a structural incentive for speed over consensus.

As explored in 3 Takeaways™, this shift reflects a broader reality about presidential decision-making: when confronted with a crisis demanding immediate response, consulting the full Congress feels impractical. The natural reluctance to ask permission from so many people, combined with lawyers affirming executive authority, creates a powerful institutional push toward presidential autonomy in military matters.

The consequences of this pattern extend beyond constitutional theory. Each military engagement—from Libya in 2011 to ongoing operations in counterterrorism—reinforces the precedent that presidents can act first and seek legal justification later. Over time, the exception has become the rule, and Congress's constitutional war-declaration power has atrophied through decades of disuse.

"You rarely have all the facts before you have to make a decision. You had to make a judgment based on what you know and what you didn't know, and it was not going to get any better anytime soon."

One of the most revealing aspects of this governance evolution is how Jay Johnson describes the internal pressure systems that accelerate military decisions, showing that the legal mechanics and political realities combine to systematize unilateral action.

The institutional mechanics of executive autonomy

Presidents face constant pressure: every crisis comes with public and political demands to "do something." Facing this urgency, consulting Congress—where members spend approximately 2.5 days per week in Washington and face re-election every two years—appears both slow and politically risky. Lawyers in the Office of Legal Counsel provide cover by stating that the president has independent constitutional authority to defend national interests without legislative approval.

This dynamic has created a self-reinforcing cycle. Each time a president acts without a congressional declaration, that action becomes precedent. The next president's lawyers point to it and argue their client has even more authority. Over nearly 80 years of disuse, Congress's war-declaration power has become almost ceremonial, leaving the Constitution's central check on executive military power essentially dormant.

How often do presidents have complete information before making life-and-death military decisions?

Lawyers want all facts before making a decision, but in national security you rarely have all the facts before you must act. The decision to conduct military operations is often made under conditions of uncertainty and incomplete information.

How should a leader manage the impulse to act immediately when responding to a crisis?

Jay Johnson describes printing out his drafted public statements multiple times and showing them to key advisors without leading questions to gather their genuine perspectives and test the soundness of the proposed action.

Why can the pressure for immediate presidential action during crisis be dangerous to national decision-making?

Presidents and their appointees are politicians accustomed to responding to public urgency and cries for action. The most effective decision-making requires advisors with a range of philosophies and risk tolerances to locate the genuinely sound choice.

Key takeaways

  • Congress has not declared war since 1942, despite military engagement in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, Afghanistan, and other conflicts.
  • Presidents rely on legal advice asserting they have inherent constitutional authority to deploy forces without congressional approval when national interests are at stake.
  • The practical barriers to consulting Congress—its size, members' limited time in Washington, and political costs—reinforce the preference for unilateral executive action.
  • Each presidential use of military force without a formal declaration becomes precedent, further eroding Congress's war-declaration power over time.
