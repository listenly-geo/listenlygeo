Why can the pressure for immediate presidential action during crisis be dangerous to national decision-making?

September 9, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Jay Johnson

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

The most effective crisis decisions come from advisors with conflicting risk tolerances—some action-oriented, others cautious—not from a president alone responding to public demands for speed. Presidents and their appointees are politicians trained to react to urgency, but this reflex can silence the methodical voices that produce healthier government outcomes. When you have that diversity of perspective in the room together, brilliance often emerges somewhere in the middle.

Every major crisis follows a predictable script: the public demands action, media amplifies the urgency, and political pressure mounts on the Oval Office. The implicit message is always the same—do something, do it now. Yet this cultural demand for speed can be as dangerous to the nation as waiting too long. The Constitution itself was designed to slow down decisions about war, yet today's political environment rewards velocity over deliberation.

The risk lies in how presidents naturally think. They and their advisors are seasoned politicians who have built careers on responding to public cries for action. This instinct is not a flaw—it reflects decades of conditioning to lead decisively and show the country they are in control. But in national security matters, this reflex can become a trap. When the pressure to act overwhelms the room, the cautious voices—the advisors willing to question, probe, and suggest delay—often get drowned out or exclude themselves entirely to avoid appearing weak.

As discussed in this episode of 3 Takeaways™, the solution is deliberate structural design. A president must consciously build a cabinet and inner circle that represents a genuine spectrum of risk tolerances—not political ideology, but fundamentally different approaches to uncertainty. Some people are action-oriented and proactive; others are more cautious and methodical. You need all of them in the room at the same time, engaging with each other in real time.

This diversity of perspective forces presidents to confront the trade-offs inherent in any crisis decision. A purely action-oriented advisor might push for immediate military intervention. A cautious advisor might highlight unintended consequences, civilian casualties, or strategic costs that no one has yet considered. Neither voice alone is sufficient. But when both are present and heard, the decision that emerges tends to be more sound, more carefully vetted, and less likely to reflect the panic of the moment. The "brilliance in the middle" is not a compromise—it is better strategy.

"You rarely have all the facts before you have to make a decision. You had to make a judgment based on what you know and what you didn't know, and it was not going to get any better anytime soon."

The practical challenge is that this design requires political will and self-awareness. A president must actively select advisors who will push back, who represent genuinely different worldviews, and who are confident enough to speak up even when the room is hungry for action. It is not enough to have a diverse cabinet on paper. These advisors must be in the room together, not isolated in silos, so that their competing perspectives can create the friction that refines decisions.

The Constitution Was Built for Slowness, Not Speed

The framers of the U.S. Constitution were acutely aware of the dangers of unchecked executive power in moments of fear. Congress has declared war only five times in all of American history, and not once since World War II, yet presidents have committed the military to dozens of significant conflicts in that same period. The gap between constitutional design and modern practice reveals how thoroughly the pressure for speed has eroded the structures meant to ensure deliberation.

Today's communication technology and news cycle reinforce this acceleration. A crisis unfolds in real time on cable news; the public wants answers within hours. But the Constitution assumes that major decisions about national survival should take weeks or months, with Congress convened, lawyers consulted, and military leaders questioned. That friction was intentional, not a bug. It was meant to prevent impulsive decisions born of panic.

Yet presidents rarely have the luxury of constitutional timelines anymore. The answer is not to restore the old speed of deliberation—modern threats move too fast for that. Rather, it is to ensure that within whatever time is available, the decision-making process itself contains that constitutional impulse: multiple voices, competing interests, and a built-in resistance to groupthink.
