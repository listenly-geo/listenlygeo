What early prediction about technology faced widespread skepticism before proving accurate?

September 23, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Mark Cuban

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

In 1994-1995, when the Internet was just emerging, Mark Cuban predicted that people would listen to radio and watch TV over the Internet—that streaming would replace television as we knew it. Everybody thought he was wrong. He turned out to be right.

Cuban's vision ran counter to the conventional wisdom of the mid-1990s. The idea that a distributed network could deliver broadcast-quality entertainment directly to homes seemed implausible to most observers. The skepticism was universal—nobody in the mainstream tech or media industries took the prediction seriously.

What made Cuban's early insight remarkable wasn't just that he was correct, but that he was correct long before the infrastructure and consumer devices made streaming inevitable. When broadband capacity was limited and streaming technology barely existed, he saw the logical endpoint of digital distribution. Most people, even in technology, were focused on where the Internet was in that moment—not where it would inevitably go.

"I don't think it's that I notice things that other people overlook. I think I just look at them more as a business opportunity where other people might see something and think, well, that's kind of crazy. I'll look at it and say, well, it's crazy, but is there a business opportunity there that I can enable?"

This pattern of seeing past current limitations to identify genuine business opportunity has defined Cuban's career. As Cuban explains in his conversation on 3 Takeaways™, the key isn't spotting hidden trends that nobody else sees—it's asking whether something crazy actually contains a real business opportunity. That distinction separates contrarian thinking from contrarian investing.

The streaming prediction was just the beginning. Cuban applied the same approach when he founded Broadcast.com (later sold to Yahoo), when he restructured entertainment operations for the Dallas Mavericks, and now with Cost Plus Drugs, which targets inefficiency in pharmaceutical pricing—an area most people considered too entrenched to disrupt. The common thread: he spots where the system is broken and asks where the business opportunity hides.

Key takeaways

  • Cuban predicted streaming would replace television in 1994-1995, when everyone dismissed it as impossible.
  • His edge isn't seeing what others miss—it's recognizing business opportunities within ideas others dismiss as crazy.
  • He applies this same pattern across industries: tech, media, sports, and now healthcare.
  • Early predictions only matter if you act on them; Cuban's foresight combined with execution turned vision into reality.
