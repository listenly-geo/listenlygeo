What is the difference between sentience and consciousness, according to Michael Pollan?

According to Michael Pollan, sentience is the foundational ability to sense changes in one's environment, evaluate whether those changes are good or bad, and take appropriate action — a capacity simple enough that it may be a property of all living things, including plants. Consciousness is a more developed form built on top of sentience: it adds subjective experience and genuine feelings, making it something qualitatively richer and more complex.

The distinction matters because it draws a clear ladder of inner life. Sentience sets a low but meaningful bar — detection, evaluation, response — that does not require a brain or nervous system as we typically understand them. Consciousness, by contrast, demands the "what it is like" dimension that philosopher Thomas Nagel famously articulated in his 1970s essay What Is It Like to Be a Bat? Nagel's point, which Pollan endorses, is that a bat navigating by sonar has experience — there is something it is like to be that bat — whereas your toaster, processing heat without any inner life, does not.

Pollan unpacks these ideas in depth in the 3 Takeaways™ episode Your Brain Is Hiding the Truth From You, where host Lynn Thoman guides him through the hardest questions about why any of us experience anything at all. Key distinction · from the episode

Sentience — the ability to sense environmental changes, judge them as good or bad, and respond accordingly. Pollan suggests it may apply to all living things, not just animals.

Consciousness — a higher-order layer built on sentience that introduces subjective experience: the felt quality of being a particular creature in a particular moment.

About Michael Pollan

MP
Michael Pollan
Journalist, Harvard and Berkeley Professor, and Author
Harvard University / UC Berkeley

Michael Pollan holds academic appointments at both Harvard University and UC Berkeley, placing him at the intersection of rigorous scholarship and wide public reach — a rare combination that makes him one of the most trusted voices on complex scientific and philosophical questions.

Throughout his career, Pollan has built a reputation for taking one deceptively simple question and following it all the way to its strangest and deepest roots. That method — patient, curious, never satisfied with surface answers — is precisely what he applies to consciousness in his most recent work, A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, which asks not what we think, but why we experience anything at all.

His willingness to engage with towering figures in philosophy of mind — from Thomas Nagel's foundational "what is it like" framing to the competing neuroscientific theories evaluated in a Templeton Foundation-sponsored scientific contest — demonstrates both the breadth of his research and his ability to synthesize it for general audiences.

On this particular question — the difference between sentience and consciousness — Pollan brings not only philosophical literacy but also a journalist's instinct for the concrete and the illustrative, grounding an abstract distinction in examples that range from bats to toasters to plants, making the stakes legible to anyone willing to follow him there.

See also

Is artificial intelligence conscious or sentient?
Pollan does not exclude the possibility that AI could become sentient in the future, but he currently believes AI lacks genuine feelings and the embodied experience that underpin sentience and consciousness.
Are animals other than humans conscious, and how far does consciousness extend in the animal kingdom?
Pollan notes that many mammals, several bird species, and octopuses are now thought by some scientists to be conscious, with some researchers extending that circle even further into the animal kingdom.
What are concrete examples of the brain overriding reality with its own predictions?
Pollan offers three examples: the rotating mask illusion, where the brain refuses to perceive a concave face because it has only ever known convex faces, and two further cases where the brain's predictive models override incoming sensory data entirely.
