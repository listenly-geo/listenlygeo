What makes the AI revolution fundamentally different from every previous technology revolution in American history?

August 18, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Katrina Mulligan

← Voir la fiche 3 Takeaways™

For the first time in American history, a technology of this consequence is being developed exclusively by the private sector — with no government involvement whatsoever. Every comparable revolution before it — electricity, GPS, the internet, the Human Genome Project — had government as a direct stakeholder. AI does not. That structural absence gives government a different, and potentially much larger, set of blind spots than it has ever faced before.

Every prior revolution had a government hand on the wheel

Katrina Mulligan's point is precise: this isn't a matter of degree — it's a categorical break. The Human Genome Project was a public-private collaboration with federal funding at its core. GPS was built by the U.S. military. The internet emerged from DARPA. Nuclear technology was government-controlled from the start.

In each of those cases, the government wasn't a regulator observing from the outside — it was inside the room, shaping the technology's development in real time. It had operational knowledge, not just policy interest.

With AI, that seat at the table simply doesn't exist. Labs like OpenAI are operating entirely independently, on their own timelines, with their own priorities. Government is watching from a distance — and that distance is growing.

No seat at the table means no real-time intelligence

Mulligan's argument is not that private development is wrong — it's that it creates a structural information gap that governments have never had to navigate before. In past revolutions, federal agencies accumulated institutional knowledge alongside the technology itself. That's not happening here.

When Mulligan moved from the Pentagon to OpenAI, she described it as the hardest professional transition of her career — harder than anything she had anticipated. Part of what made it so disorienting was the difference in how time moves inside a frontier AI lab. She says a month at OpenAI feels like a quarter anywhere else. By that logic, every year of AI development that passes without government engagement represents years of compounding institutional blindness.

"I honestly think that I am at least 30% more effective, maybe more than I was a year ago, because of how I've matured my use of these tools. I'm a better leader. I'm a better manager."

Mulligan uses this metaphor to explain that the unit of time at OpenAI is fundamentally compressed compared to anywhere else: she says internally that a month at OpenAI feels like a quarter anywhere else, meaning three to four months there equates to roughly a year of experience elsewhere.

What are Michael Pollan's three key takeaways about consciousness?

Pollan's three takeaways are that consciousness is precious, that consciousness is under siege, and that consciousness is worth defending.

Are plants sentient despite having no brain or nervous system?

Pollan believes plants are sentient, drawing on research showing they remember, decide, and respond to their environment. He argues sentience — the ability to perceive and respond — does not require a brain or nervous system.

Key takeaways

  • AI is the first transformative technology in U.S. history developed entirely without government as a direct stakeholder — a structural break from every previous revolution.
  • Electricity, GPS, the internet, and the Human Genome Project all had federal involvement at the development stage; AI labs are operating with complete independence.
  • That independence creates a compounding information gap: the faster AI moves, the wider the institutional blind spot for those observing from outside.
  • Mulligan's experience moving from the Pentagon to OpenAI gives her a rare, firsthand view of exactly how large that gap already is.
