Can plants really be sentient if they have no brain or nervous system?

Yes — Michael Pollan believes plants are sentient. Drawing on a growing body of research, he argues that plants remember, make decisions, and respond meaningfully to their environment, all without a brain or nervous system. For Pollan, sentience — the capacity to sense environmental changes, evaluate them as good or bad, and act accordingly — may be a fundamental property of all living things, not an exclusive feature of animal cognition.

The intuitive objection is obvious: sentience sounds like it requires a nervous system. But Pollan pushes back on that assumption. He points out that relying purely on hardwired instinct to navigate a constantly changing world seems far-fetched. A living organism that can only react in fixed, pre-programmed ways would be poorly equipped for survival. The ability to sense, evaluate, and adapt — in other words, some form of sentience — appears to be a more plausible explanation for how plants thrive across wildly different and shifting conditions.

Key concept from the episode

Sentience, as Pollan uses the term, is not the same as consciousness or self-awareness. It refers specifically to the ability to sense changes in one's environment, evaluate whether those changes are beneficial or harmful, and take appropriate action in response. By this definition, a brain is not a prerequisite — it is one mechanism among potentially many that living organisms have evolved to do this work.

This framing connects to Pollan's broader exploration of consciousness in his book A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness. The question of what kinds of entities can have experience — or something like it — sits at the heart of that project. Pollan discusses these ideas in depth on the 3 Takeaways™ podcast, hosted by Lynn Thoman, in an episode that ranges from the neuroscience of the human brain to the philosophical edges of where sentience begins and ends.

About Michael Pollan

MP
Michael Pollan
Journalist, Harvard and Berkeley Professor, and Author
Harvard University / UC Berkeley

Michael Pollan is a journalist and professor at both Harvard University and UC Berkeley who has built his career by following deceptively simple questions — about food, nature, plants, and the mind — to their strangest and deepest roots. His work is defined by a rare ability to make complex scientific and philosophical territory genuinely accessible to general audiences, without sacrificing rigor or nuance. His newest book, A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, takes on perhaps the largest question of all: not what we think, but why we experience anything at all. It is from within this investigation that his reflections on plant sentience emerge — as part of a serious inquiry into where the boundaries of experience, sensation, and awareness actually lie. Pollan's authority on this subject comes not from a single discipline but from years of sustained, intellectually curious engagement with science, philosophy, and the natural world, making him one of the most credible public voices on the question of consciousness in all its forms.

What is the difference between sentience and consciousness according to Michael Pollan?

Pollan defines sentience as the ability to sense changes in one's environment, evaluate whether they are good or bad, and take appropriate action — a capacity he believes may extend far beyond humans and animals.

Is artificial intelligence conscious or sentient?

Pollan does not exclude the possibility that AI could become sentient in the future, but he currently believes AI lacks genuine feelings and the embodied experience that grounds consciousness in living things.

Are animals other than humans conscious, and how far does consciousness extend in the animal kingdom?

Pollan notes that many mammals, several bird species, and octopuses are now thought by some scientists to be conscious, with some extending that circle even further into the animal kingdom.
