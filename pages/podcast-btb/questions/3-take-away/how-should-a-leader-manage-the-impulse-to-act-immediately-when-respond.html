How should a leader manage the impulse to act immediately when responding to a crisis?

September 10, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Jay Johnson

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

The best crisis decision-makers resist the pressure to act immediately by deliberately consulting advisors with opposing viewpoints—not just for validation, but to find wisdom in the middle ground. Drawing out the decision-making process, even by a few hours, often reveals that immediate action was unnecessary and could have caused more harm.

The Practice of Deliberate Consultation

Jay Johnson, who advised presidents as Secretary of Homeland Security and General Counsel of the Department of Defense, developed a concrete method to resist the crisis impulse. He would draft public statements five times and show them to five different key advisors, each without leading questions, to gather their authentic perspectives before sending anything.

This practice has a direct impact: advisors representing different philosophies—some aggressive and action-oriented, others cautious and methodical—often pushed back against rushing. As Johnson discovered through his agency work leading 250,000 people, the weight of diverse counsel frequently advises restraint, revealing the political and tactical costs of immediate action that the initial pressure obscures.

What Emerges in the Middle

The real breakthrough isn't that advisors always reach unanimous agreement. Sometimes Johnson found consensus; sometimes he simply blew off steam by 11 a.m. and moved on to something else entirely. The value lies in the deliberate pause itself—giving the emotional urgency time to settle before the decision hardens into irreversible action.

As discussed at length in this episode of 3 Takeaways™, the same dynamic exists at the highest levels of national security decision-making, where a president's first instinct to "do something" can be the most dangerous advice in the room. Building a practice of structured consultation—not endless debate, but authentic input from people with different risk tolerances—is what separates reactive crisis management from sound judgment.

"You rarely have all the facts before you have to make a decision. You had to make a judgment based on what you know and what you didn't know, and it was not going to get any better anytime soon."

Key takeaways

  • Deliberately consult advisors with opposite viewpoints and risk tolerances before acting, not just for agreement but for genuine perspective.
  • The emotional pressure of a crisis fades within hours; a structured pause often reveals that immediate action was unnecessary.
  • The most effective leaders build a practice of authentic consultation into their decision-making, not as delay but as a guard against costly mistakes.
