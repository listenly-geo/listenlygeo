How do entrepreneurs spot business opportunities where others see only obstacles?

September 20, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Mark Cuban

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

The difference isn't spotting what others miss—it's reframing how you perceive it. Look at ideas other people dismiss as crazy, then ask whether a viable business opportunity actually exists within that "craziness." This mental shift transforms perceived obstacles into actionable ventures by decoupling feasibility from initial public skepticism.

Mark Cuban doesn't claim to see invisible things. Instead, he shifts the mental frame: where most people see risk, impossibility, or sheer lunacy, he asks a practical question: "Is there a business opportunity that I can enable here?" This isn't optimism—it's methodical reframing.

His track record proves this approach works across fundamentally different industries. From founding Broadcast.com (the first streaming company in the 1990s, when video on the internet seemed absurd) to transforming the Dallas Mavericks through entertainment innovation, to launching Cost Plus Drugs in 2022, Cuban identified viable business models in spaces others had written off as either too risky or simply too different.

The mechanism isn't luck. As Cuban explains in the episode, the critical step is treating dismissal as a starting point, not an ending. When people say something is crazy, that's the moment to investigate whether the skepticism is rational or simply reflexive.

Consuming Every Angle Before Deciding

Reframing obstacles into opportunities requires deep knowledge, not surface-level familiarity. Cuban's learning approach is almost obsessive: "RTFM—read the freaking manual. I literally try to consume every single thing I can."

Most entrepreneurs spend casual time learning a new field—a few articles, maybe a book, some online browsing. Cuban goes deeper. He explores every angle, every rabbit hole, every perspective, including the antithesis of what he's trying to achieve. He asks: "What would someone who thinks this is crazy say, and why would they say it?"

This exhaustive approach serves a practical purpose: by understanding all the arguments—for and against—he can anticipate how to navigate opposition and how to structure a business that addresses legitimate concerns, not just skeptical instincts. As discussed at length in this podcast episode, this due diligence separates opportunistic gambles from calculated ventures.

"I try to find things that other people think are crazy, but I think there's a business opportunity there."

What makes this approach resilient is that Cuban doesn't assume his first judgment is correct. He tests it obsessively against every objection, every data point, every counterargument. When you've genuinely considered why the skeptics are right—and you still see the opportunity—that's when you have conviction worth acting on.

The healthcare opportunity with Cost Plus Drugs exemplifies this exactly. The industry consensus was that drug pricing is hopelessly entangled in regulation and that no business could fix it profitably. Cuban didn't dismiss that skepticism; he studied it, understood the real constraints, and found a business model that works within (and against) those constraints.
