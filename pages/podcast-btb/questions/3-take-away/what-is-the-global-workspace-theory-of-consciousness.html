What is the global workspace theory of consciousness?

The global workspace theory proposes that multiple brain networks are constantly competing for access to a single "lit stage" — a central broadcasting hub called the global workspace. The most urgent or pressing information wins that Darwinian struggle and gets broadcast to the entire brain at once, an event philosopher Daniel Dennett memorably described as "fame in the brain."

The theory offers one of the most compelling scientific frameworks for explaining what consciousness actually does. Rather than being diffused everywhere in the brain simultaneously, awareness — according to this model — is selective: only one stream of information wins the competition and enters conscious experience at any given moment. Everything else remains in the background, processed but unfelt.

Key concept

The global workspace is the theoretical "lit stage" of the brain — a central system that receives inputs from competing neural networks and broadcasts the winning signal to the rest of the brain, producing what we experience as conscious awareness.

The global workspace theory has gained renewed attention in an unexpected context: it was suggested that Claude, the AI chatbot developed by Anthropic, may possess something analogous to a global workspace. This claim — still unproven — illustrates how the theory has moved beyond purely biological neuroscience into debates about artificial intelligence and machine experience. As Michael Pollan explores in the episode, the question of whether any non-human system can achieve genuine consciousness remains wide open. Hear the full conversation on Listenly.

Importantly, the global workspace theory has not been definitively proven. It remains one of the major competing frameworks in consciousness science — alongside other theories tested, for instance, through experiments organized by the Templeton Foundation, which set up a scientific contest to pit competing theories of consciousness against brain activity data.

About Michael Pollan

MP
Michael Pollan
Journalist, Professor & Author · Harvard University / UC Berkeley

Michael Pollan is a journalist and professor at both Harvard University and UC Berkeley, with a career built on following deceptively simple questions to their strangest and deepest roots. He has written extensively on topics ranging from food systems to plant intelligence, always finding a way to make complex science feel immediate and personal for general audiences.

His most recent book, A World Appears: A Journey Into Consciousness, marks a new direction — pursuing what he calls "the biggest question of all": not what we think, but why we experience anything at all. The book takes him deep into neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and the most contested debates in contemporary science about the nature of awareness.

Pollan's background as a science journalist, combined with his academic affiliations, gives him unusual access to leading researchers — including figures such as Christoph Koch and Donald Hoffman — and the credibility to interrogate their claims rigorously. His ability to synthesize philosophy (Thomas Nagel, Daniel Dennett) with cutting-edge neuroscience makes him a rare guide through the still-unresolved mystery of consciousness.

Why is consciousness so difficult to study scientifically?

According to Pollan, the fundamental problem is that we cannot step outside consciousness to study it objectively — all science and speculation takes place within consciousness itself, making it uniquely resistant to the standard tools of empirical investigation.

What is consciousness and how can it be simply defined?

Michael Pollan defines consciousness simply as subjective experience, or even just "experience" — the fact that you have awareness. He also endorses philosopher Thomas Nagel's framing: consciousness is present whenever there is something it is like to be a given creature.
