How do presidents make the best decisions in national security crises?

September 17, 2026 3 Takeaways™ · Jay Johnson

← See the 3 Takeaways™ fiche

The best decision-making in national security crises depends on three structural elements: a skilled discussion leader who orchestrates the process, a president who listens more than he talks to hear all viewpoints, and deliberate consultation with staff experts along the wall who often understand the issue better than cabinet principals. This ensures decisions are grounded in fuller, more diverse perspective rather than shaped by seniority alone.

The architecture of clarity in the Situation Room

The process begins with intentional leadership. The discussion leader—typically the national security advisor—must actively call on people around the table and ensure every voice is heard, not just the loudest. This role is critical because without deliberate structure, crises reward speed and seniority over substance. Presidents accustomed to responding to public pressure for action need advisors who slow down the process and demand rigor.

Equally important is what the president does not do. The president listens more than he talks. This isn't passivity; it's disciplined restraint. By asking questions instead of announcing positions, the president creates psychological permission for dissent and reveals assumptions that otherwise remain hidden. As Jay Johnson describes in the episode, this listening-first approach has defined the highest-quality deliberations he has witnessed in the nation's most sensitive moments.

The wisdom of the people along the wall

After the cabinet principals have spoken, the president takes an unconventional step: he asks the staff members sitting along the wall—the junior officials, analysts, and subject-matter experts who knew the issue better than the principals—for their views. These "plus ones" often bring clarity that cabinet rank obscures. They have lived with the details. They have read the intelligence. They see risks that protocol keeps hidden from senior officials.

This practice reflects a deeper truth about organizations under pressure. The best judgment rarely lives at the top of the hierarchy; it lives in the people closest to the problem, the ones who have no incentive to present a polished consensus and every incentive to tell the truth.

"You rarely have all the facts before you have to make a decision. You had to make a judgment based on what you know and what you didn't know, and it was not going to get any better anytime soon."

That insight captures the core tension: the impulse to wait for complete certainty is itself a decision with consequences. The Situation Room dynamic Johnson describes acknowledges this. It does not promise perfect clarity; it promises that when a president must decide with incomplete information, he will at least have heard from the people who understand the gaps most honestly.

For organizations beyond government, the lesson is transferable. The full episode explores how deliberate advisor diversity—not just political diversity, but differences in risk tolerance, experience, and perspective—becomes the antidote to the pressure to act too quickly or too narrowly.

Key takeaways

  • A strong discussion leader actively calls on advisors and structures the conversation to prevent dominant voices from silencing dissent.
  • Presidential listening—asking questions rather than announcing positions—creates psychological safety for subordinates to speak honestly.
  • Staff experts along the wall often understand complex issues better than cabinet principals and must be explicitly invited to share their views.
  • Complete certainty rarely arrives in time; the goal is to make the best judgment possible with the information available and the fullest range of honest perspective.

What political benefits did President George H.W. Bush gain by seeking congressional approval for the Gulf War despite having legal authority to act unilaterally?

Bush 41's lawyers said he had legal authority to act, but Congress insisted he come to them. He made the decision, largely political, to go to Congress and seek approval.

Why did President Barack Obama's position on presidential war powers shift between his 2008 campaign and his time in office?

As a candidate in 2007-2008, Barack Obama said the president does not have authority to bomb Iran without congressional authorization unless there is an imminent threat.

What legal justification do executive branch lawyers use to authorize unilateral presidential military action?

Lawyers in the executive branch tell the president that military deployment is authorized anytime there are important national interests at stake.
