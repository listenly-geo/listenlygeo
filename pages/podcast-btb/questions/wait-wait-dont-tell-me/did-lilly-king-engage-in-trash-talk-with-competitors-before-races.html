Did Lilly King engage in trash talk with competitors before races?

August 24, 2026 Wait Wait... Don't Tell Me! · Jason Benetti

← See the Wait Wait... Don't Tell Me! podcast page

Yes. Lilly King confirmed she talked trash to her opponents before races — and her competitors did not appreciate it. In the water, where words aren't an option, she found another way: deliberately refusing to make eye contact with a rival off a turn, which she described as about as close to trash talk as you can get mid-race.

Pre-race words that landed — and a silent weapon in the pool

King was open about the fact that psychological pressure wasn't something she left at the starting block. Before races, she went after opponents verbally, and the discomfort it created was, by her own account, entirely intentional. Her rivals were not fans of the approach — which was presumably part of the point.

The dynamic she described is a familiar one in elite sport: mental dominance begins before the gun fires. The pre-race zone is not neutral territory. King used it as an extension of competition itself, something Wait Wait... Don't Tell Me! explored in its summer 2026 highlights episode alongside other stories of high-stakes performance under pressure.

No eye contact off a turn — King's in-water version of the mind game

Once in the water, trash talk becomes physically impossible. But King found a substitute. In one Olympic final, she refused to make eye contact with another swimmer coming off a turn — a deliberate, pointed snub that she herself described as the aquatic equivalent of a verbal jab.

It's a small gesture with a clear message: I am not acknowledging you as a threat. That kind of psychological edge is especially significant in elite swimming, where the margin between competitors can be fractions of a second. How does Lilly King describe the mental pressure of competing at Olympic trials in swimming?

King described Olympic trials as uniquely high-pressure because the entire point of the meet is to finish in the top two in your event in order to make the Olympic team — out of 100 to 150 swimmers competing for just two spots.
