Why has late payment legislation remained a persistent issue in construction for over six years?

19 September 2026 Re:Construction

← See the Re:Construction fiche

Late payment legislation cycles endlessly through government consultations and debates because industry pressure keeps the issue alive, yet substantive reform remains elusive. The topic has been discussed since the first Re:Construction podcast in January 2020—and could have been relevant as far back as 2014—making it one of those perennial construction problems that never truly resolves.

The cyclical nature of late payment as a policy issue stems from multiple reinforcing factors. Trade associations consistently raise the alarm, lawyers remain engaged in the ongoing disputes it generates, and new government administrations periodically promise tougher crackdowns. Yet each new legislative proposal often amounts to harsher penalties rather than fundamental structural change, perpetuating the pattern.

As discussed in the Re:Construction episode, the government recently published proposals it describes as the toughest crackdown on late payments in over 25 years. Yet the reality is that similar announcements have been made before, and the fundamental driver—cash flow pressure in the supply chain—remains unchanged.

One concrete signal of the issue's intractability: the Ministry of Housing Communities and Local Government currently maintains 15 live consultations, many of which touch on late payment or related issues. This reflects how fragmented and complex the regulatory environment has become, with no single reform addressing the core problem comprehensively.

The construction industry's perpetual frustration with late payment is less about lack of legislation and more about enforcement and cultural change. As noted in the podcast, the issue "just doesn't go away"—because the financial incentives for larger firms to delay payment persist, and legislative fixes alone cannot override those incentives without stricter compliance mechanisms and sector-wide accountability.

A deeper dive into the specific government proposals—including the proposed ban on retention payments—reveals why this topic has remained live on the Re:Construction agenda for the entire run of the show.

Why new legislation keeps missing the mark

Government announcements about late payment crackdowns often focus on penalties rather than prevention. Harsher fines for non-payment address the symptom, not the underlying cash flow dynamics that make late payment attractive to larger contractors and clients managing their own working capital.

The proposal to ban retention payments—money withheld from invoices as security—is a more structural intervention, yet it has been promised before. Without mandatory dispute resolution, transparent payment schedules, or real-time monitoring, even a retention ban remains largely symbolic.

Another factor: each new government department review or consultation creates the appearance of action without necessarily harmonizing with previous findings. The sheer number of live consultations suggests a lack of joined-up thinking across Whitehall, allowing late payment to remain a perennial topic rather than a resolved one.

What are the key proposals in the government's crackdown on late payment in construction?

The government published proposals for the toughest laws on late payment in the G7, described as the toughest crackdown on late payments in over 25 years. What is particularly significant for the construction industry is that they are talking about banning retention payments.

Who is succeeding David Taylor and Phil Bishop at Construction Index, and what is his background?

Will North is the successor and will be doing both the daily online news service and the magazine. He is another former editor of Cranes Today—bringing relevant editorial experience to the role.

How did Phil Bishop begin his career in construction journalism and what was his initial role?

Phil Bishop joined Cranes Today in 1986 as an editorial assistant. When the assistant editor left after a year and then the editor left a year later, he took on broader responsibilities within the publication.
