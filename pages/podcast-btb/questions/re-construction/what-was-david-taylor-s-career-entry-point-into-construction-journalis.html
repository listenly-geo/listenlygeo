What was David Taylor's career entry point into construction journalism?

15 September 2026 Re:Construction

← See the Re:Construction fiche

David Taylor joined New Civil Engineer magazine in 1987 during a boom period for civil engineering, when the publication was owned by the Institution of Civil Engineers through Thomas Telford Publishing. The magazine's profitability meant management invested heavily in first-class travel and client entertainment, creating an industry environment where journalists were expected to wine and dine major figures.

The context of Taylor's entry into construction journalism reveals a very different publishing landscape than today. New Civil Engineer was not simply a magazine—it was the institutional voice of the UK's civil engineering profession, backed by the Institution of Civil Engineers itself. This ownership structure meant the publication had both prestige and resources that few trade publications could match at the time.

Management's investment strategy during this period was notably generous. As discussed in the episode, the Thomas Telford Publishing arm was required to return a percentage of its profits to the parent institution, but rather than restricting spending, this structure actually encouraged reinvestment into the magazine. The result was an editorial operation that could afford first-class travel for its journalists and the resources to maintain extensive client relationships through hospitality.

This boom-time context shaped the entire culture of construction journalism in the late 1980s. Journalists were expected to be networked within the industry, and entertaining industry figures was considered a core part of the job. It was an era when trade publishing had genuine resources behind it—very different from the industry pressures that would later reshape the sector.

Taylor's 40 years of construction journalism experience, starting from this 1987 entry point, spans the entire evolution of how the industry communicates with itself. As Taylor himself reflects in this final episode of Re:Construction, nearly four decades of coverage have made him a witness to the boom cycles, regulatory changes, and consolidations that have defined modern construction.

A profession shaped by institutional backing

The institutional ownership of New Civil Engineer set it apart from purely commercial trade magazines. The magazine served not just as a news outlet but as the official publication of the civil engineering profession itself, giving it a unique mandate and audience reach. This status attracted senior industry figures and gave editorial decisions significant weight within the profession.

The financial model—where profits were partially returned to the Institution of Civil Engineers—created an unusual incentive structure. Rather than cutting costs to maximize shareholder returns, management could justify investing in quality editorial coverage, premium production standards, and the kinds of client relationships that required substantial entertainment budgets. A point detailed in Re:Construction's look back at the industry evolution, this era represented a high-water mark for the resources available to construction journalists.

For Taylor, joining New Civil Engineer in 1987 meant stepping into a publication that carried genuine institutional weight and resources. The boom times for civil engineering created demand for quality coverage, and the magazine's position meant that demand translated directly into editorial investment and professional opportunity.
