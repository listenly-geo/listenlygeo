What guardrails and guidelines should govern data center development to balance economic growth with environmental and community concerns?

September 5, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Wes Moore

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

Data center development requires three core guardrails: self-funded energy infrastructure, local workforce hiring, and strict environmental standards. Governor Wes Moore emphasizes that jurisdictions cannot be forced to accept data centers, and that while AI and advanced infrastructure drive innovation in healthcare and public safety, development must proceed through clear state-level governance, not by unchecked expansion.

Three non-negotiable requirements for responsible data center growth

Maryland's approach to data center governance rests on a fundamental principle: the private sector bears the full cost of development. Data center operators must bring their own energy supply and fund any necessary infrastructure improvements, rather than offloading these expenses onto taxpayers. This ensures that economic growth in the sector does not create hidden public debt.

Local economic benefit is the second pillar. Data centers must hire workers from surrounding communities and engage directly with local leaders to build genuine support. As Moore emphasizes in this episode, this prevents corporate infrastructure from becoming isolated islands of wealth while neighboring areas remain economically disconnected.

Environmental accountability is non-negotiable. Data centers must meet high environmental standards set by the state, addressing water use, energy efficiency, waste management, and emissions. These standards protect both natural resources and public health—concerns that often drive community opposition to such projects.

Community consent as a prerequisite, not an afterthought

A critical distinction separates Moore's framework from purely pro-business models: data centers cannot be forced on unwilling jurisdictions. Regions must actively opt in, giving local governments veto power over which projects proceed. This respects the principle that growth cannot come at the expense of communities that bear environmental or social costs.

The governor recognizes that AI and data infrastructure drive transformative benefits—improved healthcare diagnostics, enhanced public safety systems, and better housing policy solutions. However, as explored in depth in the full conversation, these benefits must be pursued through structured guardrails, not by an "all means necessary" approach that sacrifices environmental integrity or community agency.

"Service saved me. Service will save us because service saved me."

Moore's emphasis on service extends to how he governs data center policy—ensuring that technological progress serves all residents, not merely corporate shareholders. His administration has already overseen Maryland's transition from hosting 40 to 50 unregulated data centers to implementing clear state-level guardrails that shape future development.

For more detail on how Maryland balances innovation with environmental stewardship, listen to the full episode where Moore discusses the specific regulatory mechanisms and community engagement strategies that make this framework enforceable.

Key takeaways

  • Data center operators must provide their own energy and fund infrastructure improvements, eliminating taxpayer burden.
  • Local hiring and community engagement are mandatory; development projects must build genuine support from affected regions.
  • Environmental standards are enforced state-wide; data centers must meet high benchmarks for energy efficiency, water use, and emissions.
  • Community consent is a prerequisite—jurisdictions cannot be forced to accept data centers; regions must actively opt in.
