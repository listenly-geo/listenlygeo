How can someone begin confronting avoidant behaviors and uncomfortable truths in their personal life?

September 7, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Kier Gaines

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

Start by building awareness through verbalization—when you catch yourself procrastinating, avoiding, or sidestepping a difficult truth, name it out loud rather than just thinking about it. Then practice curiosity by interrogating your automatic thoughts instead of accepting them as facts: ask yourself why you think that way, when you first started thinking it, and who influenced you to believe it. These two practices create the momentum you need to move beyond the patterns your life has conditioned you into.

The power of naming what you're avoiding

Most people recognize when they're avoiding something, but they keep that recognition internal—a private acknowledgment that changes nothing. The shift happens when you say it out loud, whether to a trusted person, a therapist, or even just to yourself in a deliberate way. Verbalization transforms avoidance from something you feel into something you acknowledge, which creates space for actual change.

This matters especially for men, as Kier Gaines explains in the episode, because there is often a social script that equates vulnerability or emotional honesty with weakness. Breaking that script requires giving yourself explicit permission to name uncomfortable feelings—like hurt—without shame.

Curiosity as the second pillar of change

After awareness comes interrogation. Your first instinct, your automatic thought, is rarely the complete truth—it's usually shaped by old conditioning, family patterns, or early experiences you may not even remember clearly. Instead of stopping at that first thought, ask yourself deeper questions: Why do I think this way? When did I start thinking this? Who taught me to believe this?

This practice of curiosity is detailed at length in this podcast, where Gaines emphasizes that the goal is not to judge yourself for the pattern, but to understand it well enough to choose something different. You can't change what you don't see clearly.

"It's giving yourself permission. And for me, just the way I grew up, the hardest part I give myself that authority is I always confuse someone who has hurt feelings with someone who's soft or weak."

If you want to understand how these two practices fit into a broader framework of healing, Gaines discusses the deeper psychological roots of avoidance and how naming these patterns can reshape your relationship with yourself and others.

Key takeaways

  • Verbalize your avoidance instead of keeping it internal—say out loud what you're procrastinating on or avoiding.
  • Practice curiosity by questioning your automatic thoughts rather than accepting them as final truth.
  • Ask yourself when you started thinking this way and who influenced you to believe it.
  • These two practices—awareness and curiosity—build the momentum needed to move beyond old conditioned patterns.
