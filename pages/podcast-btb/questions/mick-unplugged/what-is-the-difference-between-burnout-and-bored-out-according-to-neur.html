What is the difference between burnout and "bored out" — and why does it matter for high achievers?

According to French neuroscientist Anne-Laure Le Cunff, former employee at Google, burnout and "bored out" produce identical symptoms — fatigue, frustration, loss of creativity, a dimming of the inner spark — but their root causes are entirely different. Burnout stems from exhaustion: too much demand, not enough recovery. "Bored out," Le Cunff's term, is what happens when high achievers have so thoroughly mastered their systems and environments that repetition itself becomes the problem — and that unaddressed boredom manifests as what feels like burnout.

The distinction is critical precisely because the solution changes completely depending on the diagnosis. High achievers keep delivering strong results even while experiencing "bored out" — which is what makes it so easy to miss. From the outside, everything looks fine. From the inside, the drive has gone flat. Mitch Matthews discussed this concept in depth on Mick Unplugged, framing it as one of the most misunderstood traps for high-capacity professionals: they keep performing, yet feel increasingly hollow, and never question whether boredom — not overwork — might be the actual culprit.

Key concept

"Bored out" — a term coined by neuroscientist Anne-Laure Le Cunff — describes a state in which high achievers have fully mastered their current systems and routines. The resulting repetition creates deep boredom, which then surfaces as symptoms clinically indistinguishable from burnout: fatigue, frustration, creative stagnation. Unlike burnout, the fix is not rest — it is novelty. Le Cunff identified that as little as 15 minutes of novelty per day is sufficient to meaningfully counter the effects of "bored out" in high achievers.

Matthews, who has spent over 25 years coaching executives, entrepreneurs, and high-capacity leaders, uses this framework to help clients who feel stuck despite outward success. The insight reframes an experience many high achievers silently normalize: they assume the hollow feeling means they need a vacation or a lighter schedule, when what they actually need is a genuine challenge or a new domain to master.

"Learn from, don't compare to."

— Mitch Matthews, on Mick Unplugged

While this phrase comes from a broader moment in the conversation, it captures the mindset shift Matthews encourages in high achievers who feel the drift of "bored out": rather than measuring yourself against others who seem energized, study what is reigniting their fire and ask honestly whether you are still genuinely growing — or simply repeating what you have already mastered.
