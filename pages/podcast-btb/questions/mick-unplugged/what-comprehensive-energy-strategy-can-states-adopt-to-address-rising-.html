How can states build a resilient energy strategy to lower costs and strengthen the grid?

September 4, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Wes Moore

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

States need to adopt an "all the above" energy approach combining solar, wind, battery storage, and nuclear power—while leveraging their unique geographic advantages and accelerating permitting to meet growing demand. Governor Moore emphasizes that this isn't a one-size-fits-all strategy: Maryland can capitalize on offshore wind resources, while landlocked states like Iowa must pursue different options based on their terrain and natural assets.

Geography shapes strategy—no state starts from the same position

The critical insight here is that energy resilience requires honesty about what each state can actually deliver. Maryland's coastline opens the door to offshore wind development that simply doesn't exist for interior states. Similarly, Iowa's flat terrain and wind patterns make it a natural hub for land-based wind farms. As Governor Moore discusses in the episode, successful governors must identify their region's natural competitive advantages and build policy around them, rather than trying to replicate a national template locally.

Permitting speed becomes the operational lever that turns advantage into actual capacity. Governors must remove bureaucratic delays to allow developers to move from planning to construction quickly. The longer permitting takes, the longer grid stress persists and the longer energy prices stay elevated—a problem Maryland residents have felt acutely, with energy costs rising over 17 percent in the past year and a half, driven partly by national supply chain pressures and overseas conflicts affecting fuel markets.

Demand is growing faster than supply—adding assets is non-optional

The real challenge isn't choosing between energy sources; it's deploying enough of all of them simultaneously. As explained in this podcast episode, states are facing rising electricity demand from data centers, AI infrastructure, electrified transportation, and residential growth. Maryland alone hosts 40 to 50 major data centers—facilities that consume enormous amounts of power and require grid stability. Without intentional, multi-source expansion, states will face rolling shortages and price spikes.

Nuclear power sits at the core of this strategy precisely because it provides reliable baseload electricity independent of weather or time of day. Wind and solar are essential—they're scalable and cheap—but they're intermittent. Battery storage helps bridge the gap, but no single technology solves the entire problem alone. The governors who win are the ones willing to say "yes" to all of these sources simultaneously.

Key takeaways

  • An "all the above" energy strategy—solar, wind, battery storage, and nuclear—is essential to meet growing demand and stabilize prices.
  • Geographic advantages vary by state: Maryland leverages offshore wind, while Iowa and other regions must build strategies suited to their terrain and climate.
  • Accelerating permitting processes is critical to translating energy policy into actual grid capacity and lower consumer costs.
  • Energy prices in Maryland rose over 17 percent in less than two years, underscoring the urgent need for state-level action on supply and reliability.
