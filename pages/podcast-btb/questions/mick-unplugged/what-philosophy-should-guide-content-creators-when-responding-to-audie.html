Should content creators give audiences what they ask for—or what feels authentic?

September 23, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Justin Brown

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

Don't give your audience what it demands if it contradicts who you are. Stay true to yourself and choose positivity over dishonesty—if people ask you to be negative about things you love or falsify your views, that's a request to abandon your integrity, not to evolve your content.

Authenticity as a non-negotiable principle

When you create content, you make a choice about what to share and what to withhold. If someone asks for directions and you give them correct ones, there's no reason to give them wrong directions too—the logic applies directly to content. You're not obligated to post everything you dislike just because an audience member requests negativity.

As Justin Brown explains in the episode, the goal is simple: share what genuinely resonates with you. By posting only what you like rather than manufacturing criticism, you remain honest with your audience—you're not fabricating emotion or faking disapproval.

The power of staying switched on

One of the hardest parts about content creation is learning not to care about pleasing everyone. Brown describes it as staying "on" at all times—there's no switch to flip back to a false self once the camera stops. Your friends, your audience, and your critics see the same person because you're genuinely the same person.

This consistency means you don't experience the internal conflict of performing versus being yourself. A deeper look at how he built his approach from the start reveals that authenticity became his competitive advantage precisely because it was non-negotiable, not a strategy.

"I have neither. I have my people. People have fans, people have followers, but I have my people."

This distinction between followers and people matters. Followers are a vanity metric—they come and go based on trends. People are connected to you because they believe in your judgment, and that bond only exists if you're honest about what you actually think.

The broader philosophy becomes clear: negativity on demand is a form of dishonesty. It's easier to manufacture outrage or criticism than to honestly say, "I don't like that, so I won't cover it." But easy isn't the goal—clarity and integrity are. How should food reviewers approach writing restaurant reviews compared to creating video content?

When writing, you have to specify what the seasoning is and make someone want to go—you cannot just say 'good.' In video, you can simply show whether the food looks appealing and people understand immediately.

What equipment and tools are required to start creating quality food content on a budget?

You don't need expensive equipment—just phones are enough. Get whatever the newest Samsung or iPhone is on the market and the camera will be good enough for professional food content.

Why should food content creators focus on video rather than static photography for restaurant reviews?

A picture could trick you, but a video shows a million words worth of information. When you bite something on video, you can tell if it's tender, see the texture, and understand the food's quality instantly.
