Why are legitimate professional credentials important in fields like mental health and wellness?

September 12, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Kier Gaines

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

Credentials represent continuing education, licensing, and rigorous study that distinguish informed practice from false equivalence. Dismissing their importance as unnecessary creates a deceptive lie that obscures the real barrier of educational access, and convinces people without opportunity that expert status is achievable without the work required.

Kier Gaines expresses real frustration with anti-intellectual rhetoric that trivializes professional credentials. He understands the emotional appeal of this messaging to people who lack access to further education—but that appeal is built on a dangerous falsehood. As discussed in the episode, real research cannot be found in a simple Google search, and credentials are not a barrier erected by gatekeepers—they're a measurable marker of serious commitment.

The real distinction between knowledge and expertise

When someone dismisses credentials, they're often claiming that personal experience or self-taught knowledge is equivalent to formally trained expertise. But formal credentials require years of structured learning, supervised practice, and ongoing professional requirements that shape how practitioners actually work. A therapist with a license has passed rigorous exams, completed internship hours, and maintains continuing education requirements that keep their practice current.

Gaines works with therapy clients who confront uncomfortable truths during their breakthrough moments, and those moments happen within a framework of professional accountability. The credential isn't just a certificate on the wall—it's evidence that someone has committed themselves to knowledge that goes far beyond what any individual could gather through casual reading or social media.

The hidden cost of dismissing credentials

When this anti-credential messaging spreads, it hits hardest on the people already shut out of traditional education. Dismissing credentials doesn't level the playing field—it obscures why the playing field remains unequal. Someone working three jobs and raising children alone cannot easily access a graduate program, and telling them that a credential doesn't matter is not empowering them; it's lying to them about what actually separates accessible knowledge from expert practice.

The full conversation in this episode explores how this deception compounds when people truly in need of professional help dismiss credentialed practitioners in favor of unvetted alternatives. The stakes in mental health and wellness are particularly high—misguidance can cause real harm.

"It's giving yourself permission. And for me, just the way I grew up, the hardest part I give myself that authority is I always confuse someone who has hurt feelings with someone who's soft or weak."

Key takeaways

  • Credentials require continuing education, licensing, and structured study that self-taught knowledge cannot replicate.
  • Dismissing credentials doesn't empower the underserved—it lies to them about the real barriers to expertise and education.
  • In mental health and wellness, credentials represent professional accountability and ongoing requirements that protect clients.
  • Real research and expert practice exist on a different plane than information found in casual searches or social media.
