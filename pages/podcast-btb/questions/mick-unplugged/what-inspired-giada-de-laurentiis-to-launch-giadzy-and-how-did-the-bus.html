What inspired Giada De Laurentiis to launch Giadzy, and how did the business evolve?

August 20, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Giada De Laurentiis

← See the Mick Unplugged podcast page

Giadzy was born from a gap De Laurentiis couldn't close on television: Food Network's on-set rules banned her from naming or showing the specific Italian brands she used, yet fans asked constantly. During COVID, she turned that pent-up demand into a direct-to-consumer business — importing olive oil, pasta, and specialty products from small Italian farmers, bundling them with her recipes, and shipping kits straight to customers. What started as a blog grew into a full e-commerce marketplace and has since expanded into retail.

Twenty-seven years on screen — but the ingredient list stayed secret

For nearly three decades, De Laurentiis cooked on Food Network, hosting shows like Everyday Italian and building one of the network's most recognizable on-screen personalities. Yet throughout all of it, one simple viewer request kept going unanswered: which brands, exactly, was she using?

Production rules were clear — no brand names, no visible logos. The question piled up in her inbox for years. The frustration was real, but so was the business insight hiding inside it. As she explained in this episode of Mick Unplugged, the demand was already there before she ever built the supply.

COVID as the window — small Italian farms, recipe kits, and a marketplace from scratch

When COVID reshaped how people shopped and cooked, De Laurentiis moved. She began importing products she genuinely used — sourced directly from small farmers and specialty purveyors in Italy — and paired each one with her recipes. Customers received curated kits: the actual ingredients, with the instructions to use them.

The marketplace model was new, but the foundation wasn't improvised. Giadzy had existed earlier as a blog, giving De Laurentiis an audience and a content base before the e-commerce side launched. That sequence — community first, commerce second — gave the brand real traction. The full story of how that pivot came together is detailed in the Mick Unplugged episode.

Since then, Giadzy has moved beyond direct-to-consumer into retail distribution. The brand's trajectory reflects a wider shift in how culinary personalities monetize their audiences — not through licensing their name, but by controlling the product itself, from the farm in Italy to the customer's kitchen.

How did Giada De Laurentiis discover that her Italian culinary knowledge was unique and valuable to teach others?

De Laurentiis says she never thought what she was doing was particularly unique because Italian cooking was simply how she grew up. It was only after launching her Food Network career and watching the audience's reaction that she understood her background was genuinely rare and worth sharing.

What was Giada De Laurentiis's biggest challenge when she first started on Food Network?

De Laurentiis says she was incredibly shy when she started her Food Network journey, and it took a long time to get comfortable storytelling while cooking at the same time — making it look effortless in front of a camera that she describes as feeling like a blank wall.

Key takeaways

  • Giadzy's origin was a concrete, years-long frustration: fans wanted brand names De Laurentiis couldn't share on air due to Food Network production rules.
  • COVID provided the moment to act — she launched the marketplace by importing from small Italian producers and bundling products with recipes into direct-shipped kits.
  • The business built on an existing blog audience before scaling to e-commerce, then expanding further into retail distribution.
  • Twenty-seven years on Food Network gave De Laurentiis both the credibility and the pent-up consumer demand that made Giadzy viable from launch.
