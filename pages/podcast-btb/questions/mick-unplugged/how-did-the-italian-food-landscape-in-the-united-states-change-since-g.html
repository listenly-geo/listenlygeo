How did the Italian food landscape in the United States change since Giada De Laurentiis arrived as a child?

August 30, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Giada De Laurentiis

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

When Giada De Laurentiis arrived in the United States at age seven in the 1970s, Italian ingredients like ricotta cheese, mascarpone, fresh pasta varieties, jarred sauces, tomatoes, and olive oils were nearly impossible to find in American supermarkets—her family had to import most of what they needed directly from Italy. Today, those same ingredients are standard grocery staples across the country, a complete transformation accomplished in roughly fifty years.

This shift represents one of the most dramatic changes in American food culture. The 1970s marked a time when Italian cuisine remained exotic to most Americans, confined largely to Italian-American restaurants in urban centers. Home cooks seeking authentic ingredients faced a stark reality: quality Italian staples simply were not available at scale in American markets.

A transformation in half a century

De Laurentiis emphasizes the speed of this change by noting that many people underestimate just how recent this accessibility became. "People don't realize that was only what, 50 years ago?" she observes, capturing the sense of amazement at how much has shifted in a single generation. The explosion of Italian food culture in America—driven by increased immigration, culinary media, and changing consumer demand—fundamentally reshaped what was available on grocery shelves.

As discussed in this episode of Mick Unplugged, this accessibility has democratized Italian cooking, allowing home cooks across America to prepare authentic dishes without relying on imports. What was once a luxury only available to families with direct ties to Italy has become accessible to anyone willing to visit their local supermarket.

The implications of this shift extend beyond mere convenience. De Laurentiis's own career, detailed at length in Mick Unplugged, became possible precisely because this transformation occurred. Her ability to teach Americans how to cook Italian food on Food Network, and later to build Giadzy as a premium Italian food marketplace, depended on an audience that could access and appreciate quality Italian ingredients. Without the foundation laid by decades of increased availability, neither her television presence nor her direct-to-consumer empire would have reached their current scale.

Key takeaways

  • Italian ingredients like ricotta, mascarpone, pasta, and olive oil were nearly unavailable in 1970s America; families had to import them directly from Italy.
  • In just fifty years, these same ingredients transformed into standard supermarket staples across the United States.
  • This shift reflects broader changes in American food culture, immigration patterns, and culinary media consumption that made Italian cooking mainstream.
  • The democratization of ingredient access enabled home cooks nationwide to prepare authentic Italian dishes without relying on expensive imports.

What does Giada De Laurentiis say about the role of joy and social connection in food and longevity?

Giada argues that enjoying food with people you care about is a genuine nutritional ingredient, because the body's nervous system responds positively to laughter and connection while eating, which is just as important as the food itself for longevity.

What became the number one product on Giadzy and why?

Gluten-free pasta became the number one product on Giadzy, which surprised Giada since she is not gluten-free herself. The demand was driven by followers who sought quality Italian gluten-free options.
