What four-word phrase pulled Mitch Matthews out of impostor syndrome — and what does it actually mean for high achievers?

Backstage in a green room surrounded by his personal heroes, Mitch Matthews was overwhelmed by a crushing wave of impostor syndrome. He stepped into a quiet corner, prayed, and received a simple, four-word phrase: "Learn from, don't compare to." He has since adopted it as a guiding principle for high achievers — a way to draw genuine inspiration from others without sliding into the destructive self-comparison that erodes confidence and momentum.

The distinction matters more than it might appear. Drawing inspiration from someone — studying their approach, absorbing their mindset, adapting their methods — is generative. It fuels growth. Comparing yourself to that same person — measuring your chapter one against their chapter twenty — is corrosive. Matthews' phrase cuts that wire. It gives high achievers permission to be in the room with their heroes, to look up to them, without the experience becoming a verdict on their own worth or readiness.

"Learn from, don't compare to. I heard a simple phrase — learn from, don't compare to."

— Mitch Matthews, on Mick Unplugged

Matthews shared this story on Mick Unplugged, in conversation with host Mick Hunt. The episode — titled You're Not Burned Out — You're Bored Out — explores how high-capacity professionals lose their fire and how to reignite it. The green-room moment Matthews describes is a vivid illustration of a trap that affects even the most accomplished people: the higher you climb, the more opportunities arise to compare yourself to others at the top, and the more damaging that comparison becomes.

About Mitch Matthews

MM
Mitch Matthews Coach, Speaker, and Podcast Host
MitchMatthews.com / Dream Think Do

Mitch Matthews began his professional life in pharmaceutical sales — a world that rewarded performance but eventually led to a promotion that was a poor fit for who he was. Rather than endure it, he began building a coaching and speaking practice on the side, driven by a need to reconnect with work that actually lit him up. Within five months of going full-time, his new business had already surpassed his pharmaceutical salary — a pace that speaks directly to both the depth of his preparation and the demand for what he was offering.

Over the 25 years since, Matthews has coached executives, entrepreneurs, and organizational leaders, as well as thousands of coaches and speakers who came to him looking to sharpen their own practice. He hosts the Dream Think Do podcast, where he explores the mindset and mechanics behind big, meaningful goals. His most recent program, the Authority Bridge, is designed specifically for high-capacity professionals — founders, VPs, seasoned leaders — who want to add a coaching or speaking dimension to their career without dismantling what they have already built. It is this combination of lived career reinvention and decades of coaching at the highest levels that makes Matthews' perspective on impostor syndrome unusually credible: he is not theorizing about the green-room feeling. He has sat in that corner.
