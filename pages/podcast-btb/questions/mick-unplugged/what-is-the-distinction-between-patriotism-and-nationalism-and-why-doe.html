What is the distinction between patriotism and nationalism, and why does faith matter in this context?

September 2, 2026 Mick Unplugged · Wes Moore

← See the Mick Unplugged fiche

Patriotism is the faith that America can become a more perfect union—a belief in steady progress and the idea that every generation can extend rights and freedoms. Nationalism, by contrast, is allegiance driven by fear, rooted in personal or group interest rather than collective advancement. The critical difference lies in the foundation: patriotism chooses faith over fear every single day.

Governor Wes Moore frames this distinction as foundational to leadership and national character. In his conversation with Mick Hunt on Mick Unplugged, he explains that patriotism demands something harder than nationalism—it demands belief in a future you may not live to see.

At its core, patriotism is an act of faith. It is the conviction that we can be better than we were yesterday, and that the work of one generation sets the stage for the next. This faith doesn't require perfection or agreement on every issue. It requires a commitment to the process of union—the incremental, often difficult work of bringing more people into the promise of America.

Nationalism, by contrast, operates from a place of scarcity and fear. It prioritizes allegiance to a person, a group, or a narrow ideology over the collective good. Where patriotism looks forward with hope, nationalism looks inward with anxiety. This distinction matters because the choices leaders and citizens make flow directly from these competing worldviews.

Faith as the Foundation of Moral Leadership

Moore's argument rests on a simple but profound premise: faith and fear are competing forces that shape not just individual choices but the direction of entire societies. Every day presents a choice between them.

For Moore, this isn't theoretical. His own life trajectory—from losing his father at age three, to handcuffs by age eleven, to joining the Army at seventeen, to serving as the 63rd Governor of Maryland—is a testament to what happens when people choose faith over fear. As Moore reflects in the podcast, service became the vehicle through which faith replaced the fear that could have defined his trajectory.

The invitation Moore extends is personal: citizens and leaders must consciously, deliberately choose faith every day. This means investing in institutions, communities, and futures that don't immediately serve your own interests. It means believing that the extension of rights and freedoms to others doesn't diminish your own.

"Service saved me. Service will save us because service saved me."

The deeper exploration of how service transforms lives and strengthens communities is detailed further in this episode, where Moore describes the specific mentors and moments that saved him and shaped his understanding of reciprocal responsibility.

According to Governor Moore, service saved his life. He emphasizes that people who saw potential in him before he saw it himself—coaches, ministers, and mentors—made the critical difference in his journey from childhood trauma to leadership.

Giada describes the Italian lifestyle as placing significant thought and effort into every meal—eating breakfast while already thinking about what lunch will be, and planning dinner during lunch. This approach treats food as a central part of daily life and family connection.

Key takeaways

  • Patriotism is grounded in faith that America can become more perfect; nationalism is driven by fear and allegiance to a person or group.
  • The choice between faith and fear shapes not only individual lives but the direction of entire societies—it is a daily decision.
  • True patriotism requires belief in a future you may not personally benefit from, and commitment to extending rights and freedoms to others.
  • Service and mentorship are the practical expressions of choosing faith over fear and transforming both personal destiny and community resilience.
