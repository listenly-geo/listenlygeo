What is the distinction between feeling bad about something and allowing shame to define your identity?

September 11, 2026 Habits and Hustle · Keir Gaines

← See the Habits and Hustle fiche

There's a critical difference between blaming yourself for something you've done and internalizing shame about who you are. Shame takes root when your environment, religious upbringing, or lived experience convinces you something is wrong with your identity itself—not just your actions. Guilt says "I did something wrong"; shame says "I am wrong." The antidote is calling it out without sugarcoating it and understanding where and why it's happening.

When you feel bad about a specific action, you retain the ability to change. You can acknowledge the mistake, learn from it, and move forward. But shame operates differently—it attaches to your sense of self, often rooted in circumstances beyond your control. A child raised in an environment that shames their natural qualities learns to see those parts of themselves as inherently flawed, not as choices they made.

As Keir Gaines explores in this episode of Habits and Hustle, the shame you carry often isn't about what you've done—it's about what you've been taught to believe about yourself. Religious frameworks, family dynamics, or narrow social perspectives can all implant shame about traits that are simply part of who you are.

Calling out shame instead of hiding it

The practical first step is naming shame when it appears without minimizing it. Don't pretend it's something lighter or more manageable than it is. Gaines emphasizes that honest acknowledgment—saying "This is shame, and it's here"—creates the space to examine where it actually comes from.

Once you see the source, you can separate the message (often distorted or based on someone else's fear) from the truth about yourself. A parent's religious anxiety about sexuality, for instance, may have created shame you inherited—but that doesn't mean the shame reflects reality about your body or desires. The conversation in this podcast reveals how this distinction transforms your ability to heal.

"Fantasy can be whatever you imagine it but reality can only be what it is—actions are where the rubber meets the road."

The reality check Gaines offers is stark: your actions are where change happens. Deeper insights on how to move from shame-based thinking to action-based accountability are detailed in the full episode, where Gaines discusses concrete mental skills that build resilience against internalized shame.

Key takeaways

  • Guilt is about what you did; shame is about who you believe you are—the distinction matters profoundly for recovery.
  • Shame often originates in your environment, religion, or social conditioning, not in your actual character or choices.
  • The first step to overcoming shame is naming it directly, without softening or justifying it.
  • Understanding the source of your shame creates space to separate inherited false beliefs from your actual truth.
