Why do high cognitive performers sometimes decline faster once dementia sets in?

High-performing individuals tend to maintain cognitive function longer because of greater cognitive reserve — the brain's accumulated capacity to compensate for early damage through coping strategies and neural mechanisms. But when that compensatory threshold is finally crossed, the decline gradient is often steeper and more rapid than in those with lower baseline performance, precisely because the resources that were sustaining them can no longer hold. Multiple studies have confirmed this pattern of accelerated decline trajectories in previously high-functioning individuals.

This paradox — that cognitive strength can mask vulnerability — is central to how Masud Husain approaches brain aging research at Oxford University. The brain is not simply "holding on" in high performers; it is actively compensating through redundant neural pathways and behavioral strategies that effectively delay the moment when symptoms become visible. The problem is that this delay compresses the observable window of decline, making the drop appear sudden and severe to those around the person — and to the person themselves.

Husain's work at the Dementia Research Group situates this insight within a broader picture: there are over 200 causes of dementia, of which Alzheimer's disease is the most common. Across these conditions, the cognitive reserve mechanism operates similarly — it protects until it cannot. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for anyone interested in the real trajectory of brain aging, not the simplified version. Key concept

Cognitive reserve refers to the brain's ability to cope with damage or disease by using compensatory strategies and neural mechanisms built up over a lifetime of cognitive activity. High performers accumulate more of this reserve — which delays the onset of visible symptoms but does not prevent the underlying damage from accumulating.

About Masud Husain

MH
Masud Husain Co-lead, Dementia Research Group · Editor-in-Chief, Brain Oxford University

Masud Husain is recognized as one of the world's leading neurologists in the fields of memory, motivation, and cognitive decline. He co-leads the Dementia Research Group at Oxford University, one of the foremost research centers studying how and why the brain deteriorates with age — and what can be done about it.

He also serves as editor-in-chief of Brain, a journal widely regarded as among the most respected publications in neurology, giving him a panoramic view of cutting-edge findings across the entire field. He himself attended Oxford University, meaning his career has been anchored to the institution at which he now leads research.

What distinguishes Husain from purely theoretical researchers is his decades of work with real patients. His understanding of dementia, apathy, and cognitive decline is built from direct clinical observation — including complex cases like patients who suffered strokes that were invisible on standard scans yet produced profound behavioral changes. This combination of clinical depth, research leadership, and editorial oversight makes him a uniquely authoritative voice on questions of how the brain ages and where conventional assumptions about that process go wrong.

Could dopaminergic drugs help people with treatment-resistant depression? Dr. Husain notes that approximately one third of people with depression do not respond to serotonin-based antidepressants. He explains that clinical trials exploring dopaminergic drug approaches offer a promising avenue for those who have not responded to standard treatments. Can apathy caused by brain damage be treated with medication, and what is the evidence? Dr. Husain treated his patient David with Ropinirole, a drug that works on dopamine receptors in the brain, after David's strokes caused severe apathy. Within three months, David had a new job and a new girlfriend — a dramatic transformation that illustrates the potential of dopaminergic treatment for apathy. Is there any scientific evidence that social media scrolling causes dopamine hits? Dr. Husain states there is absolutely no evidence that scrolling through social media produces dopamine hits, calling it a myth perpetuated in part by popular culture and misrepresentations of how dopamine actually functions in the brain.
