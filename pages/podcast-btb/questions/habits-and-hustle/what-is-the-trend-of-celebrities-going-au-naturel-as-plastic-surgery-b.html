Why are celebrities going natural while plastic surgery gets cheaper for everyone?

August 29, 2026 Habits and Hustle · Shani Suissa

← See the Habits and Hustle fiche

As plastic surgery becomes increasingly accessible to lower-income groups, celebrities are reversing course and returning to a more natural aesthetic—no heavy surgery, minimal makeup. But achieving that genuinely natural-looking result now costs significantly more than generic procedures, effectively re-establishing class separation and pricing the refined look back out of reach for ordinary people.

The dynamic is paradoxical: democratization of surgery has created an inverse status marker. When cosmetic procedures were rare and expensive, they signaled wealth and sophistication. Now that rhinoplasties, facelifts, and injectables are routine in middle-income demographics, the elites have pivoted toward restraint, knowing that true naturalness—the hardest aesthetic to achieve—commands premium pricing.

The pricing wall between generic and refined results

The cost gap is stark. A lower facelift performed by an off-social-media surgeon can cost $24,000, yet the same procedure delivered by a celebrity-tier or Instagram-famous surgeon climbs to $250,000–$500,000. Shani Suissa and host Jennifer Cohen note that surgeons active on social media charge 10 to 20 times more than their quietly practicing counterparts for equivalent work—a markup driven entirely by follower count and content production, not superior surgical skill.

As discussed at length in this episode of Habits and Hustle, the inflation extends to baseline procedures: nose jobs that once cost $8,000 now run $20,000 across the board. The surge in demand has lifted all prices, but the premium for a surgeon with a polished social media presence has created a two-tier market where the same nose job yields different aesthetics depending on the wallet size and the surgeon's online fame.

"If you are pretty before, you're going to be pretty after. If you're ugly before, you're going to be uglier with a tighter face after. It's not going to change what you are."

This observation cuts to the core issue: surgery doesn't fundamentally alter who you are, but it does reshape what you're seen as. In an era of Instagram filters and Zoom fatigue, cosmetic intervention has become normalized as self-care. Yet the obsession with "before and after" photos masks a harder truth: as Shani Suissa explains in the podcast, even the most refined procedure cannot address the psychological insecurity underneath. The result is a market where price increasingly determines aesthetic quality and longevity, not medical expertise alone.

Recovery, visibility, and the two-year rule

The physical reality of surgery complicates the celebrity narrative. Suissa remained visibly swollen from her rhinoplasty two years after the procedure—a fact confirmed by her dermatologist during a laser facial. Yet celebrity before-and-afters present surgical results as instant and permanent, glossing over months of bruising, swelling, and sometimes serious complications like septum perforation.

This gap between marketing and reality fuels demand among people who don't understand the true cost and recovery burden. Celebrities present polished outcomes precisely because they can afford extended downtime, revision surgeries, and high-end aftercare that ensures optimal healing—resources inaccessible to most patients undergoing the same procedure at a lower price point.

Natural-looking cosmetic surgery: A surgical aesthetic defined by minimal visible change to the original face, refined proportions, and results that appear to be the person's natural features rather than augmented ones. Achieving this outcome requires both superior surgical technique and meticulous aftercare, commanding premium pricing from elite surgeons. Generic procedures, by contrast, prioritize visible transformation and cost less to perform.

Why has plastic surgery become so much more accessible and normalized in recent years?

The hosts point to multiple converging factors: the rise of Zoom during and after COVID forcing people to stare at their own faces constantly, social media filters creating unrealistic beauty standards, and increased availability of procedures in countries like Turkey and Tijuana making surgery affordable to lower-income demographics.

Does plastic surgery actually resolve personal insecurities, or do people simply shift focus to new ones?

Shani Suissa states that insecurities that exist before a cosmetic procedure will still exist after it, and that patients often simply move their fixation to a different body part or feature, never achieving lasting satisfaction from surgery alone.

Is a surgeon's social media popularity a reliable indicator of their surgical quality?

Shani Suissa and host Jen argue that a plastic surgeon who is very active on social media is likely spending more time creating content than performing complex surgeries, and that the best surgeons—who charge far less—often operate quietly without online presence.

Key takeaways

  • As surgery becomes mainstream and affordable, celebrities have shifted toward natural aesthetics as a new status marker, effectively re-establishing class separation through pricing rather than accessibility.
  • Social-media-famous surgeons charge 10 to 20 times more than non-social counterparts for equivalent procedures, creating a two-tier market where online presence inflates cost without improving surgical quality.
  • Achieving a genuinely natural-looking result requires superior technique and extended recovery oversight—resources available only at premium price points—while generic procedures deliver visible transformation at lower cost.
  • Surgery does not resolve underlying psychological insecurity; patients often redirect their fixation to a new body part, meaning the emotional benefit is temporary regardless of outcome quality.
