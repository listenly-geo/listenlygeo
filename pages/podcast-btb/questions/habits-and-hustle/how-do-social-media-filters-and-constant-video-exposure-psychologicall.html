Why Social Media and Constant Video Exposure Change How We See Ourselves

1 September 2026 Habits and Hustle · Shani Suissa

← See the Habits and Hustle fiche

Humans were not biologically built to see their own reflections or outer appearance constantly. Modern inventions like cameras, mirrors, and video calls have fundamentally changed this reality, and as a result, they have degraded self-love and driven people toward cosmetic procedures they might never have considered. People born in the 1800s, without constant photographic exposure to their own faces, would largely have considered themselves quite attractive and would not be driven to seek surgical alteration.

The psychological cost of constant self-reflection is real and measurable. Every time you join a Zoom call, open Snapchat with filters, or scroll through a photo feed of yourself, you're reinforcing a relationship with your appearance that didn't exist for most of human history. This perpetual mirror effect has created a feedback loop where insecurity becomes normalized, and perceived flaws become magnified through repeated exposure and comparison.

As discussed in Habits and Hustle, the rise of social media filters has compounded this issue by creating an alternate version of yourself that feels attainable but isn't real. When you filter your face every day, then see an unfiltered version in a video call, the cognitive dissonance creates dissatisfaction. This dissatisfaction, amplified across millions of users, has directly contributed to the explosion in cosmetic procedures.

The Historical Shift in Self-Perception

People born centuries ago simply did not have the option to scrutinize their own features the way modern people do. Mirrors were rare, expensive, and not portable—you might see your reflection a handful of times per year. Photography didn't exist until the 1800s, and even then, it was a rare, deliberate event, not a daily documentation of your face.

This absence of constant visual feedback had a profound psychological impact: people accepted their appearance as fixed and natural. A crooked nose, asymmetrical features, or aging skin were simply part of being human. There was no expectation to "fix" what nature gave you, and critically, there was no visual reinforcement of insecurity because you weren't seeing your face dozens of times a day through multiple lenses.

Today, the inverse is true. You see your face in reflections, photos, videos, and filtered versions so frequently that you develop a relationship with appearance that previous generations never experienced. The podcast episode explores how this shift has transformed cosmetic surgery from a rare, stigmatized procedure into a normalized consumer choice—not because people's features have changed, but because the way we perceive and obsess over those features has fundamentally altered.

"If you are pretty before, you're going to be pretty after. If you're ugly before, you're going to be uglier with a tighter face after. It's not going to change what you are."

This quote captures a hard truth often overlooked in cosmetic surgery marketing: the procedure doesn't address the underlying insecurity—it only addresses the symptom. Someone who feels inherently unattractive before surgery will likely feel similar doubts afterward, no matter how technically skilled the surgeon. The real problem is not the nose or the face itself, but the psychological relationship created by constant exposure and social comparison.

Key takeaways

  • Constant visual exposure to your own face—through mirrors, cameras, phones, and video calls—is a modern phenomenon that humans were never psychologically designed to handle.
  • Social media filters and Zoom fatigue have created unrealistic self-images and amplified body image insecurity, driving demand for cosmetic procedures.
  • Cosmetic surgery addresses the symptom, not the root psychological issue; people who feel inherently unattractive before surgery often feel the same afterward.
  • Historical perspective matters: people without constant photographic exposure to their own faces accepted their appearance and rarely sought surgical alteration.
