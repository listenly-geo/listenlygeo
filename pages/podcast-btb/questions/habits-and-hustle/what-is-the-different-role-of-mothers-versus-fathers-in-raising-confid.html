What role do mothers and fathers play in raising confident daughters?

September 23, 2026 Habits and Hustle · Shefali Tsabary

← See the Habits and Hustle podcast fiche

Your father represents the outside world to you and teaches you how to be powerful and sovereign by allowing you space to have boundaries and rebel against him without being labeled rude or dramatic. Your mother is your closest mirror for your internal relationship with yourself—if she is self-critical and self-harming, you absorb this at a cellular level. Your daughter learns her external power from her father and her self-worth from her mother.

The father's role: sovereignty and the outside world

The father functions as a daughter's first window into how the external world will receive her. When a father allows his daughter to express disagreement, set boundaries, and even rebel without shaming her, he is teaching her that her voice has value in spaces beyond the home. This permission to push back—to say no without being called dramatic—becomes the foundation for how she will advocate for herself with peers, teachers, colleagues, and eventually romantic partners.

A father who models respect for his daughter's autonomy is essentially saying: you are allowed to be powerful here, which means you are allowed to be powerful everywhere. This is discussed in depth in Raising Conscious Daughters, where Dr. Tsabary explores how paternal permission to dissent directly shapes a daughter's confidence in social and professional settings.

The mother's role: the mirror of self-treatment

The mother-daughter relationship operates on a different frequency. A daughter watches her mother and learns what she should do with herself. If the mother is perpetually self-critical, speaks harshly about her own body, or practices perfectionism to the point of self-harm, the daughter doesn't simply observe this—she internalizes it as an operating system for how she should treat herself.

This internalization happens at a preverbal, cellular level. A daughter doesn't need to be told "be critical of yourself." She absorbs it through osmosis: through her mother's sighs at the mirror, her apologies for taking up space, her pursuit of flawlessness at any cost. The daughter's internal dialogue—the voice she hears when she looks in the mirror or makes a mistake—is largely her mother's voice. As Dr. Shefali explains in the episode, this is why conscious parenting begins with parents examining their own wounds: a mother cannot give her daughter genuine self-compassion if she does not practice it herself.

"The conscious parent raises themselves so they don't pass on their crap to their children."

The episode goes deeper into how parental awareness of body image, perfectionism, and social comparison directly affects daughters—especially in an age where girls begin dieting by age 8 or 9 and absorb self-harming behaviors from the adults closest to them.

Why both roles matter: building the whole person

A confident daughter needs both. From her father, she learns that her boundaries are legitimate and that power is not rude. From her mother, she learns that she is worthy of her own kindness and respect. Remove one, and you leave a gap. A daughter with a present, respectful father but a self-harming mother may be able to advocate externally while despising herself internally. A daughter with a nurturing mother but an absent or invalidating father may be kind to herself yet struggle to claim space in the world.

This duality—external sovereignty and internal self-worth—is what conscious parenting aims to cultivate, and it begins with parents understanding their own roles and healing their own patterns before passing them down.

What are the primary actionable steps parents can take today to protect daughters from social media harm?

First, de-stimulate and limit exposure—no phones until age 16 or 17 because girls must find themselves before they lose themselves in digital fragmentation. Second, model authentic presence: put your own phone down and show your daughter what genuine self-worth looks like offline.

How do social media algorithms specifically exploit the vulnerabilities of teenage girls and boys differently?

Algorithms know that girls are relationally sensitive and seeking validation; Meta's study revealed that the algorithm detected when a girl took a selfie and then targeted her with body-image content designed to create insecurity. Boys, by contrast, are algorithmically targeted around status and dominance.

What are the neurochemical differences between adolescent girls and boys that require different parenting strategies?

Girls are fueled by estrogen, which creates high demand and high receptivity for oxytocin in the brain, making them relationally sensitive and acutely aware of approval and rejection. Boys experience 50 times more testosterone at age 13 than their previous levels, driving them toward autonomy, competition, and building competence.
