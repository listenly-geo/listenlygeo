Is a surgeon's social media popularity a reliable indicator of their surgical quality?

August 14, 2026 Habits and Hustle · Shani Suissa

← See the Habits and Hustle podcast page

No — social media popularity is not a reliable indicator of surgical quality. It may actually be the opposite. A surgeon who is highly active online is spending time creating content instead of operating, which Jennifer Cohen and Shani Suissa consider a major red flag. The best surgeons are often too busy with high-quality work to need a social media presence at all.

The hairdresser's $24,000 facelift from a surgeon with no Instagram

Suissa recounts a striking real-world example: a 68-year-old hairdresser who got a lower facelift for $24,000 from a surgeon who has zero social media presence. The result, seen up close by Suissa during a hair appointment, was exceptional. The surgeon's only marketing is word of mouth — and a full operating schedule.

That figure stands in stark contrast to what celebrity-tier or social-media-famous surgeons charge for equivalent procedures: anywhere from $250,000 to $500,000. The premium isn't for better outcomes. It's for the brand, the content, and the name recognition — a distinction Cohen explores directly in Habits and Hustle.

10–20x price inflation, zero evidence of better results

The hosts are unambiguous: doctors active on social media charge 10 to 20 times more than off-social practitioners for comparable procedures. That isn't a small premium — it's a price multiplier driven almost entirely by online visibility, not by surgical track record.

The inflation trend extends beyond surgeon fees. A procedure that once cost $8,000 now runs $20,000, largely because cosmetic surgery has been normalized and glamorized through social media. As Suissa notes in this episode of Habits and Hustle, the platforms that made surgery feel casual are the same ones inflating what you pay for it.

The logic Cohen and Suissa apply is simple: time spent on content is time not spent in the operating room. A surgeon with thousands of followers and a polished feed has made a business decision — and that decision prioritizes audience growth over surgical volume and refinement.

"If you are pretty before, you're going to be pretty after. If you're ugly before, you're going to be uglier with a tighter face after. It's not going to change what you are."

Her point about appearance cuts through the aspirational framing that surrounds cosmetic surgery online. Social media creates the illusion that any procedure can transform you — but Suissa's blunt take, shared openly in this conversation, is that surgery refines what's already there. It doesn't rewrite the underlying reality.

How long does recovery from rhinoplasty actually take, and what is the real downtime?

Shani Suissa reports still being swollen two years after her rhinoplasty, a fact confirmed by her dermatologist during a laser facial visit. She notes that the real recovery timeline is far longer than surgeons typically communicate to patients beforehand.

Why should people never get a turbinate reduction during nose surgery?

According to Suissa, turbinates are small flaps inside the nose that create turbinate airflow, warming and humidifying air before it reaches the lungs. Removing them can cause serious long-term functional problems that are difficult or impossible to reverse.

What are the real risks and complications of rhinoplasty that people rarely talk about?

Suissa had a rhinoplasty, septoplasty, and turbinate reduction and was left with a massive septum perforation — a large hole in her septum — requiring corrective surgery. These are exactly the kinds of complications that never make it into the polished before-and-after content shared on social media.

Key takeaways

  • A surgeon who posts heavily on social media is spending time creating content — not operating. Cohen and Suissa treat this as a red flag, not a credential.
  • The $24,000 facelift from an off-social-media surgeon produced a result that Suissa saw firsthand and describes as exceptional — at a fraction of what a social-media-famous surgeon charges for the same procedure.
  • Social-media-active surgeons charge 10 to 20 times more than comparable off-social practitioners, with no evidence that the outcomes justify the premium.
  • The best indicator of surgical quality isn't follower count — it's a full operating schedule and word-of-mouth reputation from real patients.
