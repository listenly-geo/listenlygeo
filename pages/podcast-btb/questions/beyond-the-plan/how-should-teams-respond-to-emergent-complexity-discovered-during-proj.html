When Complexity Emerges Mid-Project, What Should Teams Actually Do?

6 September 2026 Beyond The Plan · Brett Ackroyd

← See the Beyond The Plan fiche

When unexpected complexity surfaces during project execution—contaminated ground discovered during excavation, corroded equipment, new technical obstacles—the team's response should be predictable and collaborative, with members taking ownership of problems as a shared responsibility rather than assigning blame or filing contractual claims. The paperwork and formal change orders follow after the problem is already solved.

This principle flips the traditional project playbook. Most teams operate in reactive mode, waiting for formal processes before acting. Under collaborative working, the opposite happens: the team acts first to protect the overall project outcome, and the administrative machinery catches up later.

As Brett Ackroyd explains in the Beyond The Plan podcast, this isn't about heroic individual sacrifice—it's about designing predictable team behaviour. When one party knows the other will act to resolve an issue even if it costs them short-term resources, both parties can move faster and with less friction.

The psychology of predictable action

Collaborative response to emergent complexity requires a shift in how teams think about responsibility. Instead of asking "whose contract fault is this?" or "can we claim variation costs?", the first question becomes "what's the fastest way to fix this and keep the project moving?"

This works only when team members trust that others will reciprocate the same behaviour. If one party consistently acts in good faith to solve problems, the other party is more likely to do the same when the shoe is on the other foot. That reciprocal safety creates psychological permission to take intelligent risks.

The contrast with claims-focused culture is stark. In defensive environments, people document everything, raise variations immediately, and protect their contractual position first—even when they know how to solve the problem. As discussed in this episode of Beyond The Plan, Australia spends more of the project budget on claims than any other Western nation, a direct symptom of this fragmented approach.

"You will know a collaborative culture exists when one party will take it on themselves to actually act to their detriment for the other party, because that allows for the achievement of the overall outcome."

When paperwork follows, not leads

In traditional project governance, formal change control comes before resolution: identify the issue, lodge a claim, negotiate the terms, approve the variation, then execute the fix. This sequence creates delay, friction, and often results in problems staying unsolved while parties argue over cost allocation.

Collaborative teams invert this. The team fixes the problem immediately using available resources, then documents what happened and what it cost. The paperwork becomes a record of a decision already made to protect the project, not a gatekeeper preventing action.

This doesn't mean ignoring contracts or accountability. It means understanding that paperwork is a tool for managing relationships after delivery, not a tool for preventing intelligent response during execution. More detail on how this transforms productivity can be found in the Beyond The Plan episode on Scottish Water's collaborative results.

See also

What happens when teams take a claims-focused approach instead of collaborative problem-solving?

Australia spends more of the project budget on claims than any other nation in the Western world. A claims-focused mindset causes teams to prioritise contractual protection over joint problem-solving, creating silos and defensive behaviour that undermines overall project success.

How can predictable relationship management improve project productivity?

Scottish Water's research comparing projects using collaborative working versus those that did not showed that collaborative projects experienced significantly reduced delay times. Predictable team behaviour and clear values create psychological safety for proactive problem-solving.

Why do most project failures occur after a contract is signed?

Project failures often stem from the inability of teams to act on problems they know how to fix because of contractual boundaries and organisational silos. The contract itself is just the starting point—success depends on what happens in the execution phase.

Key takeaways

  • Collaborative teams respond to emergent complexity immediately, fixing problems as a shared responsibility before formal documentation follows.
  • Predictable behaviour—one party acting in good faith even when it costs them—creates psychological safety for reciprocal action across the team.
  • Paperwork and change control become a record of a decision already made to protect the project, not a gatekeeper preventing action.
  • Claims-focused culture creates delay and fragmentation; collaborative response protects both timeline and relationships.
