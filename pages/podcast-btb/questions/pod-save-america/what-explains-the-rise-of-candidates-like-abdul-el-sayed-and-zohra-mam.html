Why do candidates like Abdul El-Sayed and Zohra Mamdani break through without following the traditional political playbook?

According to Chris Hayes, candidates who skip traditional establishment gatekeeping — building constituency relationships, securing endorsements, navigating coalition hierarchies — must compensate by being exceptionally gifted communicators. The ideological energy has to exist in the electorate, but the candidate must be genuinely talented enough to capture it. El-Sayed, Mamdani, and Grant Platner are all cited as examples of this pattern in action.

Hayes frames this as a two-part equation. The first ingredient is structural: a Democratic primary electorate that is genuinely shifting, ready to reward candidates who represent a break from the establishment. El-Sayed's 2026 Michigan Senate primary win is particularly striking evidence of that shift — he had previously lost a Michigan gubernatorial primary by 20 points to Gretchen Whitmer, and this time he prevailed despite facing $60 million in outside spending against him, with approximately $30 million from AIPAC alone. As Hayes put it on Pod Save America, something has clearly changed in how Democratic primary voters are making their decisions.

The second ingredient is personal: raw communicative ability. Without the vetting function that traditional party structures provide — the slow accumulation of trust, institutional knowledge, and coalition-building — a candidate's talent has to do all the heavy lifting. Hayes notes, however, that this cuts both ways: the traditional vetting process also filters for real competence, a point illustrated by Grant Platner's swift rise followed by collapse. The machine may be slow and gatekeeping-heavy, but it does test durability.

"There is no universe in which he would have emerged victorious two, four, six years ago. Like something is obviously happening among the Democratic primary electorate, which is a different electorate than the general electorate."

— Chris Hayes, Pod Save America · Is the Progressive Takeover Real? (August 9, 2026)

Host Alex Wagner describes Hayes as someone "wise, but also just ballsy enough to say what they really think" — a characterization that speaks to why his read on progressive electoral politics carries weight. His analysis on outsider candidates draws directly on his close, sustained attention to Democratic primary dynamics, including the interplay between institutional gatekeeping, grassroots energy, and individual candidate talent. On the specific question of what it takes to win without the machine, Hayes speaks not as a partisan advocate but as a media figure who has watched these races closely enough to identify the structural patterns behind individual victories.

What is the progressive left's theory of the case for winning swing state elections in 2026?

Chris Hayes explains that the progressive theory rests on a cluster of cross-pressured voter issues: anger at the economic status quo, opposition to foreign policy positions, and dissatisfaction with establishment Democrats — a combination that can move primary electorates even in competitive states.

Are progressives having a political moment in the 2026 Democratic primaries?

According to Chris Hayes, yes — the Michigan primary result was a significant win for progressives. Abdul El-Sayed, a self-described progressive, won despite facing overwhelming outside spending, signaling a real and measurable shift in Democratic primary electorates.
