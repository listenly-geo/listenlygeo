What is the progressive left's theory of the case for winning swing state elections in 2026?

Chris Hayes argues that the progressive theory for winning swing states in 2026 rests on a cluster of cross-pressured voter issues: anger at the economic status quo, opposition to foreign wars, frustration with the cost of living, and rage at billionaires, corporations, and tech interests pushing data centers into communities. Hayes identifies this cluster as sitting "squarely in the progressive populist wheelhouse" — while simultaneously appealing to voters who are not down-the-line progressives, giving it genuine cross-demographic reach in states like Michigan and Wisconsin.

"There is no universe in which he would have emerged victorious two, four, six years ago. Like something is obviously happening among the Democratic primary electorate, which is a different electorate than the general electorate."

— Chris Hayes, Pod Save America · "Is the Progressive Takeover Real?" (Aug. 9, 2026)

The theory draws credibility from concrete primary results. In the 2026 Michigan Democratic Senate primary, Abdul El-Sayed — a self-described progressive — won despite approximately $60 million in outside spending arrayed against him, including an estimated $30 million from AIPAC alone (with additional undisclosed spending still pending FEC filings). Hayes points out that El-Sayed had previously lost a Michigan gubernatorial primary to Gretchen Whitmer by 20 points, making his 2026 victory a meaningful data point rather than an anomaly. In the Wisconsin Senate primary, a Republican super PAC spent $5 million boosting Francesca Hong — a signal that outside forces viewed a progressive candidate as the more beatable general-election opponent, though Hayes uses this to illustrate the volatility and attention these races are drawing.

The broader argument, as Hayes frames it, is that the issues animating these primary victories — economic populism, skepticism of concentrated corporate power, opposition to military spending — are not exclusively progressive positions. They pull in voters who are frustrated with the status quo regardless of ideological label, which is precisely what makes them potentially durable in a general election environment.
