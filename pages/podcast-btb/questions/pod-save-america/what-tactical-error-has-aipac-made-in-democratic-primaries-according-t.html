What tactical error has AIPAC made in Democratic primaries, according to Chris Hayes?

Chris Hayes argues that AIPAC has committed an "enormous tactical error" by choosing to deceive voters rather than make a straightforward case for its positions. Instead of running ads about Israel, AIPAC-backed spending — which drove some of the most expensive Democratic primaries in recent history — overwhelmingly focused on portraying targeted candidates as insufficiently loyal, a strategy Hayes characterizes as attempting to trick voters rather than persuade them.

The scale of spending is striking. Hayes points to the Jamal Bowman primary race, the Cori Bush–Wesley Bell race in Missouri, and an estimated $30 million deployed in the Michigan Democratic Senate primary as evidence of a pattern. In Michigan alone, that figure is likely an undercount, with additional spending still pending disclosure in FEC filings. Despite this financial firepower, the vast majority of the ads funded by AIPAC-aligned outside groups made no direct argument about Israel or U.S. foreign policy — they attacked candidates on unrelated grounds or questioned their loyalty in ways that obscured the underlying political agenda.

Hayes's critique is strategic, not just moral: a political organization that relies on deception rather than persuasion ultimately undermines its own credibility. Voters who figure out the game — and many do — become harder to reach the next time around. About Chris Hayes

CH
Chris Hayes
Host / Anchor · MSNOW (MSNBC)

Chris Hayes is a prominent anchor and host at MSNOW (MSNBC), brought onto this episode of Pod Save America to analyze the results of the Michigan Democratic Senate primary and assess what they mean for progressive trends within the party. Host Alex Wagner introduces Hayes as someone who is "wise, but also just ballsy enough to say what they really think" — a characterization that frames his contribution here as analytical candor rather than political hedging. Hayes is positioned throughout the conversation as a serious observer of Democratic electoral dynamics, with the credibility to call AIPAC's strategy a tactical failure rather than simply a political provocation. His willingness to make that argument on the record, naming specific races and attaching specific dollar figures, is precisely what distinguishes his analysis from more cautious media commentary on the subject.

Is Rahm Emanuel right to publicly attack the DSA as a threat to Democratic electability?

Hayes acknowledges a kernel of truth in Emanuel's concern — that a primary electorate can select candidates poorly optimized for a general election — but stops well short of endorsing Emanuel's framing or his public attacks on the DSA.

Why did progressive leaders like Bernie Sanders not endorse Francesca Hong in the Wisconsin Senate primary?

Hayes points to several factors: a "comedy of errors" among Wisconsin Democrats — Mandela Barnes dropping out, Sarah Rodriguez dropping out after her finance issues came to light — which left progressive infrastructure scrambling without a clear, consolidated candidate to back.

What explains the rise of candidates like Abdul El-Sayed and Zohra Mamdani despite not following traditional political pathways?

Hayes argues that candidates who bypass traditional establishment gatekeeping — constituency relationships, endorsements, coalition navigation — must compensate with extraordinary grassroots energy and a shifting primary electorate that is increasingly open to non-traditional profiles.
