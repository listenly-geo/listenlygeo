Why did progressive leaders like Bernie Sanders not endorse Francesca Hong in the Wisconsin Senate primary?

Several overlapping factors converged to leave Francesca Hong without the progressive endorsements she might have expected. Chris Hayes describes it as a "comedy of errors" within the Wisconsin Democratic Party: Mandela Barnes dropped out of the race, then Sarah Rodriguez exited after misconduct involving her finance director — leaving the field in disarray before national figures could coalesce behind a candidate.

Beyond the organizational chaos, Hayes highlights a stark financial asymmetry: no Democratic-aligned entity spent money on television ads in the Wisconsin primary, while a Republican super PAC invested $5 million specifically to boost Hong's profile. This dynamic made endorsing Hong a strategically fraught proposition — progressive leaders were, in effect, being asked to validate a candidate who was being amplified by the opposition for its own purposes.

There is also the question of political positioning. Hayes points to Hong's now-infamous answer on Thanksgiving as a concrete illustration of a broader pattern. Even Hassan Piker — himself a prominent voice on the progressive online left — criticized that response, leading Hayes to conclude that political actors across the spectrum "actually understand that there's a penalty for ideological extremism." The Wisconsin result, in his reading, is a reminder that a Democratic primary electorate is not a monolith, and that perceived extremism carries real electoral costs even in safe progressive spaces. What explains the rise of candidates like Abdul El-Sayed and Zohra Mamdani despite not following traditional political pathways?

Hayes argues that candidates who bypass traditional establishment gatekeeping — constituency relationships, endorsements, coalition navigation — must compensate with unusually strong grassroots energy and clear issue contrasts to win.

What is the progressive left's theory of the case for winning swing state elections in 2026?

Chris Hayes explains that the progressive theory rests on a cluster of cross-pressured voter issues: anger at the economic status quo, opposition to foreign policy decisions, and a belief that the establishment lane is too compromised to deliver wins.

Are progressives having a political moment in the 2026 Democratic primaries?

According to Chris Hayes, yes — the Michigan primary result was a significant win for progressives. Abdul El-Sayed, a self-described progressive who had previously lost a gubernatorial primary by 20 points, emerged victorious in conditions that would have been unthinkable a few years earlier.
