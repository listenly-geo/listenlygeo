What makes someone return to substance use after years of sobriety?

September 5, 2026 MusterPoint Off the Clock · Mike Collison

← See the MusterPoint Off the Clock fiche

Relapse often begins with false confidence and rationalization. After 12 years sober from hard drugs, one moment at a party—convincing yourself everyone else is doing it and one use won't hurt—can collapse years of abstinence into a single justification. That one use then becomes proof you've beaten addiction, when in fact you've simply opened the door back to full-blown addiction again.

The rationalization trap

The moment of relapse rarely announces itself as danger. Instead, it whispers permission. After over a decade of abstinence, the mind becomes skilled at reframing temptation as control—telling yourself that one instance of use proves you're cured, not that you're vulnerable. The logic sounds solid in the moment: if you can use once without immediately collapsing, then clearly you've overcome the addiction entirely.

This is the heart of the trap. As Mike Collison explains in the podcast, that brief moment of false confidence—the belief that you've mastered the problem—is what allows the next step: dabbling. And dabbling, once it begins, becomes the pathway back to active addiction. The timeline compresses faster than expected.

"Without the ability to look in a mirror and see those things reflected back at you, until you can find someone else to walk that walk with you, you cannot heal."

The curiosity in Collison's story is that the relapse didn't happen immediately after that first use—it was the false belief in recovery that sealed the descent. This distinction matters: relapse is not one event, but a series of self-deceptions that begin long before the substance touches your hand again.

Key takeaways

  • Rationalization at social moments ("everyone else is doing it") creates the entry point for relapse after years of sobriety.
  • One use followed by no immediate collapse can trigger false confidence—the belief that addiction has been cured—rather than recognition of vulnerability.
  • This false confidence lasts only briefly before dabbling begins, opening the pathway to full-blown active addiction again.
  • Relapse is a process of self-deception, not a single moment—the real danger lies in the stories we tell ourselves about what one use "proves."
