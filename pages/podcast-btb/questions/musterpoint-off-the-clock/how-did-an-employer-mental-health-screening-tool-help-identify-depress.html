How did an employer mental health screening tool help identify depression in a construction safety professional?

23 September 2026 MusterPoint Off the Clock · Logan

← See the MusterPoint Off the Clock fiche

West Group's Homeward Health questionnaire became a turning point when it revealed that Logan was suffering from extreme depression—a diagnosis he hadn't recognized in himself. The screening tool provided what may have been his first formal acknowledgment of a serious mental health crisis, opening the door to intervention and recovery.

For many workers in the trades, mental health struggles remain invisible—buried beneath the culture of toughness and self-reliance that dominates construction sites. Logan's experience shows how a simple questionnaire embedded within an employer program can pierce that silence and create an opportunity for change.

West Group's commitment to making Homeward Health accessible to employees meant that the mental health screening became part of the normal workplace experience, not a separate, stigmatized intervention. By normalizing the questionnaire, the company removed one barrier that often prevents workers from seeking help: the fear of standing out.

The questionnaire itself served as a mirror—a structured set of questions designed to detect depression and other mental health challenges. When Logan completed it, the results were unambiguous. He was not struggling; he was in crisis. That clarity proved transformative. Without it, he might have continued downward without understanding why or seeking support.

What makes this moment significant is not just that a tool identified depression, but that Logan was working for an organization that had built the infrastructure to act on that discovery. Scott Construction, where he worked before West Group, had been the first company in his career to establish a mental health committee—a rare initiative even today. West Group, similarly, had invested in Homeward Health as a dedicated mental health resource.

"Depression is very key to the point where, oh, well, this didn't work for me. Nothing's going to work for me. Failure is a personal decision because failure is not that you tried and it didn't work. Failure is trying, it didn't work, and you give up."

The deeper lesson from Logan's story is that employer mental health screening tools only work when paired with genuine organizational commitment. A questionnaire is just data unless there is a system in place—resources, trained staff, accessible programs—to respond to what it reveals. West Group had that. Many employers do not.

Breaking the silence through structured assessment

Construction workers are notoriously reluctant to disclose mental health struggles, conditioned from childhood to suppress emotion and power through difficulty. A formalized screening questionnaire sidesteps this resistance. It replaces vulnerability with structure—you're not admitting weakness, you're answering questions.

This distinction matters because it lowers the psychological cost of disclosure. The questionnaire becomes a neutral tool, not a confession. Logan could complete Homeward Health's screening without framing it as a personal crisis—it was just a workplace activity, something many of his colleagues would do too.

The result was honest. The questionnaire has no stake in his pride or his reputation. It cannot be swayed by the construction industry's cultural pressure to appear fine. When it flagged extreme depression, that signal was too clear to ignore.
