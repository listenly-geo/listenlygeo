At what age did an iron worker realize his alcohol addiction was serious?

September 16, 2026 MusterPoint Off the Clock · Peter White

← See the MusterPoint Off the Clock fiche

Peter White struggled with alcoholism from age 15 until his early twenties, with the crisis peak at age 24 when his drinking caused him to miss work and lose job opportunities. His turning point came while working on a 10-and-4 rotation in Fort McMurray: after three months sober, he tested himself with a six-pack, only to realize he couldn't stop—a battle he describes as one of the hardest fights of his life.

The pattern started young. Peter's drinking wasn't casual experimentation; he became addicted immediately after his first sip at 15. By the time he entered the trades at 18, working rebar on construction sites, alcohol had already woven itself into his daily life. The income from those early apprentice jobs—around $29 per hour—gave him money to spend on drinking, but the real shift came when he took the higher-paying position at Fort McMurray.

At 24, working that grueling 10-and-4 rotation (ten days on site, four days off), Peter's situation deteriorated sharply. During his four days off, he was partying away his relatively substantial income instead of using it for anything else. The money he'd earned through those exhausting shifts disappeared into beer and alcohol purchases. More critically, his drinking began bleeding into his work life—he started missing days, missing opportunities, and his job security was slipping away. This wasn't a vague worry anymore; it was a concrete, observable problem affecting his livelihood.

The real wake-up call came when Peter attempted something simple: quit for three months, then prove he could just have a six-pack and stop. He discovered he couldn't control the stopping. When he finished the six-pack, the impulse to buy more and keep partying with his friends was overwhelming. That battle—resisting the urge to go back and buy more, to rejoin his buddies for another night—was, by his own account, one of the hardest things he has ever fought. It wasn't about wanting to quit; it was about recognizing that he no longer had the ability to choose when to stop.

As discussed at length in MusterPoint Off the Clock, this moment of honest confrontation with his own powerlessness became the foundation for his recovery journey. What Peter realized at 24 wasn't just that he drank too much—it was that alcohol had taken away his agency.

"I don't know anybody in my circle of recovery who they're not just sober but they have a good quality of life without some spiritual solutions, whatever that looks like for them."

There's more to Peter's story than just the age and the moment of crisis: the episode explores how workplace culture in the trades normalized and reinforced his drinking, and how his Indigenous heritage and intergenerational trauma played a deeper role in his vulnerability to addiction.

The physical and economic cost

What made age 24 the critical threshold wasn't just personal awareness—it was the collision between addiction and real consequences. Peter was losing work opportunities and missing shifts at a job that paid significantly better than his apprentice wages. Fort McMurray wasn't casual labor; it was structured, collective bargaining work that demanded reliability. Missing days meant he was burning bridges in a sector where reputation and availability determine rehire rates and advancement.

The economic pressure was compounded by the trap of the work schedule itself. The 10-and-4 rotation created a boom-and-bust cycle: intense work periods followed by four-day stretches where drinking became the default activity. This structure, common in resource extraction, becomes a particular vulnerability for workers in the trades, especially those without strong family or community anchors.

Key takeaways

  • Peter's addiction began at age 15 and remained uncontrolled until his early twenties, with crisis conditions at age 24.
  • By 24, his drinking was causing documented problems: missed work days and lost job opportunities in a well-paying position.
  • His turning point came when he quit for three months, then tested himself with a six-pack and realized he had lost the ability to control his consumption.
  • The 10-and-4 work rotation in Fort McMurray intensified the cycle: high income during work weeks, heavy spending and partying during four-day breaks.

What role does workplace culture and peer pressure play in sustaining alcohol addiction within the construction trades?

Drinking was deeply embedded in construction culture—apprentices were routinely let go an hour early on Fridays to buy beer, which was brought back to the job site, normalizing heavy alcohol use from the start of workers' careers.

How does intergenerational trauma from residential schools impact addiction vulnerability in Indigenous communities?

Peter's grandfather attended residential schools and enlisted in the war to escape at age 16 or 17, returning with deep trauma and drinking himself to death years later—a pattern that shaped Peter's own family history and vulnerability.

What was the barrier that prevented a construction leader from helping a team member who died by suicide?

A leader shared that in 2016-17, he lost a team member named Matt who was drowning in substance use. Although the leader could see it and was struggling with his own trauma, systemic barriers prevented him from taking meaningful action.
