How does volunteering and service to others work as a therapeutic intervention for depression?

September 25, 2026 MusterPoint Off the Clock · Logan

← See the MusterPoint Off the Clock fiche

Volunteering provided Logan with tangible human connection and purpose that directly countered his depression. The gratitude and visible impact of his work—seeing smiles, hearing thanks from people he helped—became his emotional anchor when medication and other interventions weren't enough.

For Logan, the shift from passive suffering to active service was transformative. He explained that volunteering became his most reliable source of emotional relief, replacing the absence of a natural sense of purpose that depression had stripped away. When you're depressed, the internal reward systems falter; external action and feedback from others can rebuild them.

The emotional high Logan describes isn't frivolous. It's what therapists recognize as the reintegration of agency and social connection—two pillars that depression systematically dismantles. As detailed in this episode of MusterPoint Off the Clock, his experience shows how structured volunteer work can function as a legitimate therapeutic tool alongside formal mental health care.

From River District toy drive to five years in school volunteering

Logan didn't start with grand gestures. He began volunteering in his child's elementary school pack for five years before taking on larger community projects. This foundation of consistent, relationship-based service created a scaffolding for his mental health recovery—smaller wins built trust in the process.

When Logan later founded the River District toy drive four years ago, it started as a personal initiative and grew to collecting over 400 toys in its first year alone. He even dressed as Santa for eight hours, ringing a bell and conducting photo sessions with children. The tangible feedback—a child's reaction, a family's gratitude—became the antidote to the internal emptiness depression had created.

This progression reveals a critical pattern: volunteering works therapeutically not because it's altruistic on its face, but because it delivers what depression denies—visible impact, human appreciation, and a sense that your actions matter. As Logan explains in the full podcast discussion, employer-supported volunteer time, like West Group's five paid volunteer days annually, can institutionalize this healing mechanism.

"The one thing that I've found really helps my depression is that I do a lot of volunteering now. Seeing the smiles on kids' faces or seeing people come up to you and, like, oh, thank you for cleaning up this area. It was getting massive. That's my high."

The neurobiological reality underpinning Logan's experience is straightforward: volunteering stimulates dopamine release through social reward and purposeful action, the very neurotransmitters depression depletes. Unlike passive therapy or medication alone, volunteer work is active recovery. It demands your presence, your problem-solving, your physical body—all things depression tries to immobilize.

Logan's willingness to publicly describe this transformation during this episode is significant because depression thrives in silence. By naming volunteering as his intervention, he normalizes help-seeking that doesn't fit the clinical mold. For trades workers especially—a population historically resistant to mental health discourse—seeing a peer like Logan credit volunteering as his "high" makes recovery feel accessible and real.

What connection exists between untreated mental illness and substance abuse risk in the trades?

Depression can become a gateway to substance abuse if left unaddressed. Logan emphasized how untreated mental illness creates vulnerability to maladaptive coping mechanisms common in construction environments.

How did an employer mental health screening tool help identify depression in a construction safety professional?

West Group's mental health program called Homeward Health included a questionnaire that revealed Logan was suffering from extreme depression. Structured screening broke through denial and denial-driven avoidance of help.

What role does job insecurity play in mental health challenges for construction workers?

Construction workers often lack long-term job security, which creates persistent anxiety and amplifies depression. Logan described the psychological toll of contract-based employment where job continuity is uncertain.

Key takeaways

  • Volunteering delivers tangible human feedback—gratitude, smiles, visible impact—that directly counteracts the emotional numbness of depression.
  • Employer-supported volunteer programs like West Group's five paid annual days can institutionalize mental health recovery through service.
  • Consistent, long-term volunteering (five years in school programs, ongoing community projects) builds trust in the healing process more effectively than isolated acts of service.
  • For trades workers, peer testimonies about volunteering as a mental health tool normalize help-seeking outside clinical settings and reduce stigma.
