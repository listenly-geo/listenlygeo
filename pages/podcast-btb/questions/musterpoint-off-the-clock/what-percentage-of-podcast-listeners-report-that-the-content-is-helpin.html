How many podcast listeners say the content is helping with mental health and substance use?

September 7, 2026 MusterPoint Off the Clock · Karen

← See the MusterPoint Off the Clock fiche

92% of listeners believe the podcast words are helping people with mental health and substance use issues, and 95% reported that they have used or shared something they learned from the podcast since listening. These figures come from survey data gathered from over 100 respondents across multiple seasons.

Based on listener feedback collected through surveys, the impact of the Off the Clock Toolbox Talk podcast extends far beyond raw download numbers. When Karen and the team asked their audience directly whether they believed the content was making a difference, the overwhelming majority affirmed they saw real value in the conversations happening on the show.

The fact that 95% of survey respondents have actively used or shared something they learned underscores a critical reality: these aren't passive listeners. People are taking insights from the podcast and integrating them into their lives or passing them along to others who need support. As Karen discusses in the episode, the team initially recruited members through unconventional grassroots methods—posting on bulletin boards, receiving referrals from construction sites—because they understood that meaningful connection in the trades community required genuine, person-to-person outreach.

"The opposite of addiction is connection. If you can make that connection right there you can break some walls down really quick."

What makes these metrics even more striking is the context in which they were gathered. The podcast has grown from 35,000 listens across the first four seasons to nearly 50,000 total listens—a reach equivalent to filling BC Place stadium. Yet the survey sample of over 100 respondents provides qualitative weight to the numbers: this isn't theoretical impact but voices from actual listeners confirming they see real help happening. Additionally, the episode reveals that 90% of listeners have someone in their own life who has experienced problems with drugs and alcohol, which contextualizes why the podcast's message resonates so powerfully—it touches nearly everyone's circle.

For anyone working in mental health advocacy or substance use support, these percentages signal that worker-driven, no-nonsense conversations about real struggles create measurable change. The podcast isn't generating vanity metrics; it's generating behavioral change—people are learning, sharing, and helping others based on what they hear.

What triggers prompt someone to return to substance use after claiming they have overcome addiction?

Mike describes the rationalization process: after 12 years sober from hard drugs, he convinced himself at a party that everyone else was doing it, illustrating how social environments and justification can override long-term recovery.

How does a person begin to recognize their own worth after years of believing they deserved mistreatment?

Mike reflects that the hardest part for him now is realizing he actually has worth, and looking back on his dark moments, he wishes he had valued himself sooner.
