What percentage of your gross income should go to housing costs?

September 3, 2026 Your Next Dollar: Money Management for High Earners · Ryan

← See the Your Next Dollar: Money Management for High Earners fiche

Housing costs should represent less than 30% of your gross income. Keeping this ratio in check opens up capacity for savings, investments, and other spending priorities without creating financial strain.

The 30% benchmark exists because housing has a compounding effect on your entire budget. When housing costs creep above this threshold, they squeeze your flexibility elsewhere—reducing what you can save for retirement, invest, or allocate to emergency funds.

As covered in the episode on tracking six financial numbers, this metric works in tandem with other expense ratios. The goal is not perfection but awareness—knowing where you stand allows you to make deliberate choices about where your money flows.

Why housing deserves a ceiling, not just a guideline

Housing differs from other expenses because it's typically your largest fixed cost. Unlike discretionary spending, you cannot easily adjust it month-to-month. Rising housing costs create structural financial strain that bleeds into every other category of your budget.

For high-income earners, the temptation to stretch on housing is especially real—bigger home, prestigious neighborhood, premium finishes. But the 30% rule keeps even high earners honest. A six-figure income does not exempt you from this discipline; it simply means the dollar threshold is higher.

The reason this matters, as discussed with financial advisor Ryan, is that housing should never become the primary driver of your net worth composition. A home builds equity, but trapped home equity cannot fund retirement or weather unexpected expenses.

"You are your own business owner, even if you are a W-2 employee in that you are selling your time and your energy and your expertise and you should sell it to the highest bidder."

This philosophy extends to housing decisions. Earning more does not justify overspending on shelter; it justifies building a more robust financial foundation elsewhere. The episode explores how essential baseline expenses—including housing—should ideally stay at 50 to 60% or less of total spending, creating room for both lifestyle and wealth accumulation.

How should someone think about depreciating assets like cars when calculating net worth?

For people just getting started, include depreciating assets like cars at their Kelly Blue Book value to build momentum and have a complete picture of your financial standing.

How frequently should someone track their net worth?

Track net worth no more than quarterly and no less than annually. Checking it monthly is overkill and creates a roller coaster effect that undermines sound financial decision-making.

What is the millennial trap and how does it relate to net worth composition?

The millennial trap occurs when people are millionaires on paper due to home value and 401k retirement accounts but have no liquidity outside of these assets, limiting financial flexibility.

Key takeaways

  • Keep housing costs below 30% of gross income to preserve flexibility for savings, investments, and other priorities.
  • Housing is a fixed expense with compounding effects across your entire budget—oversizing it creates structural financial strain.
  • Home equity is valuable for net worth but should not be your only wealth-building vehicle; liquidity matters as much as appreciation.
  • High income does not justify stretching on housing; it simply raises the dollar ceiling while the percentage discipline remains unchanged.
