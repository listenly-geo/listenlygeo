How can AI act as a 'wingman' for internal communications professionals working alone on large change programmes?

For internal communications professionals who work in isolation — no peer to review a draft, no colleague to stress-test an idea — AI can fill the role of a thinking partner. Ana-Maria Thijssen frames AI not as a tool that does her job for her, but as a virtual colleague: somewhere to bounce ideas, check for best practice, and avoid the loneliness that comes with being the only communicator on a large, complex programme.

"AI became my virtual colleague. It's not a person that does my job. It's more like an entity that helps me bounce ideas and not feel so alone."

— Ana-Maria Thijssen, Change Communications Lead

Thijssen's situation makes this need concrete. She is the sole communications professional embedded within an Organizational Change Management (OCM) team running a global, multi-wave SAP IT transformation programme. She joined around eighteen months after the programme had already launched — meaning no formal induction, no handover, and no inherited communications playbook. Without a sounding partner, every message, every stakeholder communication, and every change narrative had to be written and self-reviewed in isolation. AI changed that dynamic. Rather than simply using it to generate content, Thijssen uses it the way a communicator might use a trusted colleague — to think out loud, validate an approach before committing to it, and catch gaps in her reasoning before they reach an audience of thousands.

This framing matters because it reframes what AI actually delivers in an IC context. The value is not just efficiency — it is the emotional relief of not being entirely alone in the work. For change communications professionals navigating large-scale, multi-location programmes like the one Thijssen describes (which will ultimately reach 6,000 users across dozens of sites worldwide), that psychological dimension is rarely discussed but consistently felt. Hear the full conversation on Listenly to understand how Thijssen integrates AI into her daily workflow in practice.

Context The Organizational Change Management (OCM) model referenced here — including frameworks such as ADKAR — structures how people-side change is managed during large IT or business transformations. On programmes of this scale (multi-wave, multi-country SAP rollouts), the OCM team is typically responsible for readiness, training, and communications. When a single communicator covers the entire comms function within such a team, the absence of a peer reviewer is a genuine structural gap — not just a personal preference.
