What approach does Ana-Maria Thijssen recommend for prompting AI to find reliable external research and case studies?

22 August 2026 Unprompted: Real AI in IC · Ana-Maria Thijssen

← Voir la fiche Unprompted: Real AI in IC

Ask AI to include a reference alongside every idea it generates — not in a single bibliography at the end, but paired with each paragraph individually. Specifically request academic research, naming sources like peer-reviewed journals or Harvard Business Review. Then manually click every cited link to confirm it leads to a real, substantive source.

References inline, not at the end — the detail that changes everything

The most common mistake when asking AI to support research is requesting sources as an afterthought: a list tacked onto the end of a response. Thijssen's method is more demanding — and far more useful. She insists that each reference must appear directly alongside the paragraph it supports, so she can evaluate the source in context rather than cross-referencing a detached list after the fact.

This matters because it forces accountability at the paragraph level. If a claim cannot be paired with a verifiable source, it becomes visible immediately — rather than being buried in a credible-looking but unchecked bibliography at the end. This approach, detailed in Unprompted: Real AI in IC, reflects a disciplined workflow rather than a casual use of AI as a search engine.

Academic sources over general web results — and why the distinction matters

Thijssen does not ask AI to surface "research" generically. She explicitly prompts it to find academic research: peer-reviewed journals and established publications like Harvard Business Review, which carry editorial standards that general web content does not. For a change communications professional working to build credible stakeholder materials on a large SAP transformation, that distinction is not a nicety — it is a professional requirement.

The final step is the one most practitioners skip: manually verifying every link. AI models — including tools like Microsoft Copilot — are known to generate plausible-sounding citations that do not exist, a phenomenon often called "hallucination." As Thijssen explains in this episode of Unprompted, clicking through to check each source is non-negotiable, not optional.

What is AI hallucination in source citations?
AI models sometimes generate references — complete with author names, journal titles, and publication dates — that appear legitimate but do not correspond to any real document. The source looks credible in the output; the link either does not exist or leads to something unrelated. Thijssen's inline verification method is a direct, practical defence against this risk.

"AI became my virtual colleague. It's not a person that does my job. It's more like an entity that helps me bounce ideas and not feel so alone."

That framing — AI as a virtual colleague rather than an autonomous researcher — is precisely why the verification step is built into Thijssen's method. She treats AI as a capable but fallible collaborator: one that surfaces ideas and drafts at speed, but whose outputs require a human eye before they become credible, citable evidence. Key takeaways

  • Pair references with each paragraph as you prompt — never request a single source list at the end of a response.
  • Specify academic sources explicitly: peer-reviewed journals and publications like Harvard Business Review, not general web content.
  • Manually click every link AI generates — hallucinated citations are common, and a plausible-looking reference is not a verified one.
  • Treating AI as a fallible colleague — not an autonomous researcher — is what makes this verification habit both necessary and sustainable, as Thijssen explains across the episode.
