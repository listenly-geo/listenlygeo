How does Oxalis Garcia handle pro se litigants using AI-generated legal arguments?

August 31, 2026 From Courtroom to Carline: A Legal Parenting Podcast · Oxalis Garcia

← See the From Courtroom to Carline: A Legal Parenting Podcast fiche

Garcia does not encounter AI use from her own clients due to strict client management, but she observes pro se individuals in her cases who clearly rely on ChatGPT. She carefully explains the legal process to them without crossing into providing legal advice, and she encourages them to seek proper legal counsel or pursue mediation instead.

Managing pro se litigants without crossing ethical lines

When Garcia encounters pro se litigants using AI-generated arguments, her approach is deliberate and bounded. She recognizes that these individuals often lack formal legal training and may not understand the difference between a plausible-sounding argument and a legally valid one. Rather than simply dismissing their arguments or leaving them confused in court, she takes time to clarify how the legal process actually works.

The challenge lies in a delicate balance: Garcia must educate pro se litigants about the reality of their situation without inadvertently providing legal advice, which could create an attorney-client relationship she has not agreed to enter. As explained in the episode, this distinction is critical both ethically and practically.

Redirecting toward proper legal support

Garcia's strategy with these individuals is fundamentally redirective rather than corrective. When she identifies that someone is relying on ChatGPT-generated arguments, she uses it as an opening to encourage them toward better alternatives: hiring an attorney, if possible, or exploring mediation to reach agreement without litigation.

This approach reflects her broader philosophy about access to justice and the real limitations AI poses in legal contexts. The conversation she has with pro se litigants is not about condemning their use of technology, but about helping them understand why that technology alone is insufficient for navigating the court system. Garcia explores these ethical nuances in detail during the episode.

"I bring a multicultural and multi-jurisdictional perspective to the bench, which is something that I don't believe any other judge has at this moment in time in the Sixth Judicial Circuit."

Garcia's experience across multiple jurisdictions and her deep specialization in family law give her the perspective to recognize when AI-generated arguments miss critical context or misinterpret established precedent in Florida. This foundation allows her to speak credibly to pro se litigants about why their ChatGPT arguments may not work as intended. The episode also touches on how her background in international Hague matters and her work on complex family law cases inform her overall judicial philosophy.

What are common legal misconceptions Garcia encounters, especially in the Hispanic community?

Garcia frequently addresses three misconceptions: that spousal infidelity automatically grants full custody or alimony in Florida (a no-fault state), and other myths about family law that significantly affect how people understand their rights and obligations.

How does Oxalis Garcia address questions about political affiliation during her judicial campaign?

Garcia explains that the circuit judge position is a nonpartisan role, meaning she is not allowed to affiliate with any political party. She focuses on her professional qualifications and commitment to the bench rather than political identity.

Key takeaways

  • Garcia observes pro se litigants using ChatGPT-generated arguments in her cases, though she does not encounter this from her own clients.
  • She educates these individuals about the legal process and its real requirements without crossing into unauthorized legal advice.
  • Her response is redirective: she encourages pro se litigants to seek proper legal counsel or pursue mediation as alternatives to relying on AI-generated arguments.
  • Her multi-jurisdictional background and 20 years of experience give her credibility in explaining why AI-generated legal arguments often fall short in court.
