How has the Facebook group for college recruiting guidance grown and what value does it provide to families?

September 23, 2026 From Courtroom to Carline: A Legal Parenting Podcast · Renee Lopez

← See the From Courtroom to Carline: A Legal Parenting Podcast fiche

The Facebook group 'Educating Parents of High School Athletes on the College Recruiting Process' has grown to over 260,000 members largely through word of mouth, as existing members invite others into the community. The group provides free, practical guidance that helps families understand they do not need expensive recruiting services and teaches them to navigate the college athletic recruitment journey themselves.

What started as COVID-era webinars evolved into one of the largest support communities for families entering the college recruiting landscape. The growth was organic—members shared the resource with their networks, creating a snowball effect of parents looking for trustworthy guidance to help their student-athletes through a complex process.

The real value lies in demystifying an industry often clouded by expensive consultants and recruiting services. As Renee Lopez explains in the episode, the group teaches families the steps, timelines, and strategies that college coaches actually look for—without the premium price tag.

How the Group Empowers Families to Take Control

The Facebook group has helped hundreds of student-athletes commit to college programs while keeping education, career goals, social fit, and mental health at the center of the decision. Members gain access to resources built on real coaching experience and insights gathered from interviews with 65 college coaches and athletic directors across 19 different sports.

Beyond the Facebook community, a detailed point has been made in this podcast episode about the specific timeline families should follow—when to start reaching out to coaches, how to structure communications, and what metrics colleges actually monitor when evaluating recruits.

"The NCAA changes the rules more than we change our socks, but the idea is we want to make sure that families are keeping up to date in the process."

A fascinating detail worth exploring further: the episode dives deeper into the specific lifestyle demands of college coaching—an 80-hour-per-week reality that shaped Lopez's transition from the sideline to building resources for families who need guidance without the barrier of cost.

Key takeaways

  • The Facebook group grew to over 260,000 members primarily through organic word-of-mouth referrals from existing community members.
  • The group provides free resources that help families avoid expensive recruiting services by teaching them the process themselves.
  • Hundreds of student-athletes have successfully committed to college programs through guidance emphasizing education, career goals, social fit, and mental health.
  • The resource draws from interviews with 65 college coaches and athletic directors across 19 different sports and all NCAA divisions.

What inspired Renee Lopez to transition from college coaching to building resources for the college recruiting process?

After coaching for 14 years—a lifestyle career requiring about 80 hours per week—Lopez felt called in a different direction. She began doing webinars during COVID that evolved into a broader mission to democratize access to college recruiting guidance.

How should families approach the decision of whether their child should specialize in one sport or play multiple sports during high school?

Multiple college coaches prioritize rest and recovery as the number one concern. While some athletes can play multiple sports, the key is managing schedules to prevent burnout and maintain the academic performance that college recruiters monitor closely.

What are the key misconceptions athletes and families have about the college recruiting process?

A common misconception is that you must know somebody to be recruited. In reality, student-athletes can reach out directly to coaches and build genuine relationships through consistent communication, academic achievement, and athletic performance on the field.
