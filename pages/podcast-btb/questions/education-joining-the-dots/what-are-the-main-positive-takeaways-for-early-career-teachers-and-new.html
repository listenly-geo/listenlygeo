What gives new teachers and leaders hope after their first year?

21 September 2026 Education: Joining the Dots

← See the Education: Joining the Dots fiche

The first year brings real wins: one early career teacher taught over 800 students, creating tangible impact across the school. A new school leader discovered that her small, tightly-knit team was the greatest asset—colleagues pulling together, each bringing equal strength and expertise she could lean on.

The scale of impact often surprises new teachers. Having taught over 800 students in a single year is not just a number—it represents hundreds of young people who received direct instruction, feedback, and support at a formative moment in their education. That reach, spread across a whole school or year group, becomes a powerful reminder of why educators choose the profession in the first place.

For new school leaders, the realization is equally profound: you cannot carry the weight alone, and you shouldn't. At a small school of just 85 children, the headteacher discovered that every member of staff—regardless of experience level—brought equally valid strengths and expertise. That shift in mindset—from believing you must have all the answers to trusting the collective capability of your team—transforms the first year from exhaustion into partnership.

"I had to really remember that I can't do it all. And actually, people that are around me have amazing strengths and I need to lean on them."

This is not a soft skill or a nice-to-have insight. The TES School Wellbeing Report 2024 shows that 61% of nearly 7,000 school staff surveyed reported workload is not manageable, with middle leaders reporting 71% experiencing unsustainable demands. Early career teachers and new leaders who build collaboration into their first year model a different path—one where strength comes from shared responsibility, not individual heroism.

The episode also explores practical strategies for managing that transition, including how to communicate your needs to colleagues and create a culture where seeking support is the norm, not the exception.

Student impact and professional identity

When a first-year teacher reflects on having reached 800 students, something shifts. The job becomes less about survival and more about purpose. Each lesson, each piece of feedback, each moment of connection has been genuinely felt by a real young person. Over a full year, that compounds into a visible legacy—students who improved, who felt seen, who gained confidence or skill because of this teacher's deliberate effort.

This awareness of scale and impact is a critical anchor for retention and resilience. In a profession where workload complaints dominate the conversation, centering the positive outcome—the actual students touched—recalibrates motivation for years to come.

Building strength through delegation, not isolation

The headteacher's insight reveals a practical shift in how new leaders can approach their role. Instead of framing delegation as admitting defeat, it becomes strategic use of the team's collective intelligence. A school of 85 children is intimate enough that every staff member's contribution is visible and valued.

This translates to lower burnout, better decision-making, and—paradoxically—more effective leadership. When a headteacher trusts her team and acts on that trust visibly, staff retention improves and morale rises. It's a virtuous circle that begins with the willingness to name what you cannot do alone.

Key takeaways

  • One early career teacher reached over 800 students in their first year, creating measurable, sustained impact across the school.
  • New school leaders gain resilience by recognizing that colleagues possess equally valid expertise and strengths—delegation is strategic, not a failure.
  • Building a collaborative culture from day one reduces unsustainable workload pressures that affect 61% of UK school staff.
  • Centering student impact and team partnership transforms the first year from survival mode into professional foundation-building.
