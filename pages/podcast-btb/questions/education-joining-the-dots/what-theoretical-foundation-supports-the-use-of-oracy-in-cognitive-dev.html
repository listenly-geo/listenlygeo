What theoretical foundation supports the use of oracy in cognitive development?

5 September 2026 Education: Joining the Dots · Rachel Simons

← See the Education: Joining the Dots fiche

Vygotsky's theories establish that communication tools are essential to cognitive development because they fundamentally shape how individuals understand and interact with the world. Group activities and peer interactions enable learners to share ideas and perspectives, which enhances cognitive development and allows pupils to articulate their feelings and make sense of their learning.

Vygotsky's framework rests on the idea that language and social interaction are not peripheral to learning—they are central mechanisms through which thinking develops. When children engage in dialogue with peers, they don't simply exchange information; they actively construct understanding. Talk becomes a scaffold for thought itself.

This theoretical grounding explains why schools like those in Cabot Learning Federation have made oracy a strategic priority, as discussed in this episode of Education: Joining the Dots. When learners participate in structured group conversations, they develop not just communication skills, but deeper cognitive capacities across all subjects.

The connection between talk and academic progress becomes clearer through this lens. Peer interactions allow pupils to externalize thinking—to say aloud what they're struggling with, what they've understood, what they're curious about. This verbal articulation, far from being merely social, is cognitive work. It's how young people consolidate ideas and build mental models of the world around them.

"If young people struggle to communicate effectively, many will communicate in other ways, often through challenging behaviour."

When communication breaks down—when one in five children struggle with talking and understanding words, as reported by Speech and Language UK—cognitive development itself stalls. The theoretical foundation Vygotsky provided shows us that this isn't a separate "speech therapy" issue; it's a direct barrier to learning, well-being, and behaviour. The data bears this out: schools engaged with oracy development show correlations with improved attendance and stronger extended writing.

Rachel Simons and her team have observed how implementing oracy strategies across their trust transforms what happens in classrooms. When teachers create space for purposeful talk—when they treat group discussion and peer dialogue as non-negotiable learning time—they're operationalizing Vygotsky's insight: cognition grows through language in social contexts.

From theory to practice: Why peer interaction matters

Vygotsky's concept of the "zone of proximal development"—the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with support—depends entirely on interaction with more knowledgeable others. Group activities close this gap not through direct instruction alone, but through dialogue, questioning, and collaborative problem-solving.

This explains why schools tackling the speech and language crisis documented in the Lost for Words report—which found up to 40% of students have speech and language issues, worsening over five years—recognize that the answer isn't just remedial work. It's creating classrooms where talk is valued, where peer conversation becomes a deliberate learning tool, as educators like Simons have championed. When pupils articulate their thinking aloud, they internalize it. Thought becomes clearer. Understanding deepens.

Key takeaways

  • Vygotsky's theories position communication and language as essential drivers of cognitive development, not optional extras.
  • Group activities and peer interactions allow learners to externalize thinking, test ideas, and consolidate understanding through dialogue.
  • When children struggle to communicate, their cognitive development is directly impacted, affecting learning, behaviour, and well-being.
  • Creating purposeful talk and peer interaction spaces in classrooms operationalizes Vygotsky's theory and closes the zone of proximal development.

How does oracy development support other areas of literacy like writing and reading?

Being able to talk through ideas before putting pen to paper is incredibly useful. Staff at secondary schools report that oracy strategies supported students to complete higher quality extended pieces of writing.

How have teachers' initial reservations about oracy development changed over time in the trust?

There were initial teacher reservations when first starting to develop oracy in schools. As teachers learned and developed knowledge around oracy and speech and language, perspectives shifted and support grew.

What do pupils think about increased time for talk in schools, and what does pupil voice reveal?

In a recent survey at one secondary school, results show a huge increase in students who value talking with classmates as a way of learning. Pupil voice demonstrates strong engagement with oracy-focused practice.
