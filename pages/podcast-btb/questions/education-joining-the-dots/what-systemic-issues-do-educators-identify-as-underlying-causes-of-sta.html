What systemic barriers are driving staff wellbeing crises in schools?

24 September 2026 Education: Joining the Dots

← See the Education: Joining the Dots fiche

Schools are trapped in a perfect storm of underfunding, unchecked SEND demand, chronic staff shortages, and unmanageable workload — problems that stretch far beyond individual resilience and demand systemic intervention. External pressures from Ofsted compounds these challenges, leaving educators battling structural failures rather than isolated difficulties.

The TES School Wellbeing Report 2024 surveyed nearly 7,000 school staff and uncovered the depth of these systemic issues. Educators didn't point fingers at their colleagues or local circumstances alone; they identified budget constraints as a foundational problem that ripples through every classroom and staffroom.

Rising SEND (Special Educational Needs and Disabilities) referrals add another layer of pressure. Schools are struggling to meet these needs without corresponding increases in resources or specialist staff, forcing existing teams to stretch themselves thinner. As discussed in the episode, one headteacher named the rising SEND population as a critical concern — a problem they can identify but cannot solve alone.

Workload and the impossible mathematics of modern schooling

The numbers make the reality stark: 61% of respondents reported their workload is not manageable. That percentage climbs dramatically for specialist roles — 78% of SENCOs and 71% of middle leaders say they cannot sustainably manage their load. These are not anecdotal complaints; they reflect a structural imbalance between demand and capacity.

This isn't simply about long hours. Teachers describe being asked to do more with less: larger classes, fewer support staff, rising pastoral needs, and compliance demands that seem to multiply each term. The workload issue doesn't sit in isolation — it feeds directly into whether educators consider leaving the profession entirely.

Ofsted and external accountability as amplifiers

Beyond budgets and staffing, Ofsted inspection pressure adds a layer of external stress that schools cannot control but must navigate constantly. The pursuit of inspection readiness can distort school priorities and add administrative burden on top of existing teaching demands. Educators in this podcast identified this as a specific pressure point — not something they created, but something they must endure.

The widening gap for disadvantaged children represents a final systemic failure. Schools serving communities with higher deprivation face compounded needs — more SEND, more trauma, more poverty-related challenges — yet paradoxically receive fewer resources. This inequality isn't a school management problem; it's a policy problem.

"I had to really remember that I can't do it all. And actually, people that are around me have amazing strengths and I need to lean on them."

The core insight here is crucial: educators speaking in this episode were clear that wellbeing challenges are not personal failings to be fixed with mindfulness apps. They are systemic failures requiring policy-level change: proper funding, adequate staffing, realistic workload, and support mechanisms that match the actual complexity of modern schools.

How manageable is workload for different roles in schools according to recent survey data?

The TES School Wellbeing Report 2024 found that 61% of nearly 7,000 respondents said their workload is not manageable. When drilling down by role, 78% of SENCOs and 71% of middle leaders reported unmanageable workload.

What percentage of school staff have contemplated leaving the teaching profession?

According to the TES School Wellbeing Report 2024 UK, 74% of staff who participated had contemplated leaving the profession entirely in the past year.

What are the main positive takeaways for early career teachers and new school leaders completing their first year?

Early career teachers and school leaders highlighted real impact: one ECT taught over 800 students in his first year, while headteachers spoke of working with amazing teams and remembering their core mission to support the children they serve.
