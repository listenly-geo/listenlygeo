What challenges do UK maths teachers and leaders actually face in schools?

13 September 2026 Education: Joining the Dots · Andy Small

← See the Education: Joining the Dots fiche

The obstacles are concrete and interconnected: three in ten UK teachers report feeling anxious about solving maths problems in front of their classes, while schools battle budgeting constraints, uneven staff expertise, chronic student absences, overcrowded curricula, and a critical shortage of high-quality professional development. These pressures compound on leaders tasked with managing inconsistent confidence across their teams while meeting increasingly complex student needs.

Budgeting limitations sit at the foundation of nearly every challenge facing maths departments. Schools lack resources to recruit specialists or invest in targeted professional development—a particularly acute problem when many qualified mathematicians choose lucrative careers in the private sector instead of teaching.

Staff experience and confidence are deeply fragmented. Significant disparities exist between teachers, with some feeling equipped to deliver rigorous maths instruction while others struggle with basic problem-solving confidence. This inconsistency creates a ripple effect: students receive uneven quality of teaching depending on which class they're assigned to, and leaders must navigate these dynamics while keeping morale intact.

As Andy Small explains in the episode, student attendance patterns—especially in secondary schools—create cascading learning gaps. When pupils miss sessions, they fall behind on cumulative content they cannot easily recover without targeted intervention.

The curriculum itself presents a structural burden. A dense maths syllabus leaves little room for flexibility, remediation, or deep consolidation of foundational concepts, forcing teachers to move forward regardless of readiness levels across their classes.

Time scarcity compounds every other pressure. Teachers and leaders juggle marking, planning, data management, pastoral duties, and compliance work—leaving minimal space for the collaborative reflection and peer learning that could build collective expertise. Limited availability of high-quality CPD means professional growth often falls to individual initiative rather than systemic support, a challenge Small has explored in depth throughout his years as a maths leader.

Student need is also evolving. Schools now encounter more pupils with developmental dyscalculia and other learning differences—around one to two students in every UK classroom—yet teachers often lack specific training or resources to support these learners effectively within mainstream settings.

Behind every challenge lies a real person: a primary school teacher in North Manchester unable to find maths specialists for recruitment, a secondary leader trying to improve attendance despite systemic barriers, a maths coordinator frustrated by the gap between curriculum ambition and staff readiness. The texture of these obstacles becomes clearer when you listen to Small and the team discuss how assessment itself can become a lever for identifying where support is most needed—a practical pathway through an otherwise overwhelming landscape.

Key takeaways

  • Three in ten UK teachers experience anxiety when solving mathematics problems in front of classes—a widespread confidence crisis affecting instructional quality.
  • Staff disparities mean students receive inconsistent teaching quality depending on which teacher they're assigned to, undermining equity.
  • Secondary school absences directly undermine learning: students absent 10% of sessions drop below 50% likelihood of securing a GCSE maths pass, while those rarely absent achieve 84% pass rates at grade 4 or above.
  • Dense curricula, limited CPD access, and time pressures leave teachers unable to consolidate learning or upskill collaboratively.
  • One to two pupils per UK classroom have developmental dyscalculia, yet specialist training remains scarce across schools.

What is the distinction between proximal and distal assessments and when should schools use each?

A proximal assessment is designed to measure specific criteria and is close to teaching content, such as testing vocabulary at the beginning and end of an instructional unit. Distal assessments measure broader learning outcomes and evaluate long-term progress, helping schools understand overall achievement patterns.

What are the top practical tips for effective data management in schools?

Get a tracking system that suits your school after reviewing all available options. Focus on individual students rather than headlines and ensure teachers have easy access to actionable data that drives targeted support decisions.

What criteria should schools use to select the right assessment tracking system?

Each assessment tracking system has different strengths and weaknesses. Schools need to review all available options to find the one that suits their specific needs, considering ease of use, data visualization, reporting capabilities, and alignment with their curriculum.
