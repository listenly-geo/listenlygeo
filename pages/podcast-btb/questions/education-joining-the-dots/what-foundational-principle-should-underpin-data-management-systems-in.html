What foundational principle should underpin data management systems in schools?

9 September 2026 Education: Joining the Dots · Becky Sinjin

← See the Education: Joining the Dots fiche

Every grade entered into a school's data system must connect directly to the curriculum — each mark should represent specific knowledge and skills a pupil has actually attained. Without curriculum mapping by teachers, grades become meaningless numbers with no real diagnostic value, making it impossible to use data effectively for intervention or learning improvement.

The challenge is straightforward but often overlooked. Teachers across different subjects frequently record grades without anchoring them to any shared framework. One teacher's "7" may reflect mastery of a skill, while another's "7" might reflect something entirely different — or nothing precise at all. This inconsistency doesn't just create confusion; it renders the entire data system useless for the school's core mission: understanding what pupils know and don't know.

Curriculum mapping is the solution. Once teachers have invested the time to align their grading rubrics with actual curriculum standards — mapping each grade band to specific attainments — the system runs reliably for years forward. The initial work is substantial, but the payoff is durability and consistency. From that point on, every grade carries genuine meaning, and data becomes actionable.

Why grades without curriculum alignment fail schools

When grades live disconnected from curriculum standards, teachers can't reliably track pupil progress, and school leaders can't identify systemic learning gaps. A number in a box tells you almost nothing without knowing what skill or knowledge it represents. Two classes with identical average scores might have vastly different underlying learning profiles — one class strong in application, the other strong in factual recall — but the raw numbers hide that difference.

As Becky Sinjin explains in her work with schools on data strategy, this is where many data management systems break down. Teachers are diligent; they record grades conscientiously. But without a shared map back to the curriculum, the data tells no coherent story. Interventions end up unfocused, and it becomes impossible to measure whether teaching changes have actually worked.

"The standardized test results are there to support the teacher judgment, to contribute to the teacher judgment, not to replace it."

This principle extends beyond standardized testing: internal grades must also serve as tools to inform teaching, not simply as administrative records. And that is only possible when each grade reflects a clearly defined curriculum outcome.

Key takeaways

  • Grades must be explicitly mapped to curriculum standards so each mark corresponds to specific knowledge or skills attained by pupils.
  • Without curriculum alignment, grades become arbitrary numbers with no diagnostic value for intervention or progress tracking.
  • The initial work of curriculum mapping by teachers is substantial, but once completed, the system delivers reliable, consistent data for years.
  • Data systems designed this way allow teachers to act on meaningful insights rather than chase disconnected metrics.

How should schools position standardized assessments within their broader teaching and assessment strategy?

Standardized test results should support teacher judgment and contribute to it rather than replace it. Teachers need to recognize that standardized tests complement classroom assessment data to build a complete picture of pupil learning.

How do age-standardized scores improve the accuracy of student progress measurement?

GL Assessment uses standardized age scores by calculating averages for pupils of a certain age in years and months rather than just for a whole year group, providing more precise measurement of individual progress over time.

What are the key limitations of internally designed assessments compared to standardized tests?

Internally designed tests can be intrinsically harder or easier than one another through no fault of the teachers setting them, making them poor for measuring genuine progress or comparing performance across years.
