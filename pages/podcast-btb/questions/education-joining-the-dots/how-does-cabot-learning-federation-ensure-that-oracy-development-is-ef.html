How does Cabot Learning Federation embed oracy across all age groups?

30 August 2026 Education: Joining the Dots · Rachel Simons

← See the Education: Joining the Dots podcast fiche

Cabot Learning Federation ensures oracy development is effective across ages three to 19 through an intentional curriculum that extends beyond the national requirements, backed by consistent professional development for all adults. Every child has the right to literacy, and every adult carries the responsibility to teach it—oracy is woven through teaching and learning frameworks, given prominence in school improvement plans, and supported by a dedicated oracy lead in each setting who attends trust-wide network meetings to share research and best practices.

The approach is rooted in equity: literacy and oracy are not optional add-ons but core commitments embedded in everything the federation does. As explained in the podcast, this means moving beyond one-off interventions and instead creating a culture where quality teaching of oracy and literacy happens within every lesson, every subject, and every setting.

Professional development and shared practice across 35 settings

High-quality professional development is the backbone of Cabot Learning Federation's strategy. Every adult in the federation—teachers, teaching assistants, leaders—understands the why and the how of oracy and literacy teaching. This is not theoretical knowledge alone; it translates directly into classroom practice.

The trust-wide network meetings bring oracy leads together regularly to share research findings and proven practices. This creates a feedback loop: successes and innovations in one school quickly spread across all 35 settings, whether primary, secondary, post-16, alternative or specialist provision. The federation serves children from age two to 19, so the consistency of message and method across this enormous age span is critical. Rachel Simons details how this networking function ensures no setting is isolated in this work.

Oracy visibility in governance and improvement planning

For oracy to remain a priority, it must be visible in the federation's governance structures and school improvement plans. By giving oracy explicit prominence in these strategic documents, Cabot Learning Federation signals to every staff member and governor that this is not a peripheral initiative—it is central to the school's mission.

This structural embedding is reinforced by research: schools engaged with oracy work, such as those partnering with Voice 21, have shown higher average attendance rates compared to broader trust averages. Beyond attendance, secondary staff surveys reveal that 86% of staff believe oracy strategies supported students to complete higher-quality extended pieces of writing. These data points, discussed extensively in this episode, demonstrate that oracy is not a feel-good initiative—it directly improves learning outcomes.

"If young people struggle to communicate effectively, many will communicate in other ways, often through challenging behaviour."

Key takeaways

  • Oracy must be taught as a right, not an afterthought—every adult has a responsibility to embed it in all lessons and subjects.
  • Professional development and trust-wide network meetings ensure consistent, research-informed oracy teaching across all 35 settings, from early years through post-16.
  • Oracy leads in each setting provide local ownership and accountability, while remaining connected to federation-wide best practices.
  • Oracy visibility in improvement plans and governance structures ensures this work remains strategic and measurable, directly supporting literacy, attendance, and written output.
