How does the clearinghouse system work and what role does it play in stock trading?

September 25, 2026 Sorry, We're Closed with Pat Light · TiVo

← See the Sorry, We're Closed with Pat Light fiche

When you buy shares on Robinhood, Vanguard, or Goldman Sachs, you don't actually own them outright—brokerages operate on fractional reserve banking principles, lending out your shares to sell calls, puts, and other derivatives while a clearinghouse like Citadel maintains control over all transactions. Like a bank keeping only $10,000 in reserve on a $100,000 deposit and investing the remaining $90,000 five times over, brokers leverage your holdings across multiple financial instruments.

How fractional reserve trading works in practice

When you purchase shares through a major brokerage, the firm doesn't lock those shares in a vault with your name on them. Instead, the brokerage immediately lends out your shares to generate additional revenue, much like a traditional bank lends out depositor funds. These borrowed shares become collateral for selling covered calls, puts, and synthetic positions that other traders purchase.

This system mirrors banking's fractional reserve model precisely. As discussed on the podcast, brokerages maintain just enough capital to cover normal redemptions while deploying the majority of customer assets across derivatives and other investments. The more shares a brokerage can lend out simultaneously, the more profit it generates from transaction fees and premium collection.

The clearinghouse's role as the system controller

The clearinghouse acts as the intermediary and enforcer between brokerages, hedge funds, and individual traders. Citadel, one of the largest clearinghouses, maintains direct relationships with multiple brokers and has the authority to dictate settlement rules, margin requirements, and even halt trading when systemic risk rises.

This concentration of power means that individual brokerages like Robinhood cannot act independently—they must comply with clearinghouse rules, even if those rules restrict customer trading during volatile market events. The clearinghouse determines which trades settle, how much collateral is required, and ultimately whether a broker remains solvent.

Fractional Reserve Banking (in trading)

A system where financial institutions hold only a fraction of customer assets as liquid reserves while deploying the majority in investments, loans, or derivatives. In stock trading, this means brokerages lend out customer shares to third parties—generating revenue from interest and fees—while maintaining only enough capital to cover typical redemptions. Unlike bank deposits insured by the FDIC, securities are held in "street name" (the brokerage's name), giving the firm legal ownership rights to lend them out.
