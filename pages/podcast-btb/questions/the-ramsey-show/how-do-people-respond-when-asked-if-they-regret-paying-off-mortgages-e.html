Why do people report no regrets about paying off mortgages early?

September 16, 2026 The Ramsey Show · Graham Stephan

← See the The Ramsey Show fiche

When Graham Stephan surveyed Twitter users asking if anyone regretted paying off their mortgage early—even with favorable low interest rates—the response was overwhelming: approximately 98% reported no regrets whatsoever. While respondents acknowledged they could have mathematically earned more money investing in the markets instead, they universally agreed that the emotional and psychological relief of owning a debt-free home outweighed any potential financial gains.

The disconnect between math and emotion reveals something fundamental about how people experience financial decisions. Most survey respondents recognized the mathematical case: with mortgage rates between 2.8% and 3.3%, the average market return historically outpaces that borrowing cost. Yet the feeling of mortgage freedom proved stronger than the spreadsheet logic.

This pattern isn't random. As Graham Stephan explains in his appearance on The Ramsey Show, the psychological shift that happens when a mortgage disappears transforms how people perceive their financial stability. It's not about being irrational—it's about recognizing that peace of mind carries genuine value, even if it's not captured on a balance sheet.

"Even though everything was on auto payment, I never accounted for the fact that every single mortgage became its own mini ecosystem of thinking."

What Stephan captures here is the hidden cost of leverage: each mortgage, no matter how favorable the rate, demands ongoing mental and administrative attention. Even on autopilot, the presence of multiple mortgages creates what he calls "mini ecosystems"—separate domains of financial management that require monitoring, refinancing decisions, and psychological bandwidth. The episode delves deeper into how this cognitive load affects decision-making across an entire financial portfolio.

The survey data also hints at an important life-stage insight: people who actually take the step to pay off a mortgage early tend to be those already aligned with financial discipline and goal-oriented thinking. Discussed at length in this episode, this self-selection effect means the 98% figure likely represents people who made an intentional choice and subsequently experienced tangible life improvements—not just financial, but psychological and relational as well.

The broader implication is that optimal financial strategy is rarely 100% algorithmic. The human dimension—reducing anxiety, simplifying decision-making, strengthening household unity around shared financial goals—often justifies choices that pure mathematical optimization would reject. The Twitter survey, while anecdotal, captures something real: for the vast majority of people who commit to early mortgage payoff, the trade-off feels unambiguously correct.

The Math vs. The Feeling Gap

The core tension here is well-known in behavioral finance: mathematically, keeping a 2.8% mortgage while investing surplus cash in equity markets (historically returning 7–10% annually) makes numerical sense. Yet 98% of survey respondents said they'd make the same choice again. This suggests the intangible benefits—peace of mind, reduced administrative complexity, stronger household cohesion—genuinely move the needle in real-world decision-making.

Most respondents weren't claiming to be financially unsophisticated. Rather, they'd explicitly weighed both options and consciously chosen the path that aligned with their broader life priorities. The full episode explores why this emotional calculus matters as much as the financial one when families make decisions about debt and wealth-building strategy.

Key takeaways

  • Graham Stephan's Twitter survey found 98% of people who paid off mortgages early reported zero regrets, regardless of interest rate.
  • Respondents acknowledged they could have earned higher returns mathematically by investing instead, yet the psychological relief of debt freedom outweighed potential gains.
  • Each mortgage creates a "mini ecosystem" of ongoing mental and administrative burden, even on autopilot, that carries a hidden cognitive cost.
  • For people who intentionally pursue early payoff, the non-financial benefits—reduced anxiety, simplified decision-making, household alignment—prove decisive in their satisfaction.
