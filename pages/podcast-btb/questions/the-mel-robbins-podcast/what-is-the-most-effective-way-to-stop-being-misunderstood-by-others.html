What is the most effective way to stop being misunderstood by others?

September 10, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Gary Vaynerchuk

← See The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

Stop trying to control how others perceive you—accept that someone's subjective opinion of you can never fully know who you actually are, not even the people closest to you. The real solution is realizing that if your spouse, mother, or coworker cannot completely understand you, then a stranger in the comments section never will either.

This insight reframes the entire problem of being misunderstood. Most people spend emotional energy fighting against inevitable misinterpretation, as if perfect clarity were achievable. It isn't. The gap between who you are and how others perceive you is not a flaw to fix—it's a fundamental truth of human interaction.

As explained in The Mel Robbins Podcast episode, the discomfort people feel when misunderstood usually stems from attaching their self-worth to others' opinions. Once you accept that no one will ever have complete access to your inner world, the pressure to defend your reputation or correct every misconception dissolves. You are free to act without seeking permission from an audience that will never fully grasp your motives anyway.

This perspective connects to what Gary Vaynerchuk calls the "Let-Them Theory"—a framework detailed in the podcast for releasing the need to control external narratives about yourself. If people misunderstand you, that's their incomplete data set, not your failure.

"People are obnoxiously more capable of changing than they realize. And it's actually shockingly simple and the hardest thing in the world."

The practical impact of this mindset shift is immediate. Instead of tailoring your behavior to accommodate others' limited understanding of you, you can focus on alignment with your own values. This isn't license to be reckless or dismissive—it's permission to stop seeking validation from people whose opinions are necessarily incomplete.

Key takeaways

  • No one—not even those closest to you—can fully understand who you are because they lack complete access to your internal experience.
  • Misunderstanding is not a problem to solve but an inevitable feature of how humans perceive each other.
  • Releasing the need to control others' opinions frees you to act with greater authenticity and less internal conflict.
  • The "Let-Them Theory" provides a framework for accepting that others' incomplete data about you is their limitation, not your responsibility to fix.
