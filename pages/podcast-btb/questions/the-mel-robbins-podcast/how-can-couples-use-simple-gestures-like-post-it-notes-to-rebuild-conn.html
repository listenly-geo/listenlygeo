How can couples use simple gestures like post-it notes to rebuild connection in a long-term relationship?

August 29, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Christopher Robbins

← See the full The Mel Robbins Podcast page

Christopher Robbins has spent years hiding handwritten post-it notes inside Mel Robbins' wallet, pill case, glasses case, and credit card holder — messages like "Me again, just thinking about how much I love you." Every time Mel finds one, she immediately feels like his favorite person. That single small gesture cuts through debt, family struggles, and years of accumulated distance — and pulls her back into the relationship.

"Me again" — The two words that reset 30 years of marriage

The notes are not grand declarations. They are quiet, repeated, physical reminders — tucked into the ordinary objects of daily life. Christopher Robbins did not write them once. He kept writing them, year after year, across three decades of marriage that included financial hardship, raising three children, and extended periods of real struggle.

Mel Robbins is explicit about the effect: the moment she finds one, she does not think about whatever problem is in front of her. The note resets her emotional state. It answers the question every partner in a long relationship asks without saying it aloud — Am I still the person you chose?

This is explored in depth in the episode "30 Years of Marriage" on The Mel Robbins Podcast, recorded to mark thirty years together. It is one of three concrete lessons the couple shares from their own experience — lessons they say no one taught them early enough.

Why a handwritten note lands differently than a text message

The physical object matters. A note hidden in a wallet or glasses case is discovered at an unexpected moment — not during a conversation the partner is bracing for, not on a screen they can scroll past. It appears in private, unannounced, at a random Tuesday moment when nothing special is happening.

That surprise element is part of what makes it work. Mel Robbins describes the feeling as immediate — not a gradual warmth but an instant reconnection. The note does not ask for anything. It does not respond to a conflict. It simply says: I thought of you, and I wanted you to know.

For couples navigating the kind of slow drift that builds over years — Mel estimates she exhibited dismissive behavior toward Christopher for roughly a decade — gestures like these operate outside the friction of day-to-day tension. They arrive before any conversation has a chance to go sideways. As Robbins explains in this episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast, the smallest acts of intentionality can do what hours of talking sometimes cannot.

"At the end of your life, if you could look at another person and say, you helped me become the most authentic version of myself, and you're still my favorite person, that's the greatest wedding toast you could ever have."

The quote above captures what Robbins believes a relationship is ultimately for — not stability or companionship alone, but mutual becoming. The post-it notes are one small, concrete expression of that belief, accumulated over thirty years. You can hear the full conversation in The Mel Robbins Podcast.

Key takeaways

  • The effect is immediate: Mel Robbins describes feeling like Christopher's favorite person the instant she finds a note, regardless of what else is going wrong.
  • The gesture works precisely because it asks for nothing in return — it is not a response to conflict, just a quiet, repeated signal of choice.
