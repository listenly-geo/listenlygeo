What is the surest indicator of an impending divorce, according to divorce attorney James Sexton?

August 28, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Christopher Robbins

← See the full The Mel Robbins Podcast page

Divorce attorney James Sexton says the surest indicator of an impending divorce is not what people say — it is a sound. Specifically, the dismissive "yeah, okay" tone one partner uses when the other is speaking, paired with eye-rolling, dismissive body language, and small but consistent physical and verbal disconnections. These subtle discourtesies, not dramatic blowups, are what Sexton identifies as the real early warning signal.

A tone of voice more telling than any argument

James Sexton, a divorce attorney with decades of experience, makes a counter-intuitive point: couples who fight loudly and dramatically are not necessarily the ones headed for divorce. What he watches for is quieter — a specific flatness, a kind of checked-out politeness that replaces genuine engagement.

The dismissive "yeah, okay" is not indifference. It is something more corrosive: it signals that one partner has stopped finding the other worth the energy of a real response. That small sound, repeated over months or years, represents a withdrawal of attention and respect — the very things that hold a relationship together.

This insight is explored directly in The Mel Robbins Podcast, during a conversation about what 30 years of marriage actually teaches you — and what no one warns you about early enough.

Mel Robbins admits she was that partner — for almost a decade

What makes this episode unusually candid is that Mel Robbins does not treat Sexton's warning as an abstraction. She acknowledges, on record, that she was the dismissive partner in her own marriage to Christopher Robbins — and estimates the pattern lasted roughly a decade.

Christopher Robbins, described as a stoic and inward processor, navigated extended periods of financial hardship and professional failure during their marriage. He pursued an MBA in Boston, launched a business that did not succeed, and went through stretches of unemployment. Through all of that, the disconnection Mel describes — the subtle body language, the verbal flatness — was present in their home.

That they found their way back is the subject of this episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast. But the fact that the warning signs Sexton describes were real and present in their marriage gives his framework a concrete weight that goes well beyond theory.

"At the end of your life, if you could look at another person and say, you helped me become the most authentic version of myself, and you're still my favorite person, that's the greatest wedding toast you could ever have."

The physical dimension matters too. Sexton's warning is not limited to tone of voice. He points to small physical disconnections — the absence of touch, a turned shoulder, a gaze that moves away — as part of the same pattern. Together, these micro-signals form a picture of a relationship where one or both partners have quietly disengaged.

Sexton's read is that by the time a couple sits across from a divorce attorney, the real break happened much earlier — in those small, repeated moments of dismissal that neither partner may have named or noticed at the time.
