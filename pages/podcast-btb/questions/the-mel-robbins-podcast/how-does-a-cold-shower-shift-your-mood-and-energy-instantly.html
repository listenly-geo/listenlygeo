How does a cold shower shift your mood and energy instantly?

Turning your shower to maximum cold at the end triggers a physiological adrenaline release — a natural, immediate energy boost that, according to neuroscientist Wendy Suzuki, lasts well through the rest of the morning. The shock of cold water is not a wellness gimmick: it directly activates your body's adrenaline system, and the colder the water, the stronger the effect.

What is an adrenaline boost?

Adrenaline (also called epinephrine) is a hormone and neurotransmitter released by the body in response to sudden physical stress or shock — such as cold-water exposure. Its release produces a rapid increase in alertness, heart rate, and perceived energy. Suzuki describes the cold-shower adrenaline release as "natural," distinguishing it from caffeine or stimulants.

Suzuki recommends a simple protocol: finish your regular hot shower, then turn the water to maximum cold and stay in for approximately 20 seconds. Mel Robbins, who tested this on the podcast, confirms that 20 seconds under cold water is enough to completely shift your body state and mood. The colder the setting, the more pronounced the adrenaline response — and Suzuki is explicit that this boost stays in the system long enough to noticeably elevate energy levels throughout the day, not just in the immediate moment.

This tool was shared as one of five science-backed mood-shifting strategies on an episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast that drew from over 500,000 listener responses asking for practical help. Suzuki's cold-shower recommendation sits within a broader framework of using small, accessible physical interventions to change mental state fast — the same thinking that drives her research on how movement affects the brain.

"Every single time you move your body, you are releasing literally a flood of neurochemicals in your brain. I'm not talking about run a marathon. I'm talking about even taking a walk, a 10-minute walk gets that flood of neurochemicals going."

— Wendy Suzuki, neuroscientist, New York University

About Wendy Suzuki

WS
Wendy Suzuki Neuroscientist and Dean of the College of Arts and Science New York University

Wendy Suzuki is a world-renowned neuroscientist and the Dean of the College of Arts and Science at New York University — one of the most prominent positions in academic science in the United States. Her research has focused on memory and brain plasticity: specifically, how the brain changes in response to behavior and experience, and what everyday habits can accelerate or damage that process. Suzuki is equally known for translating that lab research into practical guidance, working to identify the simple, low-barrier things people can do daily to improve focus, learning, memory, and overall brain power. More recently, she has been pioneering research on how physical movement — including brief, accessible forms like walking — directly improves brain health and cognitive function. Her presence on The Mel Robbins Podcast reflects her rare ability to make complex neuroscience immediately actionable, which is precisely what makes her cold-shower recommendation credible: it is grounded in a deep understanding of adrenaline, physiological stress responses, and the brain's neurochemical systems.

What are the negative drivers of brain plasticity that can shrink the brain?

Suzuki identifies chronic stress and chronic anxiety as the most common negative brain plasticity drivers. These conditions cause retraction in the brain regions responsible for memory and learning.

Why does exercise increase energy even when you feel tired?

Suzuki attributes the energy boost from exercise most likely to the flood of dopamine and serotonin released in the brain. She also notes that in groups studied, this effect was consistently reported even after short bouts of movement.

How does a 10-minute walk improve focus and attention?

Suzuki explains that improved focus is the most commonly reported positive effect from studies of exercise on the brain. A single 10-minute walk is enough to trigger a full flood of neurochemicals that sharpen attention.
