How do you have an effective conversation with someone about a difficult relationship dynamic?

September 24, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Mel Robbins

← See The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

Start by naming exactly what happened, share the story you're telling yourself about it, and state clearly what you need from the other person. Then stop talking and listen to what they're willing to do — and let their answer determine what support you can actually offer.

The first step is radical honesty about the situation itself. Don't soften it, don't make excuses, and don't assume their intentions. Simply say: "This is what happened. This is the story I'm telling myself about it. This is what I need from you." That clarity removes the fog and gives the other person a real target to respond to.

What happens next is where most conversations derail. You must give the other person space to answer. Stop talking. Listen to what they actually say they're willing to do—not what you wish they'd do, not what they should do, but what they genuinely commit to. This is the moment you learn whether they're willing to meet you halfway or not.

This approach, as Mel Robbins explores in depth on her podcast, shifts the entire dynamic. Instead of trying to convince someone to change, you're simply presenting the facts and your emotional truth, then observing their response. That response becomes your guide.

"If you change nothing, nothing changes. But if you change one thing, everything could change."

Once you understand what the other person is actually willing to do, adjust your support accordingly. If they're not willing to do anything, you've learned that too—and that information is just as valuable. It tells you what boundaries you need to set and what you can realistically expect from this relationship going forward.

The power of this framework is that it removes the burden of changing the other person from your shoulders. You've done your part: you've spoken clearly, shared your emotional truth, and given them a chance to respond. What they choose to do is theirs to own. The full episode goes deeper into how this framework applies to family members, partners, and friends—with real examples of how people have used it to transform relationships that felt stuck.

Key takeaways

  • Name the situation objectively: "This is what happened, this is my interpretation, this is what I need."
  • Stop talking after you've stated your need—silence gives the other person room to respond honestly.
  • Listen to what they actually say they're willing to do, not what you hope they'll do.
  • Let their response determine the level of support you offer going forward.

What is the 'me, we, and them' framework for handling difficult relationships?

The framework consists of three parts: 'me' means creating space for yourself and focusing on what is going well and what support you need; 'we' means having honest conversations about shared concerns; 'them' means accepting what you cannot control about the other person.

How should you approach a situation where a family member refuses to take care of their health despite your efforts to support them?

You must recognize what is in your control and what is not. Your job is not to make your mother healthy; instead, you should have an honest conversation about your concerns and what support you can genuinely offer.

What specific recommendation does research suggest for protecting young people from social media harm?

Dr. Haidt's research group states very clearly in The Anxious Generation: no social media till 16. This recommendation stems from research showing significant mental health risks for younger users.
