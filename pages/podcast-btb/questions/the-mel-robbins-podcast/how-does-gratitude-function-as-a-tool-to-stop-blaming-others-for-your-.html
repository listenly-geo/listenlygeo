How does gratitude function as a tool to stop blaming others for your circumstances?

September 15, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Gary Vaynerchuk

← See the The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

Gratitude flips the blame dynamic because acknowledging what you already have forces you to recognize your privilege. If you can listen to a podcast on a phone worth several hundred dollars, you're living better than most of the world — including the 800 million people who cannot access clean water. Comparing yourself to three people younger than you with trillionaires' wealth keeps you stuck in envy; comparing yourself to your grandmother's life, or to global poverty, makes you understand you're already winning.

The shift is immediate: gratitude is a choice to measure your life against reality, not against Instagram. As Gary Vaynerchuk explains in this episode, you cannot blame your circumstances or other people if you're holding a device that connects you to all of humanity's knowledge. The blame collapses the moment you stop comparing upward and start being honest about where you actually stand in the world.

"People are obnoxiously more capable of changing than they realize. And it's actually shockingly simple and the hardest thing in the world."

Gratitude is not positive thinking or denial—it's a factual inventory. Most of the planet is suffering from scarcity; you likely have shelter, food, and opportunity. A point discussed at length in the podcast is that the moment you're grateful for what exists, blame loses its grip. You can't simultaneously claim you have nothing and acknowledge you have a phone worth hundreds of dollars—the cognitive dissonance breaks the victim narrative.

This doesn't mean ignoring real obstacles. It means placing them in proportion. If you're angry at your competitor's success instead of grateful you have a business, you're wasting energy. If you're resentful of your family's past mistakes instead of grateful you survived them, you're building on quicksand. Gratitude reorients your emotional lens from scarcity to agency—and agency is where accountability lives.
