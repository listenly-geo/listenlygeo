Why are internet platforms particularly dangerous for young people interacting with strangers and anonymous adults?

September 20, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Jonathan Haidt

← See the The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

Platforms like Instagram and Roblox allow children to interact with anonymous adults wearing masks with no accountability, creating a dangerous void where predatory behavior goes unpoliced. Research shows that 15 percent of teens aged 14-16 experience inappropriate approaches from strangers every single week.

The anonymity problem: adults hiding behind digital masks

The core vulnerability lies in the absence of real-world friction. On traditional platforms, a child recognizes their classmate, teacher, or neighbor—the social cost of bad behavior is immediate. On Instagram and Roblox, an adult can adopt any persona, speak to minors freely, and vanish without consequence.

As Jonathan Haidt explains in the episode, this design flaw is not accidental—it is baked into the platform's architecture. The affordances that make these services fun for children (instant messaging, avatar customization, minimal verification) are the exact same features that enable predatory contact at scale.

The statistical reality: one in seven teens, every week

Research conducted by Arturo Bejar, a Facebook researcher, reveals the staggering frequency of this harm. Among teens aged 14 to 16, 15 percent report inappropriate approaches from strangers on a weekly basis. That is not a yearly statistic—it is a weekly recurrence for millions of young people.

The problem is compounded by the sheer volume of users. With billions of children on these platforms and only algorithmic moderation, the probability of a minor encountering a bad actor approaches certainty over time.

Beyond stranger danger: peer-to-peer sexual degradation

The threat is not only external. Boys in schools have begun using nude pictures of female classmates as a trading commodity—bartering images for beer, money, or status. This represents a systemic breakdown in peer accountability and consent.

What makes this especially urgent is that it happens both on and off platform, but the digital infrastructure—ease of sharing, permanence, amplification through screenshots—makes the harm exponentially worse. What are the different ways social media and quick dopamine impact boys versus girls during adolescence?

For girls, the primary harm comes from constant social comparison, anxiety about likes and comments, and sexualization through get-ready-with-me videos. Boys experience a different crisis altogether.

Why do slot machine mechanics explain the addictive design of social media platforms?

Companies like Facebook use an advertising-based business model where they make money from showing ads. The more time you spend on their platform, the more ads you see and the more revenue they generate.

How did social media platforms transform from social networking systems to addictive engagement machines?

Between 2008-2010, platforms introduced the App Store, push notifications, share buttons, retweet buttons, and newsfeeds. Instead of checking a friend's profile manually, users now receive constant notifications pulling them back in.
