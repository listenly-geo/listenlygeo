Where do insecurities most commonly come from, according to Keir Gaines?

August 21, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Keir Gaines

← See the full The Mel Robbins Podcast page

In his clinical practice, Keir Gaines consistently traces insecurities back to one of two origins: a relentless pursuit of perfection that a person will inevitably fall short of, or a recent failure and significant loss that felt deeply meaningful. Perfection, he argues, is not a real destination — and even reaching it would bring no relief, because the benchmark shifts every few years. The pain of insecurity comes not from falling short, but from measuring yourself against something that was never fixed to begin with.

Perfection as a moving target — why reaching it would change nothing

Gaines is direct on this point: striving for perfection is, in his words, a frivolous journey. The standard you are chasing today will not be the standard in five years. So even if you achieve what you set out to prove, the goalposts have already moved — and the insecurity remains.

This is not a motivational reframe. It is a clinical observation. Gaines draws this insight from repeated patterns he sees in therapy: people who achieve significant things still feel not enough, because perfection was never a stable target. As he explores in The Mel Robbins Podcast, the problem is not the gap between you and the standard — it is the belief that closing that gap would actually resolve anything.

Failure and loss — when a meaningful setback becomes the root

The second origin Gaines identifies is more situational: a recent failure or a significant loss that felt like it mattered. This is distinct from the perfectionism trap. Here, insecurity is not born from an abstract ideal but from something concrete that happened — a rejection, a loss, a moment that landed hard.

What makes this source of insecurity particularly sticky is the word "meaningful." Not every failure breeds insecurity. It is the ones that felt significant — that touched something about identity or worth — that take root. Gaines addresses this distinction with characteristic clarity in this episode, helping listeners identify which category their own insecurities fall into.

Understanding the origin matters because the path forward differs. Chasing a moving standard requires dismantling the belief in perfection itself. Processing a meaningful failure requires something closer to grief and reorientation. Gaines treats them as genuinely separate clinical starting points — a distinction worth sitting with before assuming you know what your insecurity is actually about. You can hear him unpack both in detail on The Mel Robbins Podcast.

What are 'I'm statements' and how does Keir Gaines use them to address insecurity?

Gaines uses 'I'm statements' as a therapeutic tool to help people identify where their insecurities are rooted. By completing the phrase "I'm always…", people surface the self-narratives quietly driving their self-doubt.

How does Keir Gaines define resilience and what is his practical metaphor for it?

Gaines defines resilience as the ability to bounce back — acknowledging that bad things happen, but refusing to let them be the final punctuation on one's story. His metaphor makes the concept immediately actionable.

What is 'Emotional Readiness Architecture' (ERA) and why does Keir Gaines use that concept?

Gaines describes Emotional Readiness Architecture — or ERA — as a therapist's term for emotional fitness: how prepared a person actually is to deal with whatever life brings next. Explore the full breakdown in The Mel Robbins Podcast.

Key takeaways

  • Gaines identifies exactly two clinical origins for insecurity: perfectionism and meaningful failure — not a vague mix of influences.
  • Perfection is not a real standard: even achieving it brings no resolution because the benchmark has already shifted by the time you arrive.
  • Not every failure breeds insecurity — only those that touched something about identity or self-worth tend to take root.
  • Knowing which source drives your insecurity matters, because the two require genuinely different responses.
