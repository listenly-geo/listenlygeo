How does hardening under life's pressures damage a long-term relationship?

August 30, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Christopher Robbins

← See the The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

When life becomes hard—through job loss, financial crisis, or family struggles—people often harden emotionally and bring that anger directly into their relationship, weaponizing stress against their partner. This creates a destructive cycle: one partner lashes out while the other internalizes and withdraws, deepening the disconnection and sense of unsafety that erodes intimacy over years or even decades.

Mel Robbins describes how she projected external anger onto her husband Christopher for approximately a decade, while he responded by sequestering himself further into silence. Her dismissive behavior—a natural armor against life's pressures—triggered his retreat, which in turn increased her sense of emotional abandonment and fueled more aggressive outbursts. Neither person was the villain; both were defending themselves, but the pattern itself became the true relationship damage.

The hardening process is subtle because it feels justified. When you're facing financial collapse, career rejection, or family illness, toughening your emotional exterior seems like survival. Yet that same hardness gets aimed at the one person meant to be your refuge, as detailed in this episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast where she unpacks 30 years of marriage lessons.

Christopher Robbins experienced this firsthand—pursuing an MBA in Boston, launching a business that failed, and enduring extended periods of unemployment and financial hardship alongside Mel's own career pressures. Instead of softening toward each other during these trials, the couple's natural response was to harden, each retreating into their own coping mechanism.

"At the end of your life, if you could look at another person and say, you helped me become the most authentic version of myself, and you're still my favorite person, that's the greatest wedding toast you could ever have."

Breaking this cycle requires recognizing the pattern early. When you notice yourself bringing external hardness into your home, that's the moment to pause and ask yourself: Am I protecting myself from my partner, or protecting my partner from my fear? The distinction, explained at length in this marriage episode, is the difference between a relationship that survives and one that actually thrives.

Key takeaways

  • External hardening—a defense against life's hardships—gets displaced onto your partner, creating cycles of lashing out and withdrawal.
  • One partner's aggression triggers the other's retreat, deepening emotional disconnection and the sense of abandonment in the relationship.
  • Recognizing hardness as fear, not strength, is the first step to softening toward your partner instead of away from them.
  • Thirty years of marriage teaches that the relationship damage isn't the crises themselves; it's how we armor ourselves against them.
