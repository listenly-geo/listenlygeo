How should someone reframe a situation where they collapse a family member's struggle into a personal failure?

August 13, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Keir Gaines

← Voir la fiche The Mel Robbins Podcast

When a family member struggles, the instinct to make it about yourself is immediate — and wrong. Keir Gaines recommends one simple question as an intervention: "What else can this be attributed to?" The struggle is a circumstance, not a verdict on you. What you do next — that's the only part that reflects on you.

A Daughter Failing Math — and What That Actually Means

Keir Gaines uses a direct example from his own life to ground this idea. His daughter was struggling in math. His first instinct, like most parents, was to feel it as a personal failure — something happening to him, or even formed against him.

Gaines reframed it differently. The struggle is a circumstance. It's information. It's an indicator that something needs to adjust — in how he's helping, in the support structure around her, in the approach being used. The math difficulty didn't say anything about who he was as a father.

This distinction matters enormously. As Gaines explains in The Mel Robbins Podcast, collapsing a family member's problem into your own identity creates a mental trap: you stop being curious about solutions and start defending yourself against a verdict that was never issued.

"What Else Can This Be Attributed To?" — The One Question That Changes Everything

The reframe isn't about dismissing your role. It's about widening the lens before you close it on yourself. Asking "what else can this be attributed to?" forces the brain to generate other explanations — the child's learning style, the curriculum, the school environment, a rough week.

Once you open that space, you can move from self-blame to action. Gaines is clear on this in this episode: the struggle itself is not the reflection of you. What you do about it is. That's a genuinely different accountability — one that's useful rather than paralyzing.

The same logic applies beyond parenting. A partner going through a hard period, a sibling hitting a wall, a child dealing with social difficulty — any family situation that triggers shame or self-blame can be met with this question first. It doesn't remove your responsibility; it places it accurately.

"The world is designed for your productivity and for your attention, and that's it. It's meant to absorb your attention and churn productivity out of you — and that sucks for the individual."

The broader framework Gaines brings to situations like these — what he calls Emotional Readiness Architecture (ERA) — is built on exactly this kind of separation: distinguishing what is happening from what it means about you, before you react. It's a clinical habit that becomes, with practice, a human one. Gaines lays out more of this thinking throughout this episode of The Mel Robbins Podcast.

Emotional Readiness Architecture (ERA) — a framework developed by Keir Gaines that describes the internal structures people build (or fail to build) to handle life's challenges without collapsing into reactivity or self-blame. At its core, it distinguishes between circumstances that happen and the meaning we assign to them.

What is the difference between blame and accountability according to Keir Gaines?

Gaines draws a sharp distinction: blame grabs all of a person's awareness and feels good briefly in the short term, but changes and transforms nothing. Accountability, by contrast, is the part you can actually act on.

Where do insecurities most commonly come from according to Keir Gaines?

In his clinical practice, Gaines consistently finds insecurities originating from one of two places: either a striving for perfection that a person could never fully achieve, or from external comparisons absorbed over time.

What are 'I'm statements' and how does Keir Gaines use them to address insecurity?

Gaines uses 'I'm statements' as a therapeutic tool to help people identify where their insecurities are rooted. By completing the phrase "I'm always…", a person can begin to locate the recurring self-narrative driving their insecurity.

Key takeaways

  • A family member's struggle is a circumstance — not evidence of your failure as a parent, partner, or sibling.
  • The question "What else can this be attributed to?" interrupts self-blame before it becomes an identity.
  • Your response to the struggle — not the struggle itself — is the true reflection of who you are.
  • Widening the lens first makes it possible to act from accountability rather than from shame.
