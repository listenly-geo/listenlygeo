How long do you actually need to stay under cold water to get the adrenaline benefit from a cold shower?

You don't need to endure minutes of cold water — the initial shock is the whole point. According to Dr. Wendy Suzuki, the moment you feel that first jolt of cold, your body has already triggered the adrenaline response. You can step out, dry off, and the boost will carry you through the rest of the morning.

Suzuki is clear that duration is not the goal, especially at first. Even a very brief exposure is sufficient to activate the neurochemical reaction. As your body adapts over time, longer exposure may come naturally — but it is never a prerequisite. Mel Robbins, who put this into practice herself, reports that roughly 20 seconds under cold water is all she personally needs to completely shift her body state and mood. That 20-second threshold, according to Robbins, is enough to flip the switch.

This insight emerged from an episode dedicated to five science-backed tools for shifting your mood fast — a response Mel Robbins built after over 500,000 people replied to a single Instagram story asking what they needed help with. Each tool in that episode was grounded in real research, and the cold shower technique was one of the most immediately actionable. You can hear Suzuki explain the full mechanism on The Mel Robbins Podcast on Listenly.

"Every single time you move your body, you are releasing literally a flood of neurochemicals in your brain. I'm not talking about run a marathon. I'm talking about even taking a walk — a 10-minute walk gets that flood of neurochemicals going."

— Wendy Suzuki, Neuroscientist, New York University

About Wendy Suzuki

WS
Wendy Suzuki Neuroscientist and Dean of the College of Arts and Science New York University

Dr. Wendy Suzuki is a world-renowned neuroscientist and the Dean of the College of Arts and Science at New York University — one of the most prestigious research universities in the United States. Her scientific reputation rests on decades of groundbreaking research into memory formation, learning, and brain plasticity: how the brain physically changes and reorganizes itself in response to experience. What distinguishes Suzuki from many of her peers is her focus on translating complex neuroscience into practical, everyday tools accessible to anyone.

She is particularly known for her research into the connection between physical movement and brain health — specifically how exercise triggers the release of neurochemicals including dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, endorphins, and growth factors that directly improve focus, mood, and cognitive performance. This same body of research underpins her analysis of cold exposure: the cold shock response activates the same adrenaline pathway that exercise does, making it a fast-acting, low-effort alternative for moments when a workout isn't possible. Her work on the minimum effective dose of physical stimulus — such as noting that even a 10-minute walk is enough to trigger a full neurochemical flood — is directly relevant to her claim that even a few seconds of cold water are sufficient to produce a real adrenaline boost.

How does a cold shower shift your mood and energy instantly?

Dr. Wendy Suzuki recommends ending every hot shower by turning the water to maximum cold, describing it as a natural adrenaline boost. The shock of cold water triggers an immediate neurochemical response that shifts mood and energy.

What are the negative drivers of brain plasticity that can shrink the brain?

Dr. Suzuki identifies chronic stress and chronic anxiety as the most common negative brain plasticity drivers. These conditions cause retraction in the prefrontal cortex and other key brain regions associated with decision-making and emotional regulation.

Why does exercise increase energy even when you feel tired?

Dr. Suzuki attributes the energy boost from exercise most likely to the flood of dopamine and serotonin released in the brain. She also notes that in groups, the social dimension amplifies the effect considerably.
