How should you approach a situation where a family member refuses to take care of their health?

September 22, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Mel Robbins

← See the The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

You cannot make someone else healthy—that's not within your control. Instead, focus on what you can control: having an honest conversation about how their choices impact you, offering realistic support you can actually give, and then letting them make their own decisions. When you change how you show up in the relationship, everything about the dynamic shifts, not because they changed, but because you did.

Stop trying to be the solution

The core mistake people make is believing their role is to fix the other person. Whether it's a mother who won't exercise, a son who isn't motivated, or a partner who ignores your requests, you've confused supporting someone with being responsible for their behavior. Those are two entirely different things.

When you let go of the outcome—when you stop needing them to change—you reclaim your own peace. This isn't indifference; it's clarity about where your actual power lies, as Mel Robbins explores in depth in the podcast.

The three-part framework

First, have a real, vulnerable conversation about how their health choices are affecting you—not about how they should change. This isn't nagging or convincing. You're naming your own experience: "Mom, when I see you struggling with your health, I feel worried, and that worry is affecting my peace."

Second, offer support that is realistic for you to give. Don't promise you'll help them exercise five days a week if you know you won't follow through. State clearly what you can actually do, and what you can't. Be honest about your limits.

Third, and most importantly, let them make their own choice. Once you've been honest and offered real support, their next move belongs to them. You don't get to control it, and you shouldn't try to.

"If you change nothing, nothing changes. But if you change one thing, everything could change."

The shift happens when you stop making their health your emotional responsibility. The podcast details real-world examples, including how to handle the guilt that often surfaces when you step back from the helper role.

Why your change matters more than their change

You might think: "But if I let go, won't things just get worse?" The opposite often happens. When you stop showing up as the worried fixer, the dynamic itself transforms. Your family member no longer has an audience for their resistance. There's no one to convince or push against.

More importantly, you model what healthy boundaries look like. You show them that loving someone doesn't mean sacrificing your own wellbeing. That's not selfish—that's mature.

For a deeper dive into how to handle these conversations without guilt or frustration, listen to the full episode, where Robbins walks through how to shift from "fixer" to "honest family member."

Key takeaways

  • You cannot control whether someone takes care of their health—you can only control your own choices and boundaries.
  • Have a direct conversation about how their choices impact you, not about what they should do.
  • Offer support that is realistic and sustainable for you to give; don't overcommit.
  • Accept their decision once you've been honest and offered real help—your job is done, and theirs begins.
