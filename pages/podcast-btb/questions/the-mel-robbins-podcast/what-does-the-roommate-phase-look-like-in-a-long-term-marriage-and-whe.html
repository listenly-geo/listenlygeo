What does the roommate phase look like in a long-term marriage and when does it typically set in?

September 6, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Christopher Robbins

← See The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

The roommate phase is when logistics and life administration completely consume the relationship, leaving emotional connection behind. Managing children, feeding pets, buying groceries, and showing up for holidays become the entire substance of a marriage — stripped of genuine intimacy, vulnerability, or meaningful interaction. For Mel and Christopher Robbins, this phase solidified once at least two of their three children had arrived, after which emotional distancing became deeply entrenched.

The roommate phase is not a sudden crisis. It creeps in quietly, disguised as adulthood. Couples wake up one morning to realize they are no longer partners in a relationship but co-managers of a household operation. They coordinate schedules, delegate tasks, and maintain the surface appearance of marriage while the emotional core has quietly been set aside.

Christopher Robbins describes how this phase took hold in his own 30-year marriage with increasing force as their family grew. As he explains in the episode, the tipping point came not gradually but at a specific inflection — when two or more children had been born. Once that threshold was crossed, emotional sequestering became the default mode of their interaction.

What makes the roommate phase so dangerous is how normalized it feels. Neither partner may consciously recognize it as a departure from genuine partnership. Conversations become transactional. Questions shift from "How are you feeling?" to "Did you pick up milk?" Affection is replaced by efficiency. The relationship becomes a well-oiled logistics machine that requires no emotional fuel to run.

The shift is compounded by what life actually demands during those years. Raising young children, managing finances, handling career pressures, and maintaining a household leave little mental or emotional space for vulnerability. This season of intensity is explored in depth in the full podcast episode, where Mel and Christopher reflect on how external pressures hardened their connection and made it easier to retreat into separate emotional rooms.

The entrenchment happens gradually, then all at once

Christopher Robbins notes that the roommate phase did not announce itself. It settled in like an unquestioned presence, becoming more firm and more normalized with each passing year. The more children arrived, the more entrenched the pattern became, until emotional disconnection felt like the natural state of marriage rather than a departure from it.

The danger lies in how this phase can feel productive. The household runs smoothly. Bills are paid. Children are fed and transported. By all external measures, the marriage is functioning. But functioning is not the same as living. The couple exists in parallel, not in connection. And without deliberate intervention, this dynamic can persist for years as the couple navigates separate emotional worlds while maintaining the appearance of partnership.

"At the end of your life, if you could look at another person and say, you helped me become the most authentic version of myself, and you're still my favorite person, that's the greatest wedding toast you could ever have."
