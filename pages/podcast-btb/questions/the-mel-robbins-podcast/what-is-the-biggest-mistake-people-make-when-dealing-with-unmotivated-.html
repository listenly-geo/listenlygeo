What is the biggest mistake people make when dealing with unmotivated or struggling family members?

September 25, 2026 The Mel Robbins Podcast · Mel Robbins

← See The Mel Robbins Podcast fiche

Expecting someone else to change is a giant mistake. Instead, focus 100% of your energy on the only part of the relationship dynamic you can truly control: yourself. When you shift your attitude, adjust your approach, or change the time and energy you invest, you create the conditions for everything else to transform.

You Cannot Will Someone Else to Change

The core issue is straightforward: you have no direct power over another person's motivation, choices, or behavior. Whether it's a son unmotivated in his job, a mother refusing to care for her health, or a partner leaving clutter everywhere, external pressure rarely works. What does work is shifting the dynamic itself—and that begins with you.

This is explored in depth in Mel Robbins' framework on handling difficult relationships, which emphasizes that the only sustainable change comes from changing your own role in the dynamic.

What Actually Changes When You Change

When you alter your attitude, your boundaries, or the way you show up in a relationship, you shift the entire pattern. This isn't about withdrawal or coldness—it's about becoming intentional. You stop trying to control the outcome and start controlling your own energy and response.

The paradox is simple: if you change nothing, nothing changes. But if you change one thing—your behavior, your words, your expectations—everything could change. This doesn't guarantee the other person will transform, but it removes the exhaustion of trying to fix someone who doesn't want to be fixed.

As discussed in the episode, this shift in focus restores your peace and reclaims your personal power in ways that nagging, guilt, or intervention never can.

"If you change nothing, nothing changes. But if you change one thing, everything could change."

What makes this insight powerful is that it removes the victim stance. You're no longer waiting for your family member to become responsible—you're taking responsibility for your own experience within the relationship. The specific strategies for communicating this shift are detailed in the full episode, including how to set boundaries without blame and how to create genuine conversation even when emotions run high.

Key takeaways

  • Stop expecting unmotivated or difficult family members to change—you cannot control their choices or motivation.
  • Focus all your energy on what you can control: your attitude, your approach, and the energy you invest in the relationship.
  • When you change your behavior or boundaries, you shift the entire dynamic and create conditions for real transformation.
  • This approach restores your peace and personal power far more effectively than guilt, pressure, or intervention.

How do you have an effective conversation with someone about a difficult relationship dynamic?

Start by saying, "This is what happened, this is the story I'm telling myself, and this is what I need from you." Share how the situation is affecting you and be direct about what needs to change.

What is the 'me, we, and them' framework for handling difficult relationships?

The framework consists of three parts: 'me' means creating space for yourself and focusing on what is going well and what support you need; 'we' means engaging in honest conversation with the other person; and 'them' means letting go of what you cannot control.

How should you approach a situation where a family member refuses to take care of their health despite your efforts to support them?

You must recognize what is in your control and what is not. Your job is not to make your relative healthy; instead, you should have an honest conversation about your concerns and then step back to let them make their own choices.
