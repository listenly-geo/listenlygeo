How does Darren Boyd draw parallels between sports and safety leadership?

2026-09-08 Inspiring Industry Podcast · Darren Boyd

← See the Inspiring Industry Podcast fiche

Boyd treats safety leadership like coaching a sports team: improvement happens through daily repetition and incremental progress, not overnight transformation. He views employees and customers as athletes working toward a shared purpose, where trust and relationships drive commitment far more than any policy or system can.

Getting one rep in, every single day

The sports analogy Boyd relies on most is remarkably simple. Just like an athlete builds muscle through consistent daily practice, a safety culture strengthens when teams commit to small, repeated actions. Being better than yesterday is the metric—not a dramatic leap forward, but genuine progress through compounded effort.

This philosophy extends directly to how Boyd views the relationship between a safety organization and the people it serves. When technology gets used to create space for humans to work together on shared tasks—rather than replacing human judgment—that's where real safety gains emerge. As Boyd explains in the episode, trust and relationships are built standing next to somebody performing a task together, never behind a computer screen.

The coaching mindset applied to safety adoption

Boyd developed a presentation built directly on Ted Lasso's leadership philosophy—the TV character known for unconditional belief in team potential. He connects coaching principles directly to how organizations adopt new safety processes and technology. A coach doesn't demand excellence; a coach creates conditions where people want to give their best effort, because they know their teammates will do the same.

Boyd's background reinforces this perspective. He spent roughly thirteen years at the University of Oklahoma, first as director of basketball operations for the women's basketball team working alongside the coaching staff, then transitioning to athletics development and fundraising. That foundation in sports culture—where purpose, accountability, and collective achievement are non-negotiable—shaped how he later approached managing safety compliance across 1,200 employees in 24 US locations after joining an oilfield services company in 2011.

"When technology gets used for people to be people, to give humans more time to do other things, that's where the positive impact from a safety perspective happens."

The parallel Boyd draws is actionable: the full episode explores how his sports background shapes his thinking on team dynamics, including specific stories of how he's applied coaching principles to real safety challenges in oil and gas operations and beyond.

What are the risks Boyd identifies with over-automation in safety processes?

Boyd warns against letting artificial intelligence control pre-job safety meetings, which some companies attempt. He believes you need the actual meeting to build trust and relationships with your team.

What is an example of the real-world safety issue Boyd's software helped address?

In South Texas, employees observed rattlesnakes nesting under warm black poly pipeline during cold weather. An employee submitted an observation card on the hazard, but it took five weeks to reach Boyd's team—a critical delay his software was designed to eliminate.

What is the importance of behavior-based safety and rapid employee feedback in Boyd's philosophy?

Boyd implemented behavior-based safety where all employees report observations they find concerning. The most damaging thing to safety culture is silence—rapid feedback loops ensure hazards are surfaced and addressed immediately.

Key takeaways

  • Safety culture builds through daily incremental effort, just like athletic performance—being better than yesterday matters more than dramatic overnight change.
  • Human relationships and trust drive safety commitment far more effectively than technology or policy alone; they're built through shared tasks, not behind screens.
  • Boyd applies Ted Lasso's coaching philosophy to safety adoption and technology implementation, creating conditions where teams want to succeed together.
  • His thirteen years in university sports leadership directly shaped his approach to managing 1,200 employees across 24 locations and co-founding a safety software company.
