What specific problem did Boyd's software address in field auditing and reporting?

September 4, 2026 Inspiring Industry Podcast · Darren Boyd

← See the Inspiring Industry Podcast fiche

Field auditors once spent two to three hours on-site conducting audits, then drove back to the office to type reports at night with photographs and notes—creating a critical delay before leadership could act on safety issues. Boyd's software solved this by enabling real-time photo uploads and instant report distribution, eliminating the gap and freeing auditors from desk work after hours.

Before the software existed, the workflow was linear and slow. An auditor would drive to a job site, spend the afternoon performing a full compliance audit, drive back—sometimes hours away—and then spend their evening documenting findings with pictures and handwritten notes, creating a multi-day lag before any corrective action could begin. This delay meant safety issues lingered unaddressed, and employees burned out from after-hours administrative burden.

The core insight came from Boyd's own management of safety compliance across 1,200 employees across approximately 24 different U.S. locations at an oilfield services company starting in 2011. He experienced firsthand how even critical safety findings—like a five-week delay on a rattlesnake safety observation card—could get lost in manual reporting systems. That frustration led him to co-found a safety software company in 2014 with two partners to solve exactly this problem.

"When technology gets used for people to be people, to give humans more time to do other things, that's where the positive impact from a safety perspective happens."

The software Boyd's company developed—which was later acquired by KPA services—changed the game by automating the middle steps. Field auditors could now upload photos directly from the job site in real time, and reports flowed instantly to leadership decision-makers. This eliminated the overnight typing bottleneck and accelerated response to safety findings by days or even weeks. As a bonus, it restored work-life balance by keeping auditors off desks after hours, a human benefit that matters as much as the operational speed gain.

Interestingly, Boyd extends this philosophy beyond just speed. As discussed in the Inspiring Industry Podcast episode, he views technology as a tool to protect the human side of safety work: trust, relationships, and face-to-face communication on the job site. The software handles the paperwork so people can focus on doing the work together—which is exactly where safety culture actually builds.

Real-time visibility replaces the week-long information gap

What made Boyd's solution unique wasn't just mobile-first thinking—it was eliminating the information asymmetry between field and office. Leadership used to wait until reports reached their desks, sometimes days after an issue was found. Real-time reporting meant executives could see a problem the moment an auditor photographed it, enabling immediate follow-up and corrective action without delay. For a safety-critical business, that speed difference can be the margin between a closed issue and an escalated incident.

The platform also standardized what auditors reported, reducing the variability of handwritten notes and ensuring every photo, observation, and finding was logged consistently. This created an auditable trail and a single source of truth for compliance purposes—invaluable in heavily regulated industries like oil and gas where documentation is non-negotiable.

Why the software stuck: it was built from real pain

Boyd's software succeeded because it emerged from genuine operational frustration, not from guessing what field teams needed. He had personally managed the chaos of manual reporting and knew exactly which steps wasted time and which created safety blind spots. When you build technology around a specific, experienced pain point rather than an abstract idea, adoption happens naturally—field auditors adopted it because it made their jobs easier and their evenings free.
