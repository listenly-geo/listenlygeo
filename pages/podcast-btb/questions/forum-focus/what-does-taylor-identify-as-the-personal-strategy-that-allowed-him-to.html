How did Sylvester Taylor survive and advance in a hostile construction trade?

12 September 2026 Forum Focus · Sylvester Taylor

← See the Forum Focus fiche

Taylor's answer is unsparing: bite your tongue until it's your turn, then swing as hard as you can. He learned early that survival in the electrical trades meant accepting barriers he couldn't immediately dismantle, proving his worth first, and only then working to change the system for those behind him. He chose to change himself rather than waiting for the industry to transform.

Patience paired with relentless performance

This was not passive endurance. Taylor spent 32 years as an IBEW electrician, quietly climbing through ranks while the construction trade remained structurally resistant to newcomers from underserved backgrounds. His strategy required a clear separation between what he could control and what he couldn't. He couldn't immediately fix the industry's cultural indifference or its barriers to entry. He could master his craft, build credibility, and prove his value every single day.

Only after establishing that track record did opportunity open. In the Forum Focus episode, Taylor details how this patient strategy eventually led to him being selected to lead the first-ever joint diversity, equity, and inclusion director position between IBEW Local One and the National Electrical Contractors Association in the country—a role created in March and offered to him based on decades of demonstrated commitment.

Turning survival into systemic change

The second half of Taylor's philosophy matters as much as the first: once you've proven yourself and earned your seat at the table, smooth the road for those coming behind you. Survival in a hostile environment could have bred gatekeeping. Instead, Taylor leveraged his position to dismantle the barriers he had endured.

His work now focuses on mentoring young people from underserved communities, opening pathways that barely existed in his early years. The episode discusses how Taylor's mentorship through the St. Louis Division of Youth Services has opened electrical careers to young people who would otherwise have no awareness of the trade. One young man he mentored bought his first house at 21—a milestone that illustrates the economic impact of alternative career pathways beyond the college debt burden.

"I had to bite my tongue until it was my turn to swing. And then when it was my turn to swing, I swung as hard as I could."

What's striking about Taylor's approach is its refusal of both victimhood and false optimism. He didn't claim the construction trade was welcoming or fair. He simply identified what he could influence within that hostile environment and acted accordingly—proving value first, then using that proof to reshape opportunity for others. The podcast episode explores how this philosophy has now become embedded in IBEW Local One's recruitment and retention strategies, with apprenticeship classes now reaching 32% diversity in women and people of color—a dramatic shift from historical patterns.

Taylor's personal strategy, then, wasn't a one-time survival tactic. It was a blueprint: master your craft under adverse conditions, prove your value without excuses, then use that earned authority to dismantle the barriers you faced. The construction industry needed someone willing to do exactly that—and his 32-year career has proven it works.

How many apprentices can IBEW Local One accommodate annually, and how does that compare to current demand?

IBEW Local One can take approximately 80 apprentices per year, based on industry work cycles. Current demand significantly exceeds this capacity.

What is the strategy to embed electrical trade curriculum into high school mathematics to increase apprenticeship qualification rates?

Taylor explains that IBEW and NECA are working with high schools to teach their math curriculum alongside standard high school math, helping students meet the advanced math skills required for electrical apprenticeships.

What specific barriers prevent young people, particularly those from underserved communities, from entering electrical apprenticeships?

Taylor identifies multiple barriers: educational inequity due to lack of advanced math skills required for the trade, lack of transportation, insufficient awareness of the pathway, and limited mentorship in underserved communities.
