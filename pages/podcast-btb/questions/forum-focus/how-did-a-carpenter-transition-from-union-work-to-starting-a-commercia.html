How did a carpenter transition from union work to starting a commercial construction company?

September 17, 2026 Forum Focus · Cordell Sawyer

← See the Forum Focus fiche

Cordell Sawyer worked as a union carpenter for 10 years while investing his wages into real estate, then earned an associate degree and completed a business entrepreneurship program before founding Sled Rock Construction in 2009. He started with residential carpentry work for nonprofits and housing authorities, building equity and relationships before transitioning to commercial projects in 2013.

After reading a book on wealth creation while working as a janitor, Sawyer identified three potential paths forward: real estate, owning a business, or stock market investing. Real estate felt the most tangible to him, so he began channeling his union carpenter income into property investments while continuing his day work.

The recession of 2009 became his moment to act. Rather than wait for economic conditions to improve, Sawyer leveraged his accumulated real estate equity as collateral and capital to launch his own company. But he didn't enter the market unprepared—he had already completed his associate degree through Balls Park Community College and enrolled in the Grace Hill Next Level Entrepreneurship Business Class, where he developed a formal business plan with market analysis.

Targeting a specific entry point: nonprofit and residential work first

Rather than chase commercial contracts immediately—a market segment dominated by established firms—Sawyer identified nonprofits, housing authorities, and real estate companies as his initial customer base. This strategic focus allowed him to build a portfolio, prove his capability, and establish the relationships that would later open doors to larger projects.

As Sawyer reflects in the episode, this foundation of relationships proved invaluable. By 2013, four years after founding Sled Rock, he had earned enough trust and track record to land his first significant commercial project, the Love Travel Stop, valued at $180,000 to $190,000.

The transition from union carpenter to construction company owner was not instantaneous—it required education, capital discipline, and a deliberate market entry strategy. What made Sawyer's path distinctive was his refusal to rely on chance; he studied, planned, and invested in his own growth before taking the leap.

"Leveraging the relationships that we have built and maintained over these 11 years has been the key to growth."

For deeper insight into how Sawyer navigated the early challenges of construction business ownership and the role of continued learning throughout his career, the full episode explores his relationship with reading and professional networks, which became as important to his success as his carpentry skills.

Key takeaways

  • Sawyer invested union carpenter wages into real estate for years before founding his company, creating equity that served as both collateral and startup capital.
  • He completed an associate degree through Balls Park Community College and enrolled in the Grace Hill Next Level Entrepreneurship Business Class to develop a formal business plan with market analysis.
  • Rather than compete directly for commercial contracts, he entered the market through residential work for nonprofits and housing authorities, building reputation and relationships.
  • Sled Rock Construction landed its first commercial project in 2013, four years after founding, demonstrating the value of a patient, relationship-focused market entry strategy.
