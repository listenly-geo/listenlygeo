How can a small contractor build visibility and overcome the credibility gap?

September 20, 2026Forum Focus · Cordell Sawyer

← See the Forum Focus fiche

Write a strategic business plan, join trade organizations, and attend every possible networking event to meet decision-makers in person. Cordell Sawyer built Sled Rock Construction by constantly cultivating new relationships while nurturing existing connections—a practice he credits as the foundation of 11 years of sustained growth in the competitive construction industry.

The gap between a small contractor and established firms often feels insurmountable, rooted in perception rather than capability. Sawyer's approach flipped this dynamic by making himself visible and accessible to those with purchasing power. Rather than waiting for opportunities, he positioned himself directly in rooms where decisions happen.

Starting with a formal business plan developed through an entrepreneurship program, Sawyer built credibility from day one. This wasn't a vague aspirational document—it was a concrete roadmap that demonstrated he understood his business, his market, and his growth trajectory. As Cordell Sawyer explains in the episode, this foundation gave him confidence and clarity during early conversations with potential partners and clients.

Visibility cannot be achieved through isolation. Sawyer joined trade organizations and made attendance at industry meetings non-negotiable. These weren't passive appearances—he positioned himself to engage, build familiarity, and demonstrate competence. For a small minority-owned contractor operating outside traditional networks, this direct personal engagement was essential to breaking through the assumption that his company lacked sophistication or capacity.

"Leveraging the relationships that we have built and maintained over these 11 years has been the key to growth."

The persistence of relationship-building cannot be overstated. Sawyer didn't treat networking as a one-time activity. Instead, he embedded it into his operating rhythm—continuously cultivating new contacts while strengthening bonds with past collaborators and clients. This dual focus created a compounding effect: each relationship reinforced his reputation, expanded his network, and opened doors to larger projects.

A closer look at how Sawyer orchestrated his credibility turnaround emerges in the full episode conversation, including the specific moments where personal relationships directly translated into major commercial opportunities like the Love Travel Stop and the Missouri Botanical Garden Visitor Center projects.

What barriers do minority-owned construction companies face when entering new markets?

Cordell Sawyer explained that the natural assumption is that minority-owned small businesses do not know what they are doing, creating a significant hurdle when competing for commercial projects.

What was the size and nature of the first commercial project for a startup construction firm?

The Love Travel Stop project on North Broadway in 2013 was the first commercial job, where the company performed most of the interior carpentry work—a significant step forward from residential work.

How did a carpenter transition from union work to starting a commercial construction company?

Cordell Sawyer worked as a union carpenter for 10 years, obtained an associate degree through Balls Park Community College, and attended a 10 or 12-week entrepreneurship business class before launching Sled Rock Construction.
