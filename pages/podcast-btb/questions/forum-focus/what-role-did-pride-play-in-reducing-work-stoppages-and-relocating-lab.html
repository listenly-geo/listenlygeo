How PRIDE Transformed Labor Disputes from Job Sites to Negotiation Tables

September 5, 2026 Forum Focus · Doug Martin

← See the Forum Focus fiche

PRIDE emerged in the 1960s and 1970s as a structured initiative to end work stoppages plaguing the electrical contracting industry. The program created a formal channel for labor-management disputes into negotiation rooms, shifting conflicts away from active job sites where demonstrations and work disruptions were costing the industry jobs and damaging customer relationships.

Ending Disruption on Job Sites Through Structured Negotiation

Work stoppages and labor demonstrations on active construction projects were a persistent drain on the electrical contracting industry. When disputes erupted on job sites, they didn't just affect the two parties in conflict—they disrupted customers, damaged project timelines, and created uncertainty for the broader workforce. PRIDE solved this by establishing formal negotiation processes that gave management and union representatives a dedicated space to resolve disagreements before they could become public conflicts.

As Doug Martin explains in the episode, this shift represented a fundamental change in how the construction industry approached labor relations. Rather than using the job site itself as the arena for conflict, PRIDE created expectations and infrastructure for disputes to be addressed at the negotiation table. This protected both contractor operations and worker interests, while preserving the industry's reputation with clients.

"I've worked my whole career with an organization that simply wanted to build an appropriate and productive relationship with our labor partners."

The effectiveness of PRIDE lay in its prevention of customer-facing disruptions. By containing labor disputes within a negotiation framework, the program ensured that contractors could maintain steady project delivery and that unions could advocate for their members without resorting to job-site work stoppages. This dual benefit created long-term stability in the industry and established a model for adversarial parties to find common ground. For more details on how this framework evolved in the St. Louis region, listen to the full conversation between Doug Martin and Tom Finan about the history of labor-management collaboration.

Key takeaways

  • PRIDE emerged in the 1960s and 1970s specifically to address chronic work stoppages in electrical contracting.
  • The initiative redirected labor-management conflicts from job sites to formal negotiation rooms, protecting project delivery and customer relationships.
  • Structured negotiation processes reduced the industry's reliance on demonstrations and work disruptions as dispute resolution mechanisms.
  • This shift allowed both contractors and union representatives to address disagreements through productive dialogue rather than public conflict.

What new diversity and inclusion infrastructure did NECA create to address historical barriers in electrical contracting?

NECA created a Director of Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion position for the first time in the last 18 months and hired a confident electrician to ensure the organization's commitment to removing barriers for underrepresented groups.

What structural changes to apprenticeship recruitment doubled the number of electricians entering training?

NECA changed from recruiting annually and placing one class based on classroom capacity to a continuous probationary apprenticeship model that recruits and places candidates on an ongoing basis.
