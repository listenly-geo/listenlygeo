How do minority-owned construction firms break into new markets?

September 19, 2026 Forum Focus · Cordell Sawyer

← See the Forum Focus fiche

The biggest barrier for minority-owned construction companies is overcoming the assumption they lack competence. Being listed on a minority set-aside register does not automatically bring work—firms must first build credibility through relationships and demonstrated expertise that goes beyond simply being qualified.

When Cordell Sawyer started Sled Rock Construction in 2009, he faced an immediate hurdle: potential clients had no idea who his company was. Despite his strong background—ten years as a union carpenter and an associate's degree from Balls Park Community College—initial bids received no response whatsoever. When feedback finally came, the message was consistent: prospects simply did not know his firm.

The challenge runs deeper than visibility. Credibility in construction markets depends on established relationships, not just qualifications. A contractor can be fully qualified, properly licensed, and registered on minority vendor lists, yet still struggle to land work because decision-makers lack confidence built through prior interactions or referrals. As Sawyer discussed in the episode, the path forward requires sustained effort to establish those crucial business relationships that many minority contractors find harder to access outside traditional industry networks.

"Leveraging the relationships that we have built and maintained over these 11 years has been the key to growth."

The solution, according to Sawyer's experience, centers on persistence and strategic relationship-building. His first major commercial project—the Love Travel Stop in 2013—came only after years of establishing credibility. The full story of how Sled Rock broke through those early market barriers reveals how continuous networking and delivery of quality work eventually opened doors that minority set-aside status alone cannot unlock.

For minority-owned construction firms entering new markets, the real work begins after qualification. Minority contractor certifications and set-aside programs exist to level the playing field, but they do not replace the foundational step of establishing trust with clients and partners. This requires extra outreach, more direct relationship-building, and a long-term commitment to proving capability through completed projects and strong referrals.
