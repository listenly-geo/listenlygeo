How does the practice of counting minority workers on individual projects differ from achieving actual workforce diversification?

September 8, 2026 Forum Focus · Sylvester Taylor

← See the Forum Focus fiche

Simply placing minority workers on a job to meet participation requirements solves an immediate problem on paper, but workers are often laid off once the project ends, meaning no lasting change occurs. Real workforce diversification demands retention and ongoing career opportunity, not just hiring numbers that disappear when the contract does.

The difference between hitting numbers and building careers

Sylvester Taylor draws a sharp distinction between compliance hiring and genuine diversity work. Putting six people on a job fulfills a diversity quota for that specific project, yet fails to address what happens after. The workers leave, the project closes, and the construction firm's actual workforce composition remains unchanged. This approach treats minority participation as a checkbox rather than a human career path.

As Taylor explains in the episode, the construction industry has long relied on this transactional model. A contractor hires to meet contract requirements, then sheds workers when the job is done. The cycle repeats: hire for the requirement, lay off after completion, no institutional learning, no advancement, no pipeline. For workers, this means precarious employment and no pathway to stable, skilled careers in the trades.

Retention as the measure of real diversity

True diversity is measured by who stays and advances, not by who gets hired for a single project. Taylor emphasizes that meaningful change requires systems designed to keep workers in the field, develop their skills, and create pathways to supervisory and management roles. This demands intentional mentorship, apprenticeship programs, and employer commitment to minority workers beyond contract completion.

This perspective is grounded in Taylor's 32 years as an electrician with the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers and his role as co-founder and president of the Electrical Workers Minority Caucus. His appointment in March to lead the first-ever joint diversity, equity, and inclusion position created between IBEW Local One and the National Electrical Contractors Association signals a shift toward systemic change rather than project-level compliance. A detail worth hearing directly from Taylor: the episode explores his mentorship of a young man who, at just 21 years old, purchased his first house through a career pathway in the trades—a concrete outcome of retention and opportunity that temporary project placement cannot produce.

"I had to bite my tongue until it was my turn to swing. And then when it was my turn to swing, I swung as hard as I could."

Key takeaways

  • Project-based minority hiring fulfills compliance requirements on paper but creates no lasting workforce change if workers are laid off when the project ends.
  • Real diversification is defined by retention, skill development, and career advancement opportunities—not by temporary placement numbers.
  • Systemic change requires intentional mentorship, apprenticeship pipelines, and employer commitment to minority workers beyond individual contracts.
  • The construction trades have historically relied on transactional hiring models that treat diversity as a checkbox rather than a human career pathway.

See also

What does it mean to get minority communities into rooms that have been historically closed to organized labor in the construction trades?

Taylor explains that organized labor has historically said no to African-American participation, causing communities to close their doors to them in return. Rebuilding trust requires genuine, sustained commitment to inclusion—not just surface-level gestures.

What community impact did joint labor-management electrical work achieve in St. Louis?

The collaborative effort between NECA contractors and IBEW union electricians produced tangible civic contributions, demonstrating that partnership between labor and management can serve broader community benefit beyond individual profit.

What role did PRIDE play in reducing work stoppages and relocating labor-management conflict from job sites to the negotiation room?

PRIDE emerged in the 1960s and 1970s to end work stoppages plaguing the electrical contracting industry. The initiative created a structured way to channel disputes into formal negotiation rather than allowing them to disrupt construction sites.
