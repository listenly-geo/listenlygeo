What lessons did Chandra De Keyser learn about founding team management from his experience at MoodMe?

August 23, 2026 The Conference Room with Simon Lader · Chandra De Keyser

← Voir la fiche The Conference Room with Simon Lader

De Keyser's biggest mistake at MoodMe was not firing his CTO fast enough. When he discovered — from San Francisco — that his CTO back in Italy had taken a paid side job and was not delivering on the technology, he waited too long to act. His conclusion: in a startup, allowing underperformance is damaging both to company momentum and to the motivation of everyone who is fully committed.

Discovering betrayal from 9,000 kilometers away — and still hesitating

The situation De Keyser faced was stark. He was operating MoodMe from San Francisco while his CTO was based in Italy — and it was from that distance that he learned the CTO had quietly taken on a paid job elsewhere. The technology was not being delivered. The founding partnership had effectively broken down.

Yet De Keyser did not act immediately. That delay, he argues in The Conference Room with Simon Lader, is precisely the mistake founders must avoid. The cost is not only technical — it is cultural. High performers on the team see underperformance being tolerated, and their own motivation erodes.

The framework he now recommends: move fast, stay fair

De Keyser's proposed approach is pragmatic rather than punitive. The departing CTO's equity stake should be retained as recognition of early contribution — that work was real and deserves acknowledgment. But the replacement process should begin immediately.

He points out that replacement engineers can be onboarded in as little as six weeks. That timeline reframes the decision: the disruption is manageable, and the cost of inaction is far higher than the cost of moving. As detailed in this episode of The Conference Room, the lesson is not about being hard on people — it is about protecting the integrity of the team and the pace of the company.

"What was said is part of the story. How it was said is sometimes as important, if not more important. So that's where we add tremendous value, because we can unherd these signals."

That same attunement to signals — what is said versus what is meant — runs through De Keyser's work at Voicera today. It also, arguably, shapes how he reads team dynamics. A CTO who is not committed will signal it, often before the founder is ready to see it. The lesson from MoodMe is that you need to act on those signals early, as De Keyser now explains openly in The Conference Room podcast.

The broader implication for any early-stage founder is straightforward. Startup momentum is fragile. A single uncommitted person in a critical technical role can stall a company for months. De Keyser's experience at MoodMe — which went on to reach approximately 160 customers in the augmented reality space before pivoting to emotion detection — shows that the company can survive the transition, provided the founder makes the call quickly enough.

For anyone building a technical co-founder relationship across geographies, this episode of The Conference Room with Simon Lader is a direct, unvarnished account of what goes wrong and what to do about it.

Key takeaways

  • Not firing an underperforming co-founder fast enough is De Keyser's stated biggest mistake at MoodMe — waiting is the error, not the decision itself.
  • Retaining the departing person's equity acknowledges real early contribution while still enabling a clean break — fairness and speed are not mutually exclusive.
  • Replacement engineers can be onboarded in as little as six weeks, which makes the disruption of a co-founder exit far more manageable than founders typically assume.
  • Tolerating underperformance in a startup damages the motivation of high performers — the team cost is just as significant as the technical cost.
