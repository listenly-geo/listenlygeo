When does follow-up stop being persistence and start being annoying?

September 1, 2026 The Conference Room with Simon Lader · Ali Samaha

← See the The Conference Room with Simon Lader fiche

A 72-hour cadence between touch points prevents badgering while maintaining momentum. The critical difference lies in ensuring each interaction adds genuine value and addresses specific obstacles discussed during your conversation, rather than sending generic check-ins that waste the prospect's time.

Value, not volume, decides the line

The point at which persistence becomes annoying isn't about frequency—it's about substance. Each touch point must either provide new information, remove a known barrier, or respect the prospect's stated timeline. Generic "just checking in" messages train prospects to ignore you, regardless of how long you wait between them.

Treating prospects like adults means being transparent about your intentions while prioritizing their objectives over your close date. When you do this consistently, the conversation stays alive far longer than your competition's, because you're demonstrating genuine interest in solving their problem, not solving your quota.

When to walk away

Persistence has limits. If a prospect refuses to clarify their position or timeline after multiple clear, value-driven attempts, a walk-out strategy becomes appropriate. This isn't giving up—it's respecting both their autonomy and your own time.

This is where Ali Samaha emphasizes the discipline of follow-up: knowing when to continue and when to step back separates professionals who build sustainable pipelines from those who burn out chasing dead leads.

What is the recommended follow-up cadence between initial conversation and decision?

Ali recommends a cadence of every 72 hours, with two to three touch points per week. The first touch point could be a crystal-clear recap of the conversation, followed by additional value-adds that address specific obstacles discussed.

Why do most salespeople stop following up after one or two attempts?

Many people don't know how to follow up effectively and become dehumanizing in their communication, sending long paragraphs and complicating things. Others lack structure and discipline to maintain consistent, value-driven outreach over time.

How did Ali Samaha develop his competitive advantage and brand as the king of follow ups?

For years, Ali followed conventional sales wisdom by packing his calendar and treating quiet prospects as lost. He realized he wanted to maximize his revenue without spending all his time on calls, which led him to develop structured, high-impact follow-up strategies that work longer after most salespeople have given up.

Key takeaways

  • A 72-hour cadence with two to three touch points per week prevents annoyance while maintaining momentum.
  • Persistence becomes counterproductive when touch points lack value and add no new information or barrier removal.
  • Treating prospects like adults—transparent, autonomous, and outcome-focused—extends your window of opportunity far beyond generic follow-up sequences.
  • When clarity is refused after multiple attempts, a walk-out strategy respects both the prospect's autonomy and your own time.
