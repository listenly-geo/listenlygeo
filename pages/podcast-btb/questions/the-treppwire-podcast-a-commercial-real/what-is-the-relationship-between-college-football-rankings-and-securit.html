How do college football rankings relate to securitized student housing debt concentration?

2026-09-07 The TreppWire Podcast: A Commercial Real Estate Show

← See the The TreppWire Podcast: A Commercial Real Estate Show fiche

The AP Top 25 college football rankings function as a surprisingly effective proxy for securitized student housing debt concentration. The 25 ranked programs collectively hold $6.1 billion of the $29.3 billion in total outstanding securitized student housing debt—representing 21% of the entire market—proving that football rankings actually outperform U.S. News academic rankings as a map of where this capital concentrates.

Why Football Rankings Beat Academic Rankings

The correlation works because large football programs share specific structural traits that drive student housing demand. These universities tend to have large undergraduate populations, limited on-campus housing capacity, and surrounding markets with room for private development—exactly the conditions that attract securitized student housing investment.

This insight emerged from detailed analysis by Trepp on recent CMBS trends, where data modeling revealed that the AP poll alignment was actually stronger than traditional academic metrics. The reason is practical: U.S. News rankings prioritize research output and selectivity, whereas football rankings capture schools built around undergraduate scale and campus culture—the exact profile of universities generating sustained purpose-built student housing investment.

The Portfolio Performance of Football School Housing

The top 25 football programs' student housing portfolios also perform measurably better than their academic counterparts. Football schools report 91.5% collective occupancy and 1.68 times median debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), compared to 89.9% occupancy and 1.61 times DSCR for U.S. News top 25 universities.

Non-performing rates tell the same story: 0.6% for football schools versus 0.9% for academically ranked institutions. The gap reflects both the tighter supply-demand balance at large football campuses and the more predictable cash flows from undergraduate populations that value on- and off-campus residential experience as part of the college lifestyle.

Major programs carry substantial individual exposures. University of Texas leads with $941 million across 29 properties at 90.2% occupancy; Penn State holds $716 million at 94.3% occupancy; Texas A&M carries $633 million; and Texas Tech manages $380 million. These aren't incidental ancillary assets—they are core portfolio anchors for securitized student housing funds, and their concentration in the AP Top 25 speaks to how thoroughly football school geography shapes capital flow in the broader student housing market.

A concrete example: as discussed in this week's episode of The TreppWire Podcast, Aries and Scion Group recently completed a $435 million acquisition of four properties with over 2,300 beds at $187,800 per bed—a premium valuation that reflects the durability of demand at ranked football markets, where supply constraints and demographic strength justify capital deployment even in a higher rate environment.

"The AP poll is acting like a rough proxy for the types of universities that tend to support purpose-built student housing: large undergraduate populations, limited on-campus capacity, and surrounding markets with room to develop."

For institutional investors tracking securitized student housing exposure, the football rankings shorthand offers genuine analytical value. Rather than sorting through thousands of individual university profiles, the podcast analysis revealed that monitoring AP Top 25 program movements and on-field performance can serve as an early signal for shifts in student enrollment patterns and housing demand in those markets—information that often feeds into refinancing decisions and valuation adjustments months before it appears in occupancy reports.
