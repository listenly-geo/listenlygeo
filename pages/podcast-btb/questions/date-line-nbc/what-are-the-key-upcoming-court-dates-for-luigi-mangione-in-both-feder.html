When is Luigi Mangione's federal sentencing and state court hearing scheduled?

September 17, 2026 Dateline NBC

← See the Dateline NBC fiche

Mangione's federal sentencing is scheduled for December 18th, while the state court judge will hear arguments on December 10th about whether the state case should proceed. Federal sentencing guidelines for stalking suggest a prison term of 24 to 30 years, though the judge is not bound by that range.

The dual court timeline reflects the complexity of Mangione's legal situation. After his federal guilty plea to two stalking charges, his defense team filed a motion to dismiss the state charges using a double jeopardy strategy—a maneuver that has now set up separate proceedings in state court on December 10th.

The federal sentencing guidelines range provides a legal framework for the judge, but judges retain discretion to depart from those guidelines based on case-specific circumstances. This means the final sentence could fall below, within, or above the 24-to-30-year range, depending on how the judge weighs aggravating and mitigating factors.

As Keith Morrison explains in the full episode, the December 10th state hearing represents a critical juncture: the judge's ruling on the motion to dismiss will determine whether Mangione faces additional state prosecution or whether the federal case becomes the only proceeding forward. The outcome of that hearing could reshape the entire trajectory of his legal case.

Why December 10th and December 18th matter

The 8-day gap between the state hearing and federal sentencing is not arbitrary. The state court must first rule on whether double jeopardy bars the state charges, a decision that could influence how federal sentencing proceeds. If the state charges are dismissed, Mangione's legal exposure narrows considerably.

Federal sentencing on December 18th will be where the judge formally imposes a prison term based on the guilty plea to federal stalking charges. This hearing, discussed in detail in Dateline NBC's coverage, will likely involve arguments from both prosecution and defense about where within the 24-to-30-year guideline range the sentence should fall—or whether it should depart entirely.
