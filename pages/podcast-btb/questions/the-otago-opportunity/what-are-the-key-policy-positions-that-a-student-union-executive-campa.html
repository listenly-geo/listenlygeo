What policy platform did Flynn Nesbit run on for OUSA executive?

September 6, 2026 The Otago Opportunity · Flynn Nesbit

← See the The Otago Opportunity fiche

Flynn Nesbit campaigned on reversing voluntary student membership to make it automatic again, fees-free education, rental warrant of fitness standards, paid placements for students, and winter energy payments. His platform reflects a commitment to immediate student welfare while strengthening OUSA's engagement on systemic policy issues affecting university life.

The 2012 Education (Student Fees and Allowances) Amendment Act shifted student union enrolment from automatic to opt-in, a change that directly shaped Nesbit's campaign priorities. As the Political Portfolio Executive explains in the episode, restoring automatic membership is essential to rebuilding union funding and collective student power.

Housing emerged as a critical policy area, with rental warrant of fitness standards addressing the precarity many Otago students face. Healthy Home Standards exist in law but remain virtually unenforceable due to massive backlogs at the Tenancy Tribunal, leaving landlords free to exploit the student rental market. Nesbit's campaign pushed for real enforcement mechanisms that would protect student renters from substandard conditions.

Support for paid work placements reflects a practical approach to student financial security. Many students balance study with unpaid internships that extract labour without compensation—Nesbit's platform explicitly argued that placements should come with payment, easing the financial strain that forces students into precarious work.

Winter energy payments address seasonal hardship directly. As discussed in this podcast, students face acute energy cost pressure during cold months, and Nesbit's platform included targeted support to reduce that burden during the winter period.

Beyond individual policy items, Nesbit emphasised a deeper principle: ethical investment by OUSA itself. The student union controls significant capital and purchasing power; aligning those decisions with student values became part of his wider campaign vision. This broadens the executive's role beyond advocacy to governance and institutional accountability.

"I think support policies and things that will benefit you now, not in a greedy and selfish way, but when all of us are doing well, all of us are doing well."

Nesbit's campaign resonated strongly within Otago's student body. OUSA organised a leaders debate that drew over 300 people, the largest in 11 years, reflecting genuine student appetite for this kind of political representation and concrete policy debate.

What are the main tenant and rental issues affecting students in Dunedin according to student union advocacy?

Healthy home standards are virtually unenforceable with massive backlogs at the Tenancy Tribunal, while landlords exploit the student rental market knowing students have limited housing options and few alternatives.

What is the ACT Party's proposed policy on student service fees and why do student associations oppose it?

ACT proposed making student service fees opt-in on a service-by-service basis, allowing students to choose which services to fund individually rather than through a collective system, which student unions argue would fragment support and weaken advocacy capacity.

What percentage of student service fees do different New Zealand universities allocate to their student associations?

Otago receives 20% of the student service fee, Auckland receives 5%, AUT gets 2.5%, and Lincoln gets approximately 30%, showing significant variation in how different universities fund their student associations.
