What are the secrets to a great burger according to Good Good's owner Rose Gleeson?

24 August 2026 The Otago Opportunity · Rose Gleeson

← See the full The Otago Opportunity podcast page

Rose Gleeson says two techniques set Good Good apart from other burger joints: toasting the buns with a little butter, and steaming the cheese so it melts perfectly as the burger is layered. Most places skip both steps — and according to Gleeson, that's exactly where the difference shows up in the final product.

Butter on the bun, steam on the cheese — two steps most kitchens ignore

The techniques Gleeson describes are deceptively simple. Toasting the bun with butter creates a lightly crisped, flavour-rich surface that holds up to the fillings without going soggy. It's a foundational step in classic burger craft — and one that, as she notes in The Otago Opportunity, many burger places simply don't bother with.

The cheese technique is equally deliberate. Rather than relying on residual heat from the patty to melt the cheese — which often results in uneven, partially solid slices — Gleeson steams it during the layering process. The result is a fully melted, uniform coating that integrates into the burger rather than sitting on top of it.

These are not flashy innovations. They are the kind of small, disciplined habits that separate a well-executed burger from a forgettable one — and they speak directly to Gleeson's background as a trained chef, a detail explored further in this episode of The Otago Opportunity.

A lifelong passion turned into a Vogel Street kitchen — by someone who actually trained for it

Gleeson didn't stumble into Good Good. She trained as a chef for two years at Otago Polytechnic and then spent two more years working in the restaurant industry before moving into government roles. Burgers, she says, have always been her absolute favourite food — and Good Good was already her favourite place in Dunedin before she decided to buy it.

When she saw the business come up for sale, her first instinct was protective: she didn't want someone to come in and ruin it. So she stepped in herself. The result, roughly a year in, is a venue on Vogel Street that has held on to what made it special — including those two techniques she cites as non-negotiable, a perspective she shares candidly in The Otago Opportunity podcast.

"We are pretty much just keeping the lights on at the moment. And that's 100% fine. I've come to terms with that. That's all it needs to do — it just needs to pay for itself and not make me bankrupt."

That frankness about the economics of running Good Good — shared openly alongside the craft details — is part of what makes this conversation worth listening to in full on The Otago Opportunity.

What is Rose Gleeson's background before owning Good Good?

Rose Gleeson trained as a chef at Otago Polytechnic for two years and worked in the restaurant industry before transitioning to government roles at ACC and Dunedin City Council. Her culinary foundation is what now informs her approach to running Good Good.

What is the economic situation facing Good Good and the Dunedin hospitality sector?

Rose Gleeson describes the restaurant as "pretty much just keeping the lights on," with all sales going toward wages, rent, insurance, licenses, and other operating costs. She traces the difficulty to a sharp economic downturn that hit in early 2025.

What were the biggest operational challenges Rose Gleeson faced after buying Good Good?

During the changeover, Rose Gleeson accidentally signed up for another business's electricity account, causing Good Good's power to be cut off. She also navigated a range of other unexpected operational hurdles in her first year as owner.

Key takeaways

  • Toasting the buns with butter and steaming the cheese are the two techniques Gleeson identifies as the difference-makers at Good Good.
  • Both steps are deliberately skipped by many burger places — which is precisely why Gleeson considers them non-negotiable.
  • Gleeson's two years of chef training at Otago Polytechnic and hands-on restaurant experience underpin her approach to the kitchen.
  • She bought Good Good because she loved it and didn't want to see someone else change what made it special — not merely as a business opportunity.
