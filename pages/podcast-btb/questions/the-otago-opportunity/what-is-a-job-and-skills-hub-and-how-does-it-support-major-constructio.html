What is a Job and Skills Hub and How Does It Support Major Construction?

10 September 2026 The Otago Opportunity · Raymond Clark

← See the The Otago Opportunity fiche

A job and skills hub is designed to connect local workers directly to employment opportunities created by major infrastructure projects. It provides entry-level apprenticeships, helps residents gain access to jobs, and enables local businesses to become supply chain partners for project contractors.

Why Workforce Central Dunedin Was Created

Workforce Central Dunedin was conceptualized in 2017–2018 by planners looking ahead at the needs of the new Dunedin Hospital build. They identified workforce shortages as one of four critical risks to successful construction. Rather than leave hiring to chance, they established a dedicated hub to bridge the gap between local talent and project demand.

This proactive approach has proven its value. As detailed in this episode, the hub has placed 312 people into employment and trained approximately 1,300 people in various competencies over five and a half years of operation.

How It Supports Construction Projects

The hub operates on three interconnected levels. First, it channels local residents into direct employment on the hospital build itself. Second, it offers entry-level apprenticeships—pathways for people with minimal prior experience to develop marketable skills. Third, it actively helps local businesses navigate supply chain opportunities, enabling them to compete for contracts servicing the main construction contractors.

The model reflects a genuine commitment to local benefit. As Raymond Clark explains in the podcast, the hub didn't restrict itself to construction alone—it placed workers into jobs across the broader economy whenever the opportunity arose, recognizing that economic participation itself mattered.

"We put lots of people into jobs that had got nothing to do with construction because there we were and we were working and we needed to do something."

What makes Workforce Central Dunedin unique is its regional isolation within the South Island context. Approximately seven or eight job and skills hubs operate in Auckland and the North Island, whereas Dunedin stands alone in its region. This scarcity underscores both the hub's strategic importance and the challenge of replicating the model across New Zealand's geography.

For a deeper look at how the hub manages contractor timelines and navigates government social procurement rules, listen to the full episode where Raymond Clark discusses the return on investment calculated by local consultants and the practical mechanics of matching residents to roles.

Key takeaways

  • A job and skills hub directly connects local residents to jobs and apprenticeships created by major infrastructure builds.
  • Workforce Central Dunedin was established to mitigate workforce shortages, identified as a critical risk to the Dunedin Hospital construction.
  • The hub enables local businesses to become supply chain partners for project contractors, multiplying economic benefit beyond direct employment.
  • Over five and a half years, the hub has placed 312 people into jobs and trained 1,300 residents in various skills.
