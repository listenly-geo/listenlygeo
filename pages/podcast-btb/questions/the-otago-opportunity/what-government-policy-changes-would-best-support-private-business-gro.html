How can regions shift from public sector employment to private business growth?

25 September 2026 The Otago Opportunity · Daniel Harms

← See The Otago Opportunity fiche

Invest in the startup ecosystem rather than offering tax breaks to attract external businesses. Daniel Harms advocates for direct support of early-stage ventures as the most effective policy lever, arguing this builds genuine private sector capacity from the ground up. His goal for Dunedin is to grow private business employment from its current 30 percent to 40–50 percent of total jobs, shifting away from the region's heavy dependence on government spending.

The ecosystem argument over external incentives

Tax breaks and subsidies to lure established companies from elsewhere rarely deliver lasting results, Harms explains. What works is creating the conditions for local founders and entrepreneurs to thrive. In the episode, Harms describes how this shift requires more than passive incentives—it demands active investment in startup infrastructure, mentorship, and capital access.

Dunedin faces a structural challenge: approximately 70 percent of the city's jobs are tied to central or local government spending, compared to a national average of around 60 percent. This dependency makes the region vulnerable to budget cuts and limits economic diversity. A thriving private sector, as discussed in this episode, acts as both a shock absorber and a growth engine for the whole economy.

Why Dunedin can build its own capacity

Dunedin already has the ingredients for organic growth: a population of 140,000, 30,000 university students, and established companies like Fisher and Paykel, Scott Technology, and Farah Engineering that demonstrate manufacturing and engineering capability. The missing piece is focused policy support for young ventures, not importing ready-made businesses.

Harms himself embodies this model. He founded Platinum Recruitment in 2014 from a bedroom in Mosgill with no clients and no candidates, and grew it within six months into an office-based firm with three full-time hires. His story and the broader ecosystem strategy show what becomes possible when a startup finds traction in a supportive environment.

"It's not a baby anymore. It's a preteen, and you've got all the wonderful growing pains but you also have that excitement of seeing it grow and develop."

Key takeaways

  • Tax breaks and external business recruitment are less effective than direct investment in startups and local ecosystem development.
  • Dunedin's target is to grow private sector employment from 30 percent to 40–50 percent of total jobs, reducing reliance on government spending.
  • Successful private sector growth requires mentorship, capital access, and infrastructure—not just passive incentives.
  • Dunedin has existing manufacturing and engineering capability to build upon, making organic growth from local ventures more realistic than attracting distant firms.
