What does Heather McDonnell-Alford mean by "share the scar, not the wound" when posting personal content online?

25 August 2026 The Athena Rebels Podcast · Heather McDonnell-Alford

← See the full The Athena Rebels Podcast page

McDonnell-Alford's principle is straightforward: only share a personal or difficult experience publicly once you have fully processed it and reached the other side — the scar stage — not while the pain is still raw and unresolved. Sharing from a place of open wounds serves you; sharing from a scar serves your audience.

Divorce, therapy, and the decision to wait before going public

McDonnell-Alford uses her own divorce as the clearest illustration of this principle. She did not post about it while she was living through it. She waited until she had completed significant therapy and had genuinely settled — emotionally — with the experience.

Only then did she speak about it publicly. By that point, the content was no longer about her processing pain. It was about offering something useful to people who might be going through something similar. The distinction matters: one is self-expression, the other is service.

This approach is especially relevant for professionals building a personal brand on LinkedIn — a platform where McDonnell-Alford spent approximately 15 hours per week in the early stages of growing her visibility. As she discusses in The Athena Rebels Podcast, authenticity on that platform is everything — but authenticity is not the same as oversharing.

Why timing changes everything: the difference between a wound and a scar

A wound is open. It is still changing, still reactive, still capable of infection. A scar is closed. It is evidence of something survived, not something still happening.

When you post from the wound stage, the content tends to reflect your current emotional state rather than any considered insight. The risk is that it pulls readers into your unresolved experience rather than illuminating something for them. McDonnell-Alford's point — explored in depth during this episode of the Athena Rebels Podcast — is that personal content has to clear a threshold of usefulness before it belongs in a public professional space.

That threshold is emotional resolution. Once you can talk about something with steadiness, with perspective, with something to offer — that is when it becomes content worth sharing. Until then, McDonnell-Alford's advice is to keep it private.

This principle pairs directly with her broader stance on personal branding: your LinkedIn presence is a reflection of who you are, and it needs to be intentional at every level. It is a point she develops further across the full Athena Rebels Podcast episode alongside her reflections on building Counting Creators' profile in the MSP community.

"When you speak about something personally online, share the scar, not the wound. If you're still in the trauma, it's the other side."

Key takeaways

  • Only share personal or difficult experiences publicly once you have fully processed them — the scar stage, not the wound stage.
  • Emotional resolution is the threshold: content posted from a place of settled perspective serves others; content posted in the middle of unresolved pain tends to serve only yourself.
  • McDonnell-Alford applied this to her own divorce — speaking about it only after significant therapy and genuine emotional closure.
  • This principle sits at the heart of intentional personal branding: authenticity on LinkedIn does not mean sharing everything in real time.
