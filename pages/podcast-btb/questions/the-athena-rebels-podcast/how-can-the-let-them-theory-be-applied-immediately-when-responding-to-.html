How can the Let Them Theory be applied immediately when responding to someone who irritates or disagrees with you?

September 20, 2026 The Athena Rebels Podcast

← See the The Athena Rebels Podcast fiche

When someone irritates or disagrees with you, pause for five seconds, count in your head, and tell yourself "let them" before responding. This creates space between you, the other person, and the situation, allowing you to refocus your attention back on yourself and respond consciously rather than react emotionally.

The method is straightforward and can be deployed in any moment of friction. Rather than defaulting to an immediate reaction—which often escalates tension or leads to words you regret—the five-second pause gives your brain time to shift from reactive mode to responsive mode.

Saying "let them" internally serves as a mental anchor, a short phrase that signals to yourself that you're deliberately stepping away from trying to control or fix the other person's thoughts, feelings, or choices. This reframes the situation as one where you reclaim your own agency instead of being pulled into their emotional space.

As explored in The Athena Rebels Podcast, this approach is not about passivity or avoiding difficult conversations. Instead, it's an empowering tool that helps you engage from a place of clarity and choice rather than reactivity.

The beauty of the five-second technique is that it's portable—you can use it in meetings, in one-on-one conversations, via email, or even in text exchanges. The moment you feel the irritation or disagreement rising, you have a concrete intervention available to you right then.

Why the pause matters more than the response

Many people assume that a good response requires the right words. In reality, the quality of the pause determines the quality of everything that follows it. Those five seconds allow your nervous system to shift out of defensive mode.

When you're irritated, your brain is in a heightened state. The urge to correct, defend, or convince is almost automatic. The pause interrupts that automaticity. By introducing a simple ritual—counting, saying "let them"—you give yourself permission to step out of the urgency and reclaim your thinking mind.

This technique aligns with a broader insight discussed in the episode: the most powerful move is often to stop trying to change someone else and redirect that energy back to managing your own state and choices.

You can still have the difficult conversation, set a boundary, or disagree. But you'll do it from a place of intention rather than impulse, which almost always results in a better outcome.

"It's not about ignoring any kind of problems or avoiding any sort of responsibility or you need to make sure that it's an empowering thing for people to do."

One concrete detail from the episode worth exploring further: the viral nature of the Let Them Theory on TikTok, where the phrase "let them" became so popular that people were getting tattoos of it—a sign of how deeply this simple framework resonates across audiences.

Key takeaways

  • Take five seconds to pause and count in your head before responding to irritation or disagreement.
  • Say "let them" internally to create mental distance from the other person and the situation.
  • Use the pause to shift from reactive mode to conscious, intentional response.
  • This technique is empowering, not passive—it reclaims your own agency without avoiding responsibility or difficult conversations.
