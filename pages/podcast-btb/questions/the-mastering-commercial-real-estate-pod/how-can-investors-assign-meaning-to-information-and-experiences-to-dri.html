How do investors assign meaning to information and experiences to drive decision-making?

24 September 2026 The Mastering Commercial Real Estate Podcast · Joe Fairless

← See the The Mastering Commercial Real Estate Podcast fiche

Nothing has meaning until you decide to give it meaning. Ten people can hear the same conversation and draw ten different interpretations because everyone applies their own mental filters to the words they hear. You have the power to decide what things mean to you, then act on that interpretation accordingly.

This principle cuts to the heart of investor decision-making. When you encounter market data, investor feedback, or a new opportunity, the information itself is neutral. The meaning—and therefore your response—flows entirely from the lens you apply to it.

As Joe Fairless explains in the episode, this understanding becomes critical when scaling a business or managing investor relationships. The same market slowdown, the same rejection from a capital partner, the same operational challenge gets filtered through each person's unique interpretive framework. What one investor reads as a red flag, another reads as an entry point.

The practical implication: control the meaning you assign, and you control your response. Instead of passively receiving information and reacting to it, actively decide what it means. This separates reactive investors from those who navigate uncertainty with intention.

Meaning-making in investment refers to the process by which an investor interprets information, experiences, or market signals through their own cognitive framework—their beliefs, past experiences, and decision criteria. No two investors apply the same filters, so the same data point triggers different conclusions and actions.

The listening habit reinforces this. As Fairless details in the conversation, approaching every interaction with the goal of truly listening—rather than preparing your counter-argument—creates space for clearer interpretation. You hear not just the words, but the intent behind them. You gather the raw material more accurately, which sharpens the meaning you assign afterward.

"Listen, listen well, go into a conversation with the goal of listening. Have a couple talking points, but ultimately you go in to listen."

A deeper look reveals why this matters for scaling. When you're managing investor capital and building a team, miscommunication emerges from competing interpretations. You say "we're slowing acquisitions"; one investor hears "business is failing." You encounter a 97% physical occupancy but 68% economic occupancy (as in one of Fairless's early apartment deals); you interpret it as a pricing power problem; another investor interprets it as operational weakness. The data is identical. The meaning diverges.

Mastering this skill means recognizing two things: first, that you are always choosing meaning, never discovering it passively; second, that other investors and partners are doing the same, often from radically different starting points. Acknowledging this gap—and closing it through active listening and explicit alignment—is how scaling actually happens.
