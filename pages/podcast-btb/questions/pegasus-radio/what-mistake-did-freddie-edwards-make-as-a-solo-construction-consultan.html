What mistake did Freddie Edwards make as a solo construction consultant, and what would he advise others to avoid?

20 August 2026 Pegasus Radio · Freddie Edwards

← See the Pegasus Radio podcast page

Freddie Edwards missed a clear growth window in late 2023: he had enough work to justify hiring but didn't act, and his client book collapsed as a result — from four clients, to three, to two, to one, to none. His advice is direct: don't work alone for extended periods, get into a shared professional office space, and build a team early. Working solo means you have nothing to benchmark yourself against and can't tell a serious problem from a minor one.

Four clients became zero — and the warning sign was already there

By late 2023, Dragonfly Projects had enough inbound work that bringing someone on made obvious business sense. Edwards chose not to hire. What followed was a steady erosion of the client book that left the consultancy with no active clients at all.

He doesn't frame this as bad luck. He frames it as a structural failure: solo consultants have no safety net when momentum stalls, because every relationship and every mandate sits with one person. When capacity runs thin, client service suffers — and referrals dry up along with it.

Edwards eventually hired in August of his fourth year of trading, bringing in Louisa Whittaker from Gardner and Theobald — someone with approximately 12 to 13 years of commercial occupier experience — and completing the process within two weeks. The deliberate choice was to hire someone more senior, more experienced, and with different strengths. That combination, he argues, is what a young consultancy actually needs. Playing golf alone — why isolation distorts your judgement

Edwards uses a specific analogy to explain the hidden cost of working solo: playing golf on your own. Without others around you, you have no way to benchmark your game. You can't tell whether a bad round is genuinely bad or just slightly off. The same dynamic applies in a professional context.

Isolated consultants struggle to distinguish significant problems from insignificant ones. Minor setbacks feel catastrophic; genuine risks can go unrecognised. The absence of a team — or even proximity to other professionals — removes the calibration that makes good decisions possible.

His practical recommendations follow from this: work in a shared office space with similar professionals, and hire a team earlier than feels comfortable. Not later, when the client book is already contracting.

"The reason why I set up Dragonfly wasn't actually just to be able to run my own business. It was the privilege of being able to become an employer. Business is all about looking after people and businesses, and it's also about looking after our own."

Why did Freddie Edwards transition from sole trader to employer and what triggered the hiring decision?

Freddie operated as a sole trader for three years before beginning to hire in August of his fourth year, primarily because managing a young family made solo working increasingly unsustainable — and the business had reached a size where growth required more than one person.

What sectors does Dragonfly Projects specialise in and how is the business structured?

Dragonfly Projects specialises in construction project management across two distinct sectors: the commercial occupier office market and the residential developer space. The business has deliberately stayed within those two areas, with commercial occupier work accounting for roughly 75% of activity over the last four to five years.

Key takeaways

  • Missing a hiring window doesn't just slow growth — it can collapse an entire client book, as Edwards discovered firsthand in late 2023.
  • Working in isolation removes the benchmarks needed to assess problems accurately; shared professional space is a practical remedy, not a luxury.
  • When hiring, Edwards recommends deliberately choosing someone more experienced and with different strengths — not a mirror image of yourself.
  • The decision to become an employer was central to why Edwards founded Dragonfly in the first place — which makes the delay in hiring a contradiction he's candid about.
