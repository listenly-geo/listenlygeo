How do modern SNARKs relate to interactive proofs and why is the SumCheck protocol central to current SNARK design?

September 25, 2026 a16z crypto show · Noam Nisan

← See the a16z crypto show fiche

Modern SNARKs are built by combining interactive proofs with cryptographic commitment schemes, with the SumCheck protocol serving as the core subroutine in widely deployed systems like the GKR protocol. Interactive proofs themselves form the theoretical half of a SNARK—the second half comes from binding those proofs to cryptographic commitments. Prover speed remains the key bottleneck preventing widespread adoption, and SNARKs built around the SumCheck protocol offer the fastest prover performance available today.

From theory to practical verification

The relationship between interactive proofs and SNARKs represents a direct translation of theoretical computer science into production cryptography. As explained in the a16z crypto show episode, interactive proofs emerged from groundbreaking research in the 1980s and early 1990s as a way to verify mathematical claims through a back-and-forth exchange between a prover and verifier, using randomness to achieve certainty with logarithmic communication.

What makes this connection crucial is that the SumCheck protocol appeared immediately in the earliest major interactive proof work—the LFKN paper—and has remained the most efficient tool for this task ever since. The protocol reduces verification of a sum over a potentially exponential number of variables down to a manageable series of rounds.

The GKR protocol and modern SNARK architecture

The 2008 GKR protocol crystallized the relationship between interactive proofs and practical verification systems by using the SumCheck protocol as its foundational building block. GKR showed how to construct interactive proofs for circuit satisfiability that are efficient enough to matter in real systems.

When cryptographic commitments are layered on top of GKR's interactive proof structure, the result is a SNARK: a system where a prover can convince a verifier of a computation's correctness without the verifier needing to repeat that computation. As discussed in this podcast episode, this architecture directly enables zero-knowledge proofs and verifiable computation across blockchain systems and cloud computing applications.

Why prover speed defines the frontier

The practical adoption of SNARKs hinges on a single engineering constraint: the prover must run faster than the original computation it's proving. If generating a proof takes longer than running the computation itself, SNARKs become theoretically interesting but practically useless.

SNARKs that build on the SumCheck protocol achieve the fastest prover times known to researchers today. This performance advantage explains why systems like Lasso and Jolt, discussed in detail in the full episode, are reshaping zero-knowledge virtual machine (ZKVM) design. These systems recognize that the SumCheck-based architecture, despite its theoretical elegance, wins on practical metrics that matter.

"If you have some kind of theoretical inclination, you always find really fascinating theoretical questions that come up."

Beyond the mechanics of SumCheck itself, the episode explores how interactive proof theory evolved from pure mathematics into a foundational pillar of blockchain infrastructure, revealing how seemingly abstract research questions in complexity theory turned out to address the exact challenges digital trust systems face today.

Key takeaways

  • Interactive proofs form the theoretical core of modern SNARKs, combined with cryptographic commitments to create non-interactive verification systems.
  • The SumCheck protocol, first introduced in the LFKN paper of the early 1990s, remains the most efficient method for interactive proof construction today.
  • The 2008 GKR protocol demonstrated how to scale interactive proofs to practical circuit verification, laying the groundwork for modern SNARK architectures.
  • Prover speed—not verification speed—is the limiting factor for SNARK adoption, and SumCheck-based designs currently offer the fastest known prover performance.

What role did the LFKN paper play in establishing interactive proof systems for modern verification?

The LFKN paper from 1990–1992 established an efficient interactive proof for SharpSAT using the SumCheck protocol and demonstrated remarkable foresight by identifying exactly the right protocol that remains central to SNARK design today.

How does the SumCheck protocol enable verification of computation across massive datasets and cloud computing?

The SumCheck protocol allows a verifier to confirm a computation result without redoing the entire computation themselves, making it ideal for cloud computing and large-scale datasets by reducing verification burden to a manageable size.

What is an interactive proof and why did it represent a fundamental extension of mathematical proof?

An interactive proof is a proof that uses both interaction and randomness, unlike traditional mathematical proofs that are static and deterministic. This fundamental extension, pioneered in the 1980s, enabled new forms of verification for computations far more efficient than classical proofs.
