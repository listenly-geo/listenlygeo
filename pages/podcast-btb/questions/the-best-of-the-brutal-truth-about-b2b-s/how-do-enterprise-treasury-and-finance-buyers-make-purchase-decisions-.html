How do enterprise treasury and finance buyers make purchase decisions differently from traders and other personas in fintech sales?

25 Sep 2026 The Best of the Brutal Truth about B2B Sales & Selling · Guillaume

← See The Best of the Brutal Truth about B2B Sales & Selling fiche

Treasurers and directors of finance operate with analytical rigor and deep trust-orientation—not on price alone or the transactional energy of traders. They make logical decisions, but emotion and personal relationships anchor those choices, meaning process alone can kill a deal if it undermines the human connection.

Unlike the trader persona of decades past, treasury and finance leaders today are structured, disciplined professionals. This shift matters for sales teams working in fintech: the old playbook of aggressive, deal-driven energy does not land. Instead, as Guillaume explains in the episode, success comes from recognizing that these buyers are making logical choices rooted in rational analysis—but those logical choices rest on trust in the person and company behind the solution.

The decision-making pattern differs fundamentally from earlier eras. They do not default to the lowest-cost option if they lack confidence in the vendor or the relationship. This is where many fintech sales teams miss the mark: they treat treasury and finance buyers as purely data-driven, process-oriented personas, then wonder why a technically superior or cheaper product loses to a competitor. The reality is more human. These buyers want to trust the person representing the vendor, not just the product specs.

"I stop lying, be honest, ask questions, really understand what they're after, and I work for a Fortune 500 company so I'm trying to be the advocate of FIS when I'm at the client and be the client advocate when I'm working with my internal stakeholder."

The core principle here is dual advocacy. Guillaume's quote captures the tension treasury buyers feel: they need a vendor representative who genuinely understands their constraints and can argue their case internally. When a sales professional operates only as a company advocate, treasurers sense a lack of authenticity. Honesty and curiosity—not aggressive closing—build the trust that leads to purchase decisions, even in highly analytical environments. A treasury buyer will choose a vendor whose rep took time to ask real questions and understand their specific workflow over a vendor whose rep pushed a standard pitch.

One practical consequence: processes can feel suffocating to these buyers if they block human interaction. A lengthy approval chain or templated approach that removes the relationship element signals risk to them, not security. Treasurers are dealing with mission-critical systems—moving cash, managing exposure, settling trades. They want to know the person behind the solution will show up when things go sideways. That cannot be conveyed through a process; it only comes through genuine, unscripted conversation. The full interview digs deeper into how to navigate this balance in practice.

Why emotion matters in analytical buying decisions

Treasury and finance professionals pride themselves on data-driven judgment. Yet the research on buying behavior—and Guillaume's experience—shows that emotion underpins logic in ways many sellers overlook. These buyers are not "unemotional"; they are emotionally selective. They choose vendors and sales reps based on whether they feel heard, respected, and honestly advised.

The paradox: an analytical buyer is often more influenced by trust and credibility than by a cheaper price or a flashier feature set, precisely because they are analytical. They know that a treasury system is only as good as the support and relationship behind it. A technically superior product from a vendor rep who makes them feel rushed or misunderstood becomes a liability. They will choose a slightly less advanced system from a rep and company they trust, because they understand the true cost of a failed implementation.

Guillaume's episode explores this further, revealing how the shift in treasury roles over the past 20 years—from reactive operators to strategic decision-makers—has fundamentally changed what these buyers need from their vendor partners.

What specific approach helps sales managers coach senior reps on complex enterprise deals without prescribing how they should sell?

Guillaume uses a chess player mentality—thinking through scenarios of moving one piece and its potential impacts, generating multiple outcomes. Rather than telling reps exactly what to do, he coaches them to see patterns and anticipate consequences before they materialize in the field.

Why did a successful fintech sales professional transition from individual contributor to sales management?

Guillaume shifted to leadership because he enjoys working with people and helping others add value. In his sales quadrant—prospecting, building relationships, closing—he realized he could multiply his impact by enabling an entire team rather than carrying the quota himself.

What is the impact of wasting time on low-probability deals instead of focusing on opportunities more likely to close?

Most sales professionals spend less than half their calendar time on deals that either close or have a high likelihood of closing, meaning roughly 50 percent of their effort goes to deals with poor outcomes. Redirecting that wasted time to high-probability deals or deals requiring genuine nurturing compounds results dramatically.
