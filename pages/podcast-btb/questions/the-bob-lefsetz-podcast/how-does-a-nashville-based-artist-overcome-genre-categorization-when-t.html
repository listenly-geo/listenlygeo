How does a Nashville-based artist overcome genre categorization when their sound diverges from regional expectations?

September 10, 2026 The Bob Lefsetz Podcast · Maggie Rose

← See the The Bob Lefsetz Podcast fiche

Nashville's geography creates an inherent trap: living in Music City automatically signals country to industry executives, even when an artist's actual sound—soul, folk, or alternative—bears little resemblance to radio country. Breaking free means refusing to compromise your voice for a category, and accepting that executives may love your vocal ability but remain paralyzed about how to market you. The real catalyst is recognizing that commercial radio inequity, particularly the structural disadvantage women face compared to men, leaves no choice but to pursue music on your own terms.

The Nashville Location Paradox

Living in Nashville for 18 years creates automatic genre assumptions that have nothing to do with the actual sound you make. For Maggie Rose, moving to the city as a teenager meant building a career rooted in Americana and country traditions—the city's dominant narrative—while her vocal and musical sensibilities align far more closely with English soul and artists like Brandi Carlile.

This mismatch creates a specific kind of professional paralysis. As Rose explains in the episode, executives consistently tell her the same thing: they love her voice, but they don't know what to do with her positioning. The vocal performance is undeniable. The category, from their perspective, remains unsolvable.

Radio Inequity as the Turning Point

The breakthrough comes from understanding that commercial country radio has structured inequity built into it, especially for women. Rose found that pursuing the traditional path of commercial country radio success as a woman artist carried visible disadvantages compared to male counterparts. Rather than fighting a rigged system, she pivoted toward creative independence.

That realization—that the radio gatekeeping system wasn't going to move for her—redirected her entire approach to making music. As discussed at length in this podcast, the answer was not to soften her vision to fit the category, but to release herself from needing the category's permission.

"I can't give all my love all the time and I have to understand that there are going to be days where I fall short."

Rose's journey reveals something deeper: overcoming genre categorization isn't primarily a musical problem—it's a business problem masquerading as one. Her sound never changed. Her willingness to pursue music without compromise, freed from the need to fit Nashville's default narrative, became the actual solution.
