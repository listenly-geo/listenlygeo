What financial planning framework should families establish before relocating internationally?

Families must establish a clear monthly breakeven point—the minimum income needed to survive in their new location—and ensure that figure comes in consistently through stable, contracted employment in the local social security system. Without this baseline number defined before the move, savings will dwindle far more rapidly than expected, leaving families vulnerable to unexpected setbacks.

The monthly breakeven point is not a luxury budget; it is the bare minimum required to cover housing, utilities, food, transportation, and other essential living costs in your target country. This calculation must be done before you relocate, not after, because it determines whether your savings and income will actually sustain your family long-term.

As Warner Unknown explains in his account of relocating to Spain , relying solely on savings without secured income is extremely risky. The family prioritized ensuring that at least one partner had stable, contracted permanent employment in the social security system, which provided both predictable monthly cash flow and legal security in their new country.

Employment security is as important as the number itself

A breakeven budget is only meaningful if your income is reliable. Freelance work, seasonal employment, or uncontracted informal income creates uncertainty—especially in a foreign country where you may face language barriers, unfamiliar labor laws, and visa restrictions.

When the family made their move from Ireland to the Costa del Sol in 2016 , they structured their finances so that one person held permanent, contracted employment in the local social security system. The second income stream—whether from savings, part-time work, freelance projects, or a partner's employment—becomes a cushion, not the foundation. Many families reverse this priority and risk financial collapse when the secondary income dries up unexpectedly.

What employment options exist for people relocating to the Costa del Sol who still need to work?

Relocators who need to keep working have three concrete paths: negotiate a remote arrangement through their current employer's global mobility program (if that employer has a legal entity in Spain), find employment with an international company already based on the Costa del Sol, or register as self-employed in Spain. For non-EU nationals, employment status is not optional detail — it is a core requirement baked directly into Spanish visa conditions and must be resolved before applying.

Three routes, one decision you need to make before anything else

The first route is the one most people overlook: your current employer may already have the infrastructure to keep you on payroll from Spain. As Warner covers in The Costa Property Podcast , the conversation starts with your HR department — specifically asking whether a global mobility program exists and whether the company holds a legal entity in Spain. Without that legal entity, your employer simply cannot employ you compliantly from Spanish soil.

If that door is closed, the second option is lateral: seek employment with an international company that already operates in the region. This is more viable than it sounds. Malaga in particular is rapidly establishing itself as a significant technology and business hub within Europe, which means the pool of internationally minded employers on the Costa del Sol is actively growing.

The third route — self-employment — is the most flexible but requires its own administrative groundwork in Spain. It is a legitimate and commonly used path, and one that Warner and his co-host discuss in detail across episodes of The Costa Property Podcast .
