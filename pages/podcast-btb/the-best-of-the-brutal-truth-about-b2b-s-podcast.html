How do enterprise treasury and finance buyers make purchase decisions differently from traders and other personas in fintech sales?

Treasurers and directors of finance operate with analytical rigor and deep trust-orientation —not on price alone or the transactional energy of traders. They make logical decisions, but emotion and personal relationships anchor those choices, meaning process alone can kill a deal if it undermines the human connection.

Unlike the trader persona of decades past, treasury and finance leaders today are structured, disciplined professionals. This shift matters for sales teams working in fintech: the old playbook of aggressive, deal-driven energy does not land. Instead, as Guillaume explains in the episode , success comes from recognizing that these buyers are making logical choices rooted in rational analysis—but those logical choices rest on trust in the person and company behind the solution.

The decision-making pattern differs fundamentally from earlier eras. They do not default to the lowest-cost option if they lack confidence in the vendor or the relationship. This is where many fintech sales teams miss the mark: they treat treasury and finance buyers as purely data-driven, process-oriented personas, then wonder why a technically superior or cheaper product loses to a competitor. The reality is more human. These buyers want to trust the person representing the vendor, not just the product specs.

Treasury and finance professionals pride themselves on data-driven judgment. How can salespeople use analogies and metaphors to explain complex enterprise solutions?

Connect complex solutions to what your customer already knows by using familiar metaphors—like comparing portfolio options to vehicles they drive. Pair these analogies with simple, one-page summaries that can be easily shared across the organization, turning abstract enterprise concepts into concrete mental models.

Making the unfamiliar familiar through vehicle analogies

When explaining enterprise solutions, the gap between what you're selling and what your customer understands is where confusion lives. The antidote is anchoring new concepts to existing knowledge . Rather than launching into technical specifications, ask customers about their current situation—what they drive, how they operate, what matters to them—and then map your solution to those real-world experiences.

Herb Klan frequently uses vehicle analogies in his sales conversations at Cisco Systems. Instead of describing network infrastructure capabilities in isolation, he compares different portfolio options to vehicles a customer might own. A customer who drives a Ford Maverick has a different set of needs than one driving a luxury sedan—and the same logic applies to IT solutions. The analogy creates immediate recognition , making the unfamiliar feel concrete and discussable.

As explained in this episode , the power of this approach is that it doesn't require your customer to become an expert in your domain—it requires you to speak their language.

One-page summaries: simplicity as a selling tool

Analogies work best when paired with digestible formats that travel through the organization . A one-page summary is not a stripped-down version of your pitch—it's a decision-making tool. Enterprise sales involve multiple stakeholders, budget committees, and approval layers. Your customer will hand off your explanation to people you'll never meet.

Format matters here. A one-page summary using simple language and a visual structure (often reinforced with PowerPoint) ensures that when your customer explains your solution to their CFO, their IT director, or their executive sponsor, the core message survives intact. Simplicity becomes a form of credibility —if you can't explain it simply, the customer's team will assume you don't understand it either.

As discussed in the podcast episode , Herb Klan has built this into his sales methodology: analogies create the mental model, one-page documents enable the handoff, and PowerPoint reinforces the visual story. The result is a solution that can travel through an organization without losing meaning.

How should sales professionals approach complex multi-stakeholder deals across different organizational levels?

Treat every enterprise deal as a journey through five to ten stakeholders—sometimes a dozen—across multiple organizational levels . Start from the ground up: clear your proposal with financial analysts first, then move methodically through the finance manager, director, and controller before it ever reaches the CFO. Build groundswell support at each level so that by the time the decision reaches the top, no one is surprised and everyone already understands what you're proposing and how it impacts them.

The systematic bottom-up sales architecture

Enterprise deals rarely close with a single conversation at the C-suite level. Instead, you must think of your sales process as a structured hierarchy that mirrors the customer's organizational chart . This isn't a workaround—it's the blueprint for winning major deals.

The pattern is straightforward: financial analysts validate the technical and financial soundness of your solution, then pass it up through middle management layers—each one adding their own context and buy-in. As Herb Klan explains in the episode , this methodology came directly from his audit background at GE Corporate, where every finding had to clear each level of financial scrutiny before escalation was even considered.

The risk of skipping levels is catastrophic: a CFO blindsided by details their finance director didn't mention is a deal killed instantly. Surprises at the top always mean rejection .

Building groundswell: why "no surprises" is your competitive advantage

The real power of this approach lies in psychological buy-in, not just bureaucratic compliance. When the finance manager has already vetted your proposal with the director, and that director has already discussed it with the controller, the conversation with the CFO becomes a formality—a confirmation, not a pitch.

This creates what we might call "informed consensus." Each stakeholder sees how your solution impacts their domain—whether that's risk, cash flow, operational efficiency, or strategic capability. When Klan moved from audit to sales at GE Healthcare , he applied this same logic to customer-facing deals: he never let a decision-maker meet the proposal for the first time in the boardroom. They'd already been primed, educated, and aligned three levels down.

Educate Before You Sell

The instinct for most sales professionals is to lead with the solution. But when your buyer has zero frame of reference for a decision of this magnitude, that approach lands flat. The second move is to connect your solution to what actually matters to the organization. Define objective stakes —whether financial outcomes, operational improvements, clinical results, or technical performance—and then map them to the company's stated strategic priorities and mission statement.
