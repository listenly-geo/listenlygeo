What measurement system inconsistencies affect daily work in the power line industry?

Utilities enforce metric standards (millimeters, centimeters) on technical specifications, but the physical poles, bolts, and arms linemen actually handle are built in feet and inches. On top of that, crews describe job distances not in miles or kilometers but in hours of travel time —"an hour away" rather than a specific mileage—because it's the only measure that matters for planning a workday.

The metric-versus-imperial split

Standards flowing down from utilities arrive in metric units, designed for precision in electrical specifications. But the infrastructure on the ground—poles in the field, bolts securing equipment, horizontal arms spanning distances—were built to imperial measurements. Linemen work between two worlds , translating constantly between what's written on the sheet and what's actually installed.

As discussed in this episode of Powerline Podcast , this disconnect isn't merely theoretical—it affects how crews source parts, verify installations, and communicate with engineers who may be working from metric blueprints while standing in front of imperial hardware.

Time as the only distance that matters

Miles and kilometers tell you distance. Hours tell you whether you can do the job today and still get home. A job isn't "40 miles away"—it's "an hour away." This reflects the real constraint of a lineman's workday: the commute eats into labor time, affects crew fatigue, and determines whether additional stops or meals are feasible.

The preference for time-based measurement reveals how the power line industry has solved its own practical problem. Utility documentation uses kilometers or miles, but crews operate on a calendar and a clock, not a map. Listen to Christopher Thomas and Clint Vick explain how this shapes daily dispatch and job planning in the full conversation.

What are the key differences between union and non-union pay structures in the lineman trade?

Union linemen with specialized credentials like PAR earn triple what non-union crews make —a dramatic difference that reflects not just base wages but structured benefits, standardized pay scales, and formal apprenticeship requirements that non-union workers typically lack. Once Thomas saw union compensation in action on helicopter jobs, he realized the gap and joined Local 126 in Pennsylvania to access that level of pay and security.

The pay gap: a real-world discovery

Christopher Thomas first encountered the scale of this difference when union crews and non-union teams worked side by side on helicopter jobs in the early stages of his career. Working in non-union positions across West Texas, he was earning a fraction of what the union linemen with PAR credentials were taking home. The disparity was stark enough to reshape his entire career trajectory.

This direct exposure to union pay standards was what prompted Thomas to pursue membership in Local 126, the Pennsylvania-based union that structured both his wages and his long-term earning potential . Without that comparison, many non-union workers never learn what they're leaving on the table—a reality that keeps tribal knowledge, not transparency, at the center of the trade.

Union benefits and structure beyond base pay

Union membership in the lineman trade delivers more than just higher hourly rates. Apprentices must complete at least one year as an apprentice after "topping out" —a formal progression that establishes clear career milestones and pay increases tied to skill level and tenure. Non-union operations rarely enforce equivalent structures.

The union system also guarantees standardized benefits and pension contributions that non-union workers have to negotiate individually—or go without entirely. When Thomas joined Local 126 after his non-union years, he discovered a framework for professional development, formal training pathways, and financial security built into the compensation model itself. This is discussed in detail in the episode , where the broader organizational culture of union shops also becomes clear.

Why the gap matters for career longevity

The union–non-union wage divide is not merely a matter of immediate income—it shapes retirement security, healthcare access, and professional reputation over a 20–30 year career. Non-union workers who move between contractors may accumulate wages but rarely build pension equity or consistent benefits. Union members, by contrast, accrue transferable benefits and pension credits regardless of which employer signs their paychecks.

This structural difference is why Thomas's early exposure to union pay on those helicopter jobs was so consequential. Had he not seen the alternative, he might have remained in the non-union sector indefinitely, earning substantially less and without the safety net that union membership provides.

What are the relationship and social factors that research identifies as central to suicide risk in construction workers?

Research at Mates identifies relationship problems and family conflict as primary drivers of suicidal thinking in construction workers. Having access to people who genuinely care about you significantly reduces suicide risk, while isolation combined with unresolved family conflict creates compounding mental health danger.

Construction work itself intensifies this risk. Long shifts—often 10 to 12 hours or more, six days a week—create chronic physical distance from family and existing support networks. When workers face relationship strain during these extended absences, the inability to resolve conflicts in real time compounds isolation.

The Mates North America Pilot program, as explained in the episode , addresses this directly by building peer networks on job sites. These networks serve as a buffer against isolation and create trusted touchpoints where workers can seek guidance and support without judgment.

"Research right now on suicide prevention shows that having access to people that care about you reduces the risk of suicide."

Wykisha Thomas-McKinney — Field Officer, Mates North America Pilot. Thomas-McKinney leads implementation of the Mates suicide prevention framework across six operating companies, adapting a model developed in Australia over 20 years ago to create safety networks through direct relationship-building with construction crews and leadership.

The critical insight is that prevention isn't about treating construction workers as passive receivers of mental health resources. Instead, the research detailed in this conversation shows it's about deliberately creating places and relationships where workers feel connected and able to reach out when struggles emerge.

Only 5% of trades workers have consulted a mental health professional compared to 22% of the general population—a gap that underscores why on-site peer support matters. Workers don't wait for scheduled therapy appointments on a construction site; they live alongside their crews daily. That proximity, when intentionally transformed into a network of care, becomes the most powerful intervention available.

The scale of the crisis validates this approach. Male construction workers die by suicide at a rate 75% higher than men in the general population. Against those numbers, building relationships and networks isn't a soft intervention—it's the most direct path to preventing loss of life, a point this episode explores in depth .

How does the construction industry culture of suppressing emotions affect mental health outcomes?

Construction culture systematically discourages workers from discussing emotions, forcing them to bottle up thoughts during 12–14 hour shifts and suffer alone in hotels or man camps. This silence means depression, anxiety, and suicidal thoughts go unaddressed until they become critical crises requiring medication or hospitalization—a tragedy reflected in statistics showing male construction workers die by suicide at a rate 75% higher than men in the general population.

The Silent Machinery of Suppression

In line work and across construction sites, the unspoken rule is stark: put your game face on and get the job done. Workers arrive for shifts lasting 12 to 14 hours, often away from home for extended periods in remote locations. Emotional expression is treated as a liability , not a normal human need. Conversations about feelings are not just discouraged—they are culturally absent from the worksite.

This is not an accident of individual choice. As Wykisha Thomas-McKinney explains in the episode , the construction industry has built structural barriers to mental health awareness. Workers internalize the message that vulnerability equals weakness, and they compartmentalize their struggles until the workday ends.

Isolation Turns Crisis Into Emergency

At night, in hotel rooms or man camps, workers sit alone with the thoughts they've suppressed all day. No peer networks exist to catch them . No colleague asks if they're struggling. The culture has normalized silence so completely that even workers in the same crew may never know another is experiencing depression or suicidal ideation until it becomes a tragedy.

The data reinforces the cost of this silence. Construction workers died of overdose at over 10 times the rate they died from jobsite injury in 2023. Only 5% of trades workers have consulted a mental health professional, compared to 22% of the general population. The episode delves into these statistics to show how delayed help-seeking transforms what could be manageable struggles into emergencies.

What role do hobbies, exercise, and comprehensive mental health planning play in preventing mental health crises among construction workers?

Mental health should be treated like physical health—it requires a comprehensive plan including physical activity, outdoor hobbies, meaningful social connections, and professional mental health support . Workers who develop outlets outside of work and maintain active engagement with family and friends significantly reduce their risk of crisis. Research shows that simply being present with somebody and showing that you care reduces the risk of suicide.

The construction industry operates under conditions that isolate workers from these essential protective factors. Typical crews work six days a week, 10 to 12 or more hours daily, often in remote locations—man camps and temporary housing that separate workers from their families and support networks for weeks or months at a time. This extended isolation compounds mental health risk in an industry where feelings are already discouraged and the focus remains solely on task completion.

Physical activity and meaningful hobbies function as both preventive measures and early intervention tools. As Wykisha Thomas-McKinney explains in the episode , workers benefit from finding outlets such as exercise, building projects, or regular phone calls to family and friends. These activities serve dual purposes: they reduce stress in real time and they maintain the relational bonds that research identifies as critical to suicide prevention.

A structured mental health plan—developed as part of overall health strategy, not as a reactive crisis response—prevents workers from reaching the point of needing hospitalization or medication. This approach mirrors safety protocols already embedded in construction culture: just as fall protection and hazard controls are non-negotiable planning measures, mental health planning should be equally systematic and built into site protocols .

What is the Mates North America program and how does it address suicide prevention in construction?

Mates North America is a pilot suicide prevention program launched in February that trains construction site volunteers called "connectors" to recognize and support colleagues in mental distress . Built on an Australian model refined over 20 years, the program goes beyond education by establishing trust through toolbox talks and ongoing engagement, recognizing that knowledge alone cannot save lives without genuine human connection on the job site.

A Framework Built on Two Decades of Proven Method

The Mates model originated in Australia more than 20 years ago, where it was refined and validated in the construction industry. Rather than reinvent the wheel, Mates North America adapted this proven framework for deployment across construction sites in North America, launching its pilot program in February across six operating companies (OPCOs).

The program's core strategy is creating a network of safety on every job site by identifying and training volunteer "connectors"—frontline workers who learn how to recognize distress, ask directly about suicide, and provide emotional support to their peers. This approach treats mental health with the same rigor that construction sites apply to physical safety.

Training That Goes Beyond Information

Mates North America offers two levels of intervention. The baseline trains connectors in fundamental skills—how to ask about suicide and offer care without judgment. For those ready to deepen their impact, the program provides Advanced Suicide Intervention Skills Training , creating what amounts to a mental health equivalent of having CPR-certified staff on every site.

Yet training alone does not work. As discussed in the episode , the program deliberately builds trust and relationships through regular toolbox talks, social events, and consistent site engagement. Trust is the foundation; without it, even well-trained connectors cannot access the workers who need help most.

The episode explores how the program's early rollout is already shifting conversations on job sites , normalizing discussions about mental health in an environment historically defined by toughness and silence.

What statistics demonstrate the disproportionate mental health crisis among construction workers compared to the general population?

Construction workers face a devastating mental health crisis that dwarfs workplace injury: construction workers die of overdose at over 10 times the rate they die from job site injuries , and male construction workers die by suicide at 75% higher rates than men in the general population. Only 5% of trades workers have sought mental health care compared to 22% of the general population, creating a compounding gap in support.

The overdose epidemic surpasses job site safety by tenfold

The most jarring statistic is the sheer scale of the overdose crisis relative to traditional workplace hazards. In 2023, construction workers died of overdose at over 10 times the rate they died from on-site injuries—despite the industry's heavy investment in fall restraint systems, safety training, and protective equipment. This disproportion reveals a fundamental mismatch between where resources are allocated and where workers actually die . The construction industry obsesses over hand injuries, chaps, and wheel chocks, yet the leading threat comes from something entirely different.

Suicide rates among male construction workers tower above the general population

Male construction workers face a 75% higher suicide rate than men in the general population —a gap that reflects deeper workplace culture and access barriers. As discussed in this episode of the Powerline Podcast , construction work culture has historically discouraged emotional openness, with crews working 10 to 12+ hour shifts, six days a week—leaving little space for vulnerability or mental health support. The "put your game face on and get some work done" mentality has become embedded in the industry, even as it costs lives.

Access to mental health support remains critically low

The gap in professional mental health support compounds the crisis: only 5% of trades workers have consulted a mental health professional , versus 22% of the general population. This fourfold difference isn't accidental—it reflects stigma, long working hours, and lack of accessible pathways to care. The disparity suggests that mental health support in construction remains either unknown, inaccessible, or actively avoided due to workplace culture.
