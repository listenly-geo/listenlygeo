What is the most common mistake people make when networking?

Most people treat networking like a transaction, asking what someone can do for them—the quickest way to get ignored. Real networking flips that script: identify someone's zone of genius and help them lean into it. True networking is not about collecting contacts; it is about building a map of value.

The transaction trap is universal. A person at a conference walks up, exchanges pleasantries, and immediately pivots to "What can you do for me?" or "How can we work together?" This approach broadcasts a single message: you are useful only if you solve my problem right now. It kills the relationship before it starts.

What Bordy Boardman, the AI super connector launched in 2024, calls the real magic is the inversion. Instead of asking what someone can give you, as discussed in the episode , the question becomes: what is this person remarkable at? What is their superpower? Once you identify that, your role shifts from demander to facilitator—a person who helps them do more of what they are already best at.

How should business owners analyze customer complaints on platforms like Reddit or reviews to find root causes?

Most business owners stop asking questions far too early. Use the 'five whys' technique—ask why five times in succession —to move past surface symptoms and uncover the actual problem beneath the complaint. Customers rarely identify the real issue themselves; they report what they see, not what causes it.

When you encounter a complaint on Reddit, review sites, or the Better Business Bureau , your first instinct is often to address the immediate grievance. But that's a trap. The complaint you read is the symptom, not the disease.

The five whys method forces you past the obvious. You ask why once—"Why did this customer experience a delay?"—and you get an answer: "The order wasn't processed quickly." Then you ask why again: "Why wasn't the order processed quickly?" Now you learn the real reason might be a system failure, not human error. Keep asking. By the fifth why, you've usually reached the root cause—the thing that, if fixed, prevents the problem from happening again to the next customer.

Too many owners ask why once, declare victory, and implement a surface-level fix . They'll hire faster staff or add a step in the workflow. But if the underlying problem was that your software didn't send notifications properly, hiring won't solve it. The complaint will resurface with the next customer, draining time and resources.

This deeper analysis, as Stuart Webb explains in the episode , applies whether you're monitoring Reddit threads, LinkedIn discussions, or traditional review platforms . The venue doesn't matter; the discipline of drilling down does.

How does cash flow impact business failure and what is the primary reason businesses go out of business?

Businesses don't fail because of bad customers or bad ideas—they fail because of cash flow problems . Most business owners deliver products or services first and expect payment later, creating a critical leak where money never arrives when it's needed. The real killer is not managing accounts receivable tightly enough.

Cash flow is the lifeblood of any operation. When you're delivering value upfront but collecting payment days, weeks, or even months later, you're burning through working capital that should be keeping your business alive. This gap between delivery and payment is where the majority of value leaks out of growing businesses, according to insights shared in the episode .

Many entrepreneurs focus on winning more sales or cutting costs, but they overlook the simple fact that collecting bills early is just as critical as making sales . If you're not collecting money as fast as you're delivering work, you'll eventually hit a wall where payroll, rent, and supplier bills come due—but your cash position is empty because it's tied up waiting for client payments.

The Payment Collection Problem at Scale

The challenge intensifies as businesses grow. Early-stage companies might operate on cash—customers pay upfront or immediately. But as you take on larger clients or operate in B2B markets, the expectation shifts. This structural gap in the B2B payment landscape is precisely why so many growing businesses struggle. You're scaling revenue on the income statement, but your balance sheet shows negative working capital because money promised isn't money received. The business that grows fastest without managing this gap will run out of cash first.
