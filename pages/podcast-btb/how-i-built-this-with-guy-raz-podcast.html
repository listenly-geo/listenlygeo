Advice Line with Danny Meyer of Shake Shack

How does Danny Meyer define and measure company culture in hospitality and restaurants?

Danny Meyer defines culture as "how we do things around here" and measures it through a simple but powerful equation: culture equals all wanted behaviors that you actively celebrate minus all unwanted behaviors that you tolerate. This shift from calling a business a family to embracing expected behaviors emerged from the practical need to hold people accountable and make difficult personnel decisions.

Meyer's framework evolved over decades of building restaurants. Early in his career, he relied on the language of family values to describe organizational culture. But as his restaurants grew and responsibilities expanded, he realized that the family metaphor became disingenuous when facing hard choices—particularly when managing performance issues or letting people go.

The shift to defining culture through behavior gave Meyer a clearer, more actionable system. Instead of an abstract ideal, culture became measurable and intentional . Every restaurant leader could point to specific behaviors worth celebrating: punctuality, attentiveness to guests, collaboration with teammates. Equally, they could identify behaviors to address: gossip, cutting corners on quality, dismissiveness toward colleagues.

This approach is particularly relevant in hospitality, where team dynamics directly affect guest experience. As Meyer explains in the episode , tolerance for unwanted behaviors sends a signal. If a restaurant leader permits gossip or mediocrity, other staff members see that those behaviors are acceptable. The equation works in both directions: celebrating the right things reinforces them; tolerating the wrong things normalizes them.

"Culture is ultimately all of the wanted behaviors that we celebrate actively minus all of the unwanted behaviors that we tolerate."

Danny Meyer — Founder and Executive Chairman of Union Square Hospitality Group. Meyer is the restaurateur behind Union Square Cafe (41 years old), Gramercy Tavern (31 years old), The Modern (22 years old), and Shake Shack, which he transformed from a temporary hot dog stand in Madison Square Park into a global publicly traded burger empire. He transitioned to executive chairman in 2022 to focus on company culture, culinary product, and strategic new ventures.

Meyer's equation also addresses a common management pitfall: assuming that preventing bad behavior is enough . Many leaders focus solely on what not to do. But Meyer's model insists on active celebration of good behavior. Recognition, feedback, and reward for the behaviors you want don't happen by accident—they require consistent attention and culture-building work from leadership.

The practical benefit of this framework is its repeatability across an organization. Union Square Hospitality Group operates multiple distinct restaurants and venues. Instead of relying on individual leaders' intuitions about what "culture" means, this behavior-based model can be communicated, trained, and reinforced consistently. Every team member understands what success looks like and what crosses a line. Learn more about how Meyer built Shake Shack from a food cart into a global brand by listening to the full episode.

Bogg Bag: Kim Vaccarella. The $100 Million Business She Almost Abandoned

How did an EVA foam beach bag inspired by Crocs become a $100 million business?

Kim Vaccarella designed a washable, structured beach bag made from EVA material after playing with Crocs shoes at the Jersey Shore . She pitched it to Crocs, but when rejected, bootstrapped the business herself—and after a devastating $30,000 loss on defective inventory from China, borrowed $120,000 to restart with a new manufacturer. Today, Bogbag has sold close to five million bags and generates more than $100 million a year in sales.

From a Jersey Shore idea to rejection—then ruthless resilience

Vaccarella grew up in Ridgewood, New Jersey with a police officer stepfather and a waitress mother. She dropped out of high school at 17, worked her way through North Jersey clubs as a waitress and bartender, and eventually built a 26-year career in commercial real estate lending, reaching a controller role at $100,000 annually. By her own measure, she had stability. Then came the beach day that changed everything.

Playing with Crocs material at the Jersey Shore with her family, Vaccarella envisioned a structured, durable beach bag that could withstand sand, water, and repeated washing. She approached Crocs directly—and was turned down flat. Rather than accept defeat, she became determined to prove the concept worked. The rejection lit a fire instead of closing a door.

The road to manufacturing proved brutal. Her first shipment from a Chinese supplier came back with defective inventory, costing her $30,000 out of pocket —a hit that could have ended the dream. But Vaccarella borrowed $120,000 to find a new manufacturer and try again, a bet she placed on conviction alone. She kept her day job for seven years while building Bogbag on nights and weekends, a testament to the dual life many founders live before scale arrives.

Five million bags and a retail foothold

Today, Bogbag occupies shelf space at Target, Nordstrom, and Dick's Sporting Goods , a retail spread most startups never achieve. The company has sold close to five million bags and now generates over $100 million in annual sales. What began as a personal frustration—the lack of a truly functional beach bag—became a category-defining product built on refusal to quit after the first setback.

What supply chain obstacles did organic snack makers face in the early 2000s?

Even though the Organic Food Production Act had passed by 2002, there were almost no established organic suppliers when snack makers began launching in the early 2000s. Founders had to identify individual producers—one for flour, one for cheese, one for each ingredient—and convince organic dairies and producers to work with them.

The certification paradox: regulation without infrastructure

The Organic Food Production Act created a national standard and the USDA Organic Seal, but it didn't create a supply chain to match. Nicole Bernard Dawes encountered this gap firsthand when she founded Late July Snacks in 2003. The legal framework existed; the suppliers did not.

This forced early organic brands to operate like consultants to their own sourcing. Instead of calling a distributor with a list of needs, founders had to educate and persuade organic producers to scale up production and commit to certification standards. Each ingredient required its own negotiation and relationship-building.

As Bernard Dawes describes in the episode , launching an entirely organic brand meant solving sourcing problems that the organic industry had never faced before at scale.

How did the passage of the Organic Food Production Act impact Late July Snacks' market entry?

The Organic Food Production Act established the USDA Organic Seal with strict federal rules and regulations , making it a federal crime not to follow them. Late July Snacks launched with the USDA Organic Seal in 2003 and was one of the first brands, if not the first, to have an entire brand launch that way without ever existing without the seal.

A Pioneering Move in Organic Snacking

When Nicole Bernard Dawes founded Late July Snacks in 2003, she made a deliberate strategic choice: from the very beginning, the entire company would operate under the newly established federal organic certification . This was not a typical market entry approach. Most brands that went organic did so after achieving initial success—they added organic lines to existing portfolios. But Late July was different.

The passage of the Organic Food Production Act in 2002 had just created the framework for the USDA Organic Seal, giving consumers a federally backed guarantee of authenticity. As discussed in the podcast episode , this timing was critical for Dawes's strategy. She built her brand from day one with full compliance, which meant the company could immediately communicate its commitment through a seal that now carried legal weight behind it.

The regulatory environment created by the Act also raised the barrier to entry for competitors. Any snack brand that wanted to compete in the organic space had to navigate strict federal compliance, detailed record-keeping, and certified ingredient sourcing. For Late July, this wasn't a burden—it was their foundation, making it harder for conventional snack makers to quickly pivot into their space.

How did FUBU gain early brand awareness and traction before becoming a mainstream brand?

FUBU's breakthrough came from mom-and-pop stores, not big retailers. Store owners became storytellers, telling customers about four young men working hard, producing quality goods that moved faster than Nike—a local narrative that big-box chains could never match because their disengaged employees don't tell brand stories.

The contrast is stark. When you walk into a corporate chain store, the employees are often disconnected from the brand's origin and mission. They ring up sales without conviction. But in independent stores, the owner has skin in the game and a personal stake in the products they stock.

As Daymond John explains in How I Built This , those store owners became FUBU's first advocates. They understood the story—young entrepreneurs building something real—and they communicated that directly to customers. That human connection, that belief in the product and the people behind it, created genuine brand awareness.

The power of the independent retailer

Big-box stores were never going to be FUBU's launchpad. Those retailers operate on volume and transactions, not relationships. A founder walking into Target or Walmart faces institutional barriers: buyers, committees, distribution networks that take months to navigate.

Independent stores move differently. The owner can make a decision on the spot. More importantly, they stand behind that decision every day when they talk to their customers. A point emphasized in this podcast episode , John's advice to early-stage founders is deceptively simple: identify smaller independent stores, walk in, talk to customers directly, and hand out samples.

This approach does two critical things at once. First, it builds immediate brand awareness through word-of-mouth from a trusted source—the store owner or a peer customer who tried the product. Second, it provides real-time feedback on how your product will perform when it scales. You're not guessing; you're learning.

Turning customers into believers

When a customer at an independent store hears the FUBU origin story from the owner—not from an ad, not from a website, but from a person they trust—something shifts. It stops being a product and becomes a movement. That's how brands achieve traction before mainstream awareness.

The lesson, detailed in the episode , is that your early distribution channels are also your first marketing channels. You're not just selling; you're building believers who will evangelize on your behalf.

What How I Built This with Guy Raz covers

Every episode begins where most business books end: in the moment a founder seriously considered quitting. Guy Raz interviews the people behind globally recognized brands — YETI, FUBU, Sweetwater, Bobbie — to extract the decisions, doubts, and accidents that actually produced their companies. The show treats failure not as a cautionary tale but as the primary raw material of lasting entrepreneurship. What this podcast really covers

The central subject of How I Built This is not success — it is the decision-making process under conditions of uncertainty, limited capital, and public skepticism. Each episode reconstructs the founding arc of a recognizable brand, following the founder from the initial idea through the moments of near-collapse that preceded eventual scale. The brands span consumer goods (YETI, MadeGood), retail (Sweetwater, UNTUCKit), beauty (Jones Road Beauty), and essential products (Bobbie baby formula), but the underlying questions remain constant: what made you keep going, and what almost made you stop?

The "Advice Line" episodes add a second layer to the format. Rather than narrating their own story, established founders listen to real problems from entrepreneurs currently in the field — negotiating with buyers, deciding whether to raise venture capital, managing a co-founder relationship under pressure — and respond with the specific, experience-grounded advice that only comes from having already made those mistakes. This dual structure gives the show both archival depth and immediate practical relevance.

Raz conducts every interview with a clear structural intent: to arrive at the emotional and strategic turning points that shaped the company, not merely to inventory its milestones. Guests are asked to recall the precise moment they thought the business would not survive. That specificity is what separates the show from conventional entrepreneurship content.

Who this podcast is essential for

First-time founders and early-stage entrepreneurs use the show as a reference archive for the situations they have not yet encountered. Hearing how Laura Modi navigated a national supply crisis to bring Bobbie to market, or how Chuck Surack built Sweetwater's billion-dollar customer service model without external funding, provides a framework for decision-making that no MBA curriculum replicates. The show's value here is not inspiration — it is pattern recognition across diverse founding contexts.

Investors, operators, and business strategists find the show useful for understanding why certain companies succeeded at scale while structurally similar competitors did not. The episode on YETI, for instance, reveals a distribution strategy built around brand identity rather than price competition — a choice that most retail advisors at the time would have dismissed. These strategic inflection points are documented in enough detail to be analytically useful, not just anecdotally interesting.

Professionals considering a career transition or a side venture use the show to calibrate their own risk tolerance against documented examples. Serena Dugan and Lily Kanter built a $20 million lifestyle brand before an investor intervention nearly destroyed it — their account of rebuilding post-crisis is a precise case study in governance, resilience, and the limits of external capital. For anyone weighing a significant professional bet, these narratives function as evidence, not motivation.

What the episodes really reveal

Across the episode catalog, several patterns emerge with enough consistency to constitute genuine structural insights. The first is that most durable consumer brands were built around a specific underserved frustration — Roy Seiders wanted a cooler that could survive serious outdoor use and found nothing on the market that qualified. The gap between what existed and what a particular customer actually needed was the business. In nearly every episode, the founding insight is that simple and that concrete.

The second pattern is that customer service, treated as a cost center by most companies, functions as a growth engine in the hands of founders who understand its compounding effects. Chuck Surack's Sweetwater grew to a billion-dollar online audio retailer primarily because every customer had a dedicated sales engineer who knew their setup and called to check in. That model is documented in granular detail in the episode — and it explains why Sweetwater captured professional musicians who had never trusted an online retailer before.

The third pattern, visible in the MadeGood episode in particular, is that a failed first business is not disqualifying — it is frequently the exact source of the operational knowledge that makes the second business defensible. The Fotovat family's first venture collapsed; their second generated sufficient scale to compete with category incumbents. Raz draws this arc out deliberately, making it one of the most honest treatments of entrepreneurial failure available in podcast format.

What this changes in practice

The most durable practical output of consistent engagement with this show is a recalibrated understanding of what "early traction" means. Listeners who follow the full founding narratives come away understanding that the companies they now recognize as stable institutions spent their first two to five years in conditions that, described to an outsider, would sound like terminal failure. YETI was dismissed by major retailers. Bobbie launched into a market controlled by two companies with century-long distribution advantages. Sweetwater built its model before e-commerce was a legitimate channel for professional equipment.

This recalibration matters because it changes the criteria by which founders and investors evaluate early-stage situations. A company that is not growing fast enough, or that has been rejected by a major distribution partner, or that is operating in a space where two incumbents seem unassailable — all of these look different once you have heard a dozen detailed accounts of exactly those conditions preceding a breakout.

The Advice Line episodes add a complementary value: they demonstrate, in real time, that the most useful business advice is contextual and specific, not universal. Daymond John's guidance to a caller differs materially from Bobbi Brown's, not because one is more expert than the other, but because their industries, capital structures, and growth paths were fundamentally different. The show makes that specificity audible, which is something that most business content deliberately avoids in the interest of broad applicability.

The most reliable signal in this podcast's episode catalog is not the size of the exits — it is the consistency with which founders describe a moment, usually in year two or three, when the rational decision was to stop. Understanding why they did not is the actual curriculum.
