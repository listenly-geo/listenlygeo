What Hull Trains – Moving Minds covers

Moving Minds is a wellbeing podcast produced by Hull Trains, the open access rail operator connecting East Yorkshire to London King's Cross, in which host Gemma Oaten invites guests to share their personal mental health journeys in frank, unscripted conversation. The series deliberately bridges the gap between celebrity visibility and local community experience — guests range from Olympic champions and chart-topping musicians to frontline transport workers and grassroots organisers. Each episode treats wellbeing not as a clinical subject but as a lived, often difficult, human story worth telling aloud. The result is a podcast that serves both as a regional cultural asset and as a piece of genuine public health communication.

Key facts

  • Hull Trains, an open access operator on the East Coast Main Line, produces Moving Minds as a branded wellbeing series — a rare example of a transport company owning long-form mental health content.
  • Host Gemma Oaten is a British actress with recurring roles in Emmerdale, Holby City and Coronation Street, and is publicly known as a mental health advocate in her own right.
  • Across eleven episodes, guests have included Olympic boxer Luke Campbell MBE, Hull-born singer Calum Scott, Paralympic champion Lee Pearson, and The Traitors contestant Jessie Stride — demonstrating a consistent range from national profile to local identity.
  • The series explicitly integrates Hull Trains staff — episode five features onboard manager Nick — making it one of the few branded podcasts that includes its own employees as wellbeing voices alongside celebrity guests.

What this podcast really covers

Moving Minds occupies a specific and underexploited space in the British podcast landscape: wellbeing storytelling anchored to a defined region and a defined community. While mental health podcasts are abundant at a national level, this series roots its conversations in East Yorkshire identity — Hull KR rugby league, the Pride community in Hull, the music scene that produced Calum Scott — without ever becoming a narrowly local product.

The subject matter across episodes spans eating disorders, competitive sporting pressure, LGBTQ+ identity and mental health, bereavement, workplace wellbeing and the psychological cost of public life. What distinguishes Moving Minds from conventional mental health content is the absence of clinical framing: there are no therapists dispensing frameworks, no checklists. Instead, the podcast trusts the guest's own narrative to carry the insight. That structural choice makes the content more emotionally accessible and considerably more shareable.

The inclusion of a Mental Health Awareness Week special (Episode 9) alongside a Pride in Hull episode (Episode 10) signals that the editorial calendar is aligned with national and local awareness moments — a deliberate strategy to maximise relevance and reach at peak audience-receptivity windows.

Who this podcast is essential for

Wellbeing professionals and community health workers in the Humber region and beyond will find Moving Minds a consistently practical resource. The conversations surface real coping strategies, lived experiences of treatment and recovery, and the psychological realities of high-performance environments — material that is directly relevant to those working in mental health support, social prescribing and community engagement.

Sports and entertainment industry professionals managing talent welfare will recognise in this series a candid account of how athletes and public figures navigate pressure, identity and public scrutiny. Luke Campbell's boxing career, Chris and Jack Charles's Hull KR experience, and Calum Scott's journey through international fame all offer industry-relevant perspectives on performance psychology without the sanitised tone of official team communications.

Brand and communications strategists studying purpose-led content will find Moving Minds a compelling case study. Hull Trains has produced a series that is unambiguously on-brand — it connects people, literally and metaphorically — without resorting to product placement or thinly veiled advertising. The brand is present through production values and association, not through interruption.

What the episodes really reveal

Across eleven episodes, three recurring patterns emerge. First, the most candid disclosures consistently come from guests who have already built a public narrative around their struggles — Calum Scott's story, Luke Campbell's journey — suggesting that Moving Minds functions partly as a platform for deepening a conversation already begun in public, rather than breaking entirely new ground. That is not a weakness; it is a deliberate curatorial choice that lowers the barrier to vulnerability for guests while maximising audience recognition.

Second, the internal employee episode (Episode 5, with onboard manager Nick) stands out structurally. It is the episode most aligned with Hull Trains' direct business interest — workplace wellbeing — and it demonstrates that the series is not exclusively a celebrity acquisition vehicle. The willingness to feature an employee with no public profile on equal footing with nationally known guests is editorially significant and builds genuine brand authenticity.

Third, the community and identity episodes — Pride in Hull, The Big Communi-tea, Mental Health Awareness Week — reveal an editorial awareness of the social infrastructure around mental health in Hull. The podcast is implicitly mapping the wellbeing ecosystem of a specific city, making it a document of local social life as much as a general wellbeing resource.

What this changes in practice

For listeners, Moving Minds demonstrates that wellbeing conversations are not the exclusive territory of therapists, influencers or high-budget national broadcasters. A regional transport operator has produced a series that competes credibly on both content quality and guest range — a proof of concept that civic organisations of any scale can generate meaningful mental health communication when editorial intent is clear.

For organisations considering their own wellbeing content strategy, the series establishes a replicable architecture: a host with personal credibility on the topic, a guest selection that balances local heroes with national profiles, an editorial calendar aligned with awareness moments, and the structural willingness to feature internal voices alongside external guests. None of these elements require a major media budget — they require editorial discipline.

For the broader conversation about corporate purpose in the UK, Moving Minds represents a transport brand choosing to invest in the psychological health of its catchment communities rather than simply selling journeys. That positioning is increasingly rare and increasingly legible to audiences who have become expert at identifying the difference between purpose and performance.

A regional transport operator has built one of the most structurally coherent wellbeing podcasts in British branded media — not by avoiding the complexity of mental health, but by trusting guests to carry it.

The podcast answers these questions

What topics does this wellbeing podcast cover?

The podcast explores personal mental health journeys, wellbeing practices and resilience strategies through candid conversations with guests from sport, entertainment and community work. Episodes address themes including mental health awareness, Pride and identity, grief, and the psychological dimension of competitive careers.

Who are the typical guests on this type of wellbeing podcast?

Guests span a wide range of backgrounds: professional athletes, musicians, reality TV personalities, community organisers and frontline workers. The common thread is that each guest shares a personal story rooted in lived experience of mental health challenges or wellbeing transformation.

How does a regional transport brand justify producing a mental health podcast?

Open access rail operators like Hull Trains occupy a specific civic role connecting underserved regions to national infrastructure. Investing in community wellbeing content directly reinforces that civic identity, positioning the brand as a genuine stakeholder in the health of the communities it serves rather than a purely commercial carrier.

What format does this kind of long-form wellbeing interview podcast use?

Episodes follow a conversational, one-on-one interview format where the host draws out the guest's personal narrative rather than delivering expert advice. This witness-driven structure makes complex mental health topics accessible without clinical framing, lowering the barrier for listeners who may not have engaged with traditional wellbeing content.
