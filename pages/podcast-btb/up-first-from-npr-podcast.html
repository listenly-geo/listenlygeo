How does rainfall distribution differ across the US during El Niño years?

A divided rainfall pattern with real consequences

El Niño doesn't distribute its effects evenly across the country. The Pacific Ocean's warming has a clear geographic divide: southern states absorb excess moisture and precipitation, creating conditions ripe for severe rain events and the flooding damage they bring. Meanwhile, northern regions see precipitation pull away entirely, deepening existing water stress.

For the Intermountain West, this dual impact creates a critical vulnerability. The region was already facing drought and low snowfall patterns , and El Niño's tendency to dry out the north compounds those water supply challenges during the very years when the phenomenon dominates global weather.

This year's El Niño is particularly powerful— modern El Niños are about 36 percent stronger than their pre-industrial counterparts , which means the rainfall extremes on both sides of the country will likely be more pronounced. The southern flood risk escalates, and northern drought intensity deepens.

A pattern layered on top of climate change

Understanding this rainfall split matters because it's happening within a warming climate. The planet is already 2.5 degrees Fahrenheit hotter from fossil fuel burning , which adds heat and destabilization to whatever rainfall pattern El Niño imposes. A naturally stronger El Niño amplifies an already warmer baseline, pushing both extremes—flooding in the south and drought stress in the north—further beyond historical norms.

How does El Niño form and what are its planetary effects?

El Niño is a natural cycle where vast amounts of warm water accumulate in the Pacific Ocean around the equator, releasing substantial heat to the atmosphere. This creates far-reaching ripple effects that dramatically alter weather patterns worldwide—causing severe droughts and lack of rain in some regions while bringing extreme rainfall and flooding in others.

A planetary cycle with widespread disruption

El Niño occurs naturally as the Earth cycles through different climate states. When warm water builds up in the equatorial Pacific, it fundamentally disrupts normal atmospheric circulation patterns. The heat released into the atmosphere doesn't stay confined to the ocean—it travels across the globe.

The effects are already visible across continents. In 2026, this year's El Niño event has transformed weather from Germany to India, disrupting farming communities and water supplies. As explained in the episode , this unusually powerful version has already shattered temperature records and caused significant crop failures.

Modern El Niños are getting stronger

What makes today's El Niño particularly concerning is its intensity. Modern El Niños are approximately 36 percent stronger than those observed in the pre-industrial era. This amplification reflects how human-caused climate change is altering natural systems.

The planet has already warmed by 2.5 degrees Fahrenheit from fossil fuel burning, and this baseline warming is supercharging the natural El Niño cycle. The combination creates more extreme weather swings—deeper droughts in some zones, more catastrophic rainfall in others. A point explored further in Up First from NPR , this phenomenon is just beginning to unfold.

The regions most vulnerable include the Intermountain West and the Galapagos, where El Niño's effects are historically severe. Yet its reach is truly planetary—no continent remains untouched by the cascading atmospheric changes it triggers.

How are beavers being used to fight wildfires in the United States?

Beavers have been released into fire-damaged areas of Washington state following wildfires across the American West. Research shows that landscapes with beaver activity retain significantly more water — and that higher moisture makes those areas measurably more resilient to fire. Beaver reintroduction has become an emerging, low-cost tool in wildfire mitigation strategy.

Water retention, not firefighting — the mechanism that makes it work

Beavers do not fight fires directly. Their value lies in what they build: dams that slow water flow, raise the water table, and saturate the surrounding land. The result is a wetter, greener landscape that is far harder for wildfire to consume.

This principle — that beaver activity fundamentally changes the hydrology of a watershed — is the same logic driving reintroduction programs in Britain, as NPR reporter Lauren Frayer documented in Up First from NPR . The connection between beavers, water retention, and fire resilience crosses continents.

In Washington state, authorities acted on this evidence after wildfires swept through. Beavers were released into burned zones specifically to accelerate ecological recovery and reduce vulnerability to future fire events.

A fraction of the cost — and probably better results

One of the most striking aspects of beaver-led land management is the cost comparison. In Britain, engineering solutions to manage water at a single site were estimated at hundreds of thousands to over one million pounds. Beavers solved the same problem at a fraction of that cost — and with lasting ecological benefit, as Frayer reported in this episode of Up First .

The same economic logic applies to wildfire mitigation. Conventional firebreaks and water infrastructure are expensive to build and maintain. A beaver colony, by contrast, continuously engineers its own habitat — at no additional cost once established.

Why are some Scottish farmers opposed to the reintroduction of beavers?

Scottish farmers argue that beavers dam irrigation channels, flood food crops, collapse riverbanks, and fell centuries-old trees. On top of that, Scottish law makes it illegal to disassemble a beaver dam on private property if it has been in place for two weeks or more — leaving farmers with no practical recourse over what happens on their own land.

Kate Maitland and the National Farmers Union: concrete damage, not abstract concern

Kate Maitland, representing the National Farmers Union in Scotland, is among the most prominent voices laying out the agricultural case against beaver reintroduction. The concerns she articulates are not theoretical. Beavers build dams instinctively and prolifically — and those dams can redirect or block the irrigation channels that farmers depend on to manage water across their fields.

The consequences include flooded food crops and destabilized riverbanks. Beavers also fell trees, including specimens that have stood for centuries. These are real, recurring losses that hit working farms directly, as Lauren Frayer documents in this episode of Up First from NPR .

A law that strips farmers of agency on their own property

The legal dimension sharpens the conflict considerably. Under Scottish law, it is illegal to disassemble a beaver dam on private property once it has been in place for two weeks or more. Two weeks is not a long time. A dam built over a single weekend can, within a fortnight, become legally untouchable.

This means a farmer who discovers a dam blocking a drainage channel cannot simply remove it without risking a legal penalty — even on land they own. The rule was designed to protect a newly reintroduced species, but its effect is to transfer control of private agricultural land to a wild animal and the state. Who were Millie and Bjornar and what role did they play in Britain's beaver reintroduction?

Millie and Bjornar were two Norwegian beavers relocated by wildlife officials in 2009 to Napdale Forest, a temperate rainforest in western Scotland, as part of Britain's official beaver reintroduction program. They became the founding pair of the modern British beaver population — described by scientists as the "Adam and Eve" of the species in Britain — and went on to produce more than a dozen offspring, known as kits.

Their story, however, took an unexpected turn. Scientists later discovered that Millie and Bjornar were not just a founding pair — they were also father and daughter. The revelation prompted authorities to import additional beavers from Norway, Germany, and Poland in order to diversify the gene pool and ensure the long-term viability of the reintroduced population. Their complicated family history aside, Millie and Bjornar remain the biological cornerstone of every beaver now living wild in Britain. Beavers had been absent from Britain since the early 20th century, and by the time Millie and Bjornar were selected for relocation, only around 1,200 beavers remained across Europe and northern Asia combined. Their placement in Napdale Forest was carefully chosen, and within years their engineering instincts — damming, channeling, and reshaping waterways — began transforming the landscape in ways that would later attract serious attention from climate scientists and flood management authorities.

Why did beavers go extinct in Britain and when did it happen?

Beavers were hunted to extinction in Britain by the early 20th century. Humans pursued them relentlessly for three distinct resources: their waterproof fur, their meat, and their anal secretions — a substance called castoreum — which was prized in medieval perfumes for its vanilla-like scent and consumed by Romans for its supposed medicinal properties. By the time the last British beavers disappeared, only approximately 1,200 individuals remained across all of Europe and northern Asia.

The demand for castoreum illustrates just how comprehensively beavers were exploited. Far from being hunted simply for food or pelts, they were targeted for nearly every part of their body, making population recovery essentially impossible before legal protection existed. The scale of that collapse — from widespread presence across Britain to complete local extinction — took centuries of sustained hunting pressure to achieve.

Castoreum is a substance secreted by the anal glands of beavers. In the medieval period, it was harvested for use in perfumes due to its vanilla-like scent. Romans also consumed it believing it had medicinal properties. This demand made beavers targets not just for their pelts and meat, but for their internal secretions — contributing directly to their extinction across Britain.

It was only in 1992 that the European Union passed legislation requiring member states to investigate species driven extinct by human activity and explore reintroduction programs — the legal trigger that eventually brought beavers back. The first step came in 2009, when two Norwegian beavers named Millie and Bjornar were relocated to Napdale Forest in western Scotland, marking Britain's first beaver reintroduction in roughly 400 years. NPR's Lauren Frayer reported the full story for Up First , traveling across Britain to document how these animals — once erased by human greed — are now being actively recruited to solve the flooding crises caused by climate change.

What are beavers and why are they considered important for climate adaptation in Britain?

Beavers are semi-aquatic rodents — the second largest rodent in the world after capybaras — with webbed back feet and flat tails that they use to build dams. In Britain, their ability to dam rivers and retain water has made them effective natural flood-control engineers, capable of transforming small rivers into vast wetlands and acting as a buffer against the increasingly erratic weather caused by climate change.

After being hunted to extinction in Britain in the early 20th century — at a point when only approximately 1,200 beavers remained across all of Europe and northern Asia — they were reintroduced starting in 2009, when the first two Norwegian beavers, Millie and Bjornar, were relocated to Napdale Forest in western Scotland. That reintroduction was triggered in part by a 1992 EU law requiring member states to investigate human-caused extinctions and explore bringing species back.

The results have been striking. In Ealing, West London, a 20-acre park formerly used as a golf course was transformed into a water-retaining wetland within weeks of beavers arriving at the Ealing Beaver Project . The site had previously suffered persistent flooding at a nearby London Underground station — a problem that costly engineering solutions had failed to fix cheaply. Beavers solved it at a fraction of the estimated hundreds of thousands to over one million pounds that conventional flood mitigation would have required.

What the science calls it Beavers are described in this episode as "wetland engineers" — animals whose dam-building behavior naturally slows and retains water flows, creating wetland ecosystems from ordinary river channels. This process, which Britain hadn't seen for 400 years, is now being deliberately harnessed as a low-cost, high-impact climate adaptation tool. "The beavers can do it, probably a fraction of the cost, probably better than we can, certainly more sustainably for the environment."

What Up First from NPR covers

Up First condenses the three most consequential news stories of the day into a 10-minute briefing, released before most Americans start their morning commute. The show spans domestic politics, foreign policy, economics, public health, and social affairs — delivering not just headlines but context and analysis from NPR's national reporting team. Available seven days a week, it adapts its format to the news cycle: fast and structured on weekdays, more expansive on Sundays. It is built for listeners who need to stay informed without sacrificing the depth that wire headlines alone cannot provide.

Key facts

  • Up First publishes new episodes weekdays at 6:30 a.m. ET, Saturdays at 9 a.m. ET, and Sundays by 8 a.m. ET — seven days a week without interruption.
  • Each weekday episode covers exactly three stories in approximately 10 minutes, a fixed format designed for predictable daily consumption.
  • The podcast rotates among four weekday hosts — Leila Fadel, Steve Inskeep, Michel Martin, and A Martinez — with Ayesha Rascoe and Scott Simon anchoring weekend editions.
  • Sunday episodes adopt a different editorial structure: a single long-form story examined in depth by Ayesha Rascoe under the banner "The Sunday Story."

The show draws on NPR's full network of correspondents and bureaus across the United States and internationally. Explore every episode of Up First from NPR.

What this podcast really covers

Up First is a news briefing built on editorial selection, not aggregation. Each episode opens with an implicit editorial judgment: these three stories, out of everything happening in the world today, are the ones that matter most. That act of curation — repeated 260-plus times a year — is itself a statement about how NPR's editorial leadership reads the American news landscape.

Recent episodes illustrate the range: U.S.-Iran nuclear negotiations and their diplomatic stalemate, primary election results from the Midwest, a worsening black lung crisis in Appalachian coal communities, federal immigration detention policy and its financial beneficiaries, inflation data releases and their economic implications, and the downstream effects of a proposed Hollywood studio merger. The show does not specialize. It generalizes deliberately, treating informed citizenship as a form of professional competence.

Politics — particularly federal executive action, legislative dynamics, and electoral contests — occupies the largest share of airtime. Foreign policy follows closely, especially when U.S. military or diplomatic stakes are involved. Health, environment, and social justice stories appear consistently but are rarely the lead. The pattern reveals a program calibrated to the news priorities of educated, politically engaged American adults.

Who this podcast is essential for

Three audiences return to Up First with the most consistency, each for structurally different reasons.

Policy and public affairs professionals use the show as an ambient briefing layer — not a replacement for deep reading, but a 10-minute orientation that surfaces which issues have broken into mainstream visibility overnight. For anyone whose work intersects with regulation, legislation, or public opinion, knowing what 30 million NPR listeners are hearing each morning carries its own strategic value.

Business leaders and executives who operate in regulated industries, trade-exposed sectors, or geopolitically sensitive markets treat the show as a low-cost, high-reliability signal scanner. An episode covering Iranian nuclear talks, U.S. missile stockpile concerns, or a Senate race in Michigan translates directly into risk and opportunity framing for sectors ranging from energy to defense contracting to financial services.

Educators, journalists, and researchers find the show useful not only for its content but for its editorial model: how NPR decides to frame, sequence, and contextualize complex stories within 10 minutes is itself an object of professional study in communication-adjacent fields.

What the episodes really reveal

Analyzing the titles and subjects of recent episodes reveals several structural patterns that go beyond daily news selection.

First, the show consistently pairs domestic electoral politics with a foreign policy development — primary results alongside Iran negotiations, Senate races alongside international arms supply questions. This pairing is not accidental: it reflects an editorial view that American political outcomes and international events are co-determinant, and that listeners should hold both in mind simultaneously.

Second, recurring issue threads — Iran's nuclear posture, Midwest electoral dynamics, U.S. immigration enforcement economics — surface across multiple episodes weeks apart, suggesting the program functions as a longitudinal tracker of slow-moving stories, not merely a reactor to breaking news. A listener following consistently across months gains an interpretive frame unavailable to someone who reads only single-day headlines.

Third, structural inequality stories (the black lung crisis in Appalachia, sexual abuse at elite institutions, immigration detention as a profit center) appear regularly amid the dominant political coverage. Their presence signals a deliberate editorial commitment to accountability journalism that exists alongside, rather than subordinate to, the horse-race political coverage that dominates cable news competitors.

What this changes in practice

The discipline of a 10-minute daily briefing changes how its regular listeners relate to complexity. Rather than waiting for a major investigative piece or a weekly digest, Up First conditions listeners to expect incremental updates — partial information, evolving negotiations, contested data — presented with enough context to be actionable without being definitive.

For professionals whose work requires tracking regulatory shifts, geopolitical developments, or social trends, this daily rhythm builds a cumulative model of the world that is more accurate than any single long read. The show's format implicitly trains its audience in holding uncertainty: Iran talks "drag," missile stockpiles are "running low," and a new attorney general arrives amid contested political conditions. These are not resolved stories; they are open situations requiring ongoing attention.

Practically, the show rewards habitual, sequential listening over sporadic dipping. Its value compounds. A professional who listens daily for a quarter develops a mental map of active U.S. policy fronts, electoral dynamics, and international pressure points that a once-a-week listener simply cannot replicate. The 10-minute format is the mechanism; the compounding knowledge base is the actual product.

A daily news briefing that selects three stories out of thousands is not neutral curation — it is editorial argument made audible, repeated every morning, at scale.
