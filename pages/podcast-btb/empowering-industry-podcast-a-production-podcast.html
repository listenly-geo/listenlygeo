What ethical principle from Elemar McKay's engineering motto guides professional integrity in technical roles?

In engineering, integrity and honesty are limited resources you introduce only once in your career . If you do not know something, you must say so and find out—misrepresenting knowledge erodes the trust that is essential when your designs affect people's lives.

This principle, learned from foundational Hydro engineer Elemar McKay, sits at the foundation of engineering ethics. The core idea is simple but unforgiving: once your reputation for honesty is damaged, it cannot be fully restored . In a profession where decisions directly impact human safety, that loss of trust becomes catastrophic.

The distinction matters. Other professions can often recover from lapses in credibility or recover from limited misjudgments. Engineering is different. As Glenn Powell explains in the episode , when engineers design systems affecting people's lives—whether it's a pump in a critical infrastructure application or a pressure relief valve in a hazardous environment—perfect honesty is not a luxury, it is a necessity.

The practical implication is direct: admit uncertainty rather than guess . If you lack knowledge in a specific area, the ethical response is not to bluff your way through or present partial understanding as complete expertise. Instead, acknowledge the gap explicitly and commit to finding the answer. This builds credibility rather than eroding it.

"Your integrity and your honesty is kind of a limited resource, and you only ever have the chance to introduce yourself once in your career."

Glenn Powell — Senior Engineer at Hydro, where he has worked for 13 years across the reliability services group, pump test lab operations, and global engineering initiatives. This principle also reflects a deeper truth about engineering culture. Knowledge builds on previous work—if the foundation is dishonest, everything built above it becomes unstable. A junior engineer who hides a knowledge gap may pass that problem to colleagues downstream, creating hidden risks. A senior engineer who misrepresents expertise can lead entire teams off course.

The logic extends to team dynamics. In discussions on how Hydro approaches complex projects , this ethic shows up constantly: engineers who will openly say "I don't know, but let me research this" are trusted far more than those who speculate or overstate their capabilities. Trust enables collaboration. Without it, teams fragment into silos of defensive experts.

Why this matters more in engineering than elsewhere

Engineering is one of the few professions where a dishonest decision doesn't just affect a business outcome—it can directly harm people. A financial advisor who misrepresents market risk causes monetary loss. An engineer who misrepresents system capacity or material properties can cause injury or death. This asymmetry makes honesty non-negotiable, not aspirational.

It also means the standard for integrity is higher because the stakes are higher. You can often rebuild professional relationships in other fields through apologies, corrections, or time. In engineering, some decisions are irreversible—they're built into steel, concrete, or hydraulic systems that will operate for decades. Once you've cut corners or misled colleagues on a design, that mistake might be discovered only when the system fails in the field.

For anyone building a career in technical fields, this principle from Elemar McKay is worth internalizing early. What is the Centaur system and how does wireless condition monitoring improve pump maintenance?

The Centaur is a wireless condition monitoring system that uses vibration data to diagnose machine health in real time. Instead of traditional route-based monitoring with only two data points per year, the Centaur system delivers 24 data points every day by streaming updated vibration readings every hour, or every five minutes if necessary, enabling remote access via phone or laptop for immediate maintenance decisions.

This shift from annual or semi-annual inspections to continuous hourly monitoring fundamentally changes how maintenance teams operate. Analysts at Hydro's development center in Chicago process the incoming vibration data and deliver customers actionable feedback about asset health before problems become critical failures.

Traditional pump maintenance relied on periodic physical visits by technicians following fixed schedules. With Centaur, the system transforms into a global network of sensors feeding live intelligence back to specialists, as Glenn Powell explains in detail in the full episode .

From scheduling-based rounds to continuous intelligence

The gap between old and new monitoring methods is stark. Traditional route-based monitoring captures data only when a technician physically visits the pump location—typically twice per year or less. This means a catastrophic mechanical failure could occur undetected for months between inspections.

Centaur eliminates that risk window. By collecting vibration signatures every single hour , the system creates a continuous timeline of machine behavior, making it possible to spot the earliest signs of bearing wear, misalignment, or imbalance before they cascade into downtime. This is particularly valuable for critical infrastructure where pump failures carry operational and financial consequences.

The system also enables a more strategic approach to maintenance planning, as discussed in the podcast , because teams can now schedule interventions based on actual machine condition rather than calendar dates alone.

What are the maintenance advantages of proactive hydrant renewal compared to reactive failure management?

Proactive hydrant maintenance works exactly like automotive oil changes— preventing failure before it happens keeps water distribution systems performing better, lasting significantly longer, and eliminates the cascade risk of fire damage during an outage. Because all internal hydrant components are accessible from the ground and designs have remained stable for 50 to 70 years, Mueller Water Products can restore aging hydrants to like-new condition with improved materials and pressure ratings, avoiding costly full replacements.

A 50-Year Maintenance Window Built Into Every Hydrant

Fire hydrants are engineered to last, but age brings invisible degradation. As discussed in the Empowering Industry Podcast episode , proactive renewal mirrors the principle behind automotive preventive maintenance—you don't wait for an engine to seize before you change the oil.

The practical advantage lies in accessibility and modularity . Unlike buried infrastructure that requires excavation or replacement, hydrant internals—seals, pistons, valves, and flow components—can be removed and restored by unscrewing the top and using basic tools. This means water utilities can restore hydrants installed in 1910 or keep those now 50 years old performing reliably without disrupting the entire water main.

Avoiding Silent Failure and Fire System Collapse

Reactive maintenance waits for hydrants to fail—corroded caps, stuck valves, weak pressure, or dry barrels during fires. At that moment, fire protection is already compromised , and emergency responders have no backup at a critical site.

Proactive renewal prevents this cascade. By rebuilding hydrants before catastrophic failure, utilities maintain full system redundancy. Field trials around 40 years of hydrant age revealed broken and corroded parts, confirming that proactive intervention at that window preserves both water flow and emergency response capability.
