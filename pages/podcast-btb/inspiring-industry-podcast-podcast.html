How is the Inflation Reduction Act signed into law in 2022 impacting Topsoe and the broader energy sector?

The IRA is a game changer for Topsoe because the company's basket of technologies in decarbonization aligns perfectly with the legislation. A perfectly aligned regulatory framework

When the Inflation Reduction Act passed in 2022, Topsoe found itself in an exceptional position. The company's portfolio of clean energy technologies—spanning sustainable aviation fuel, renewable diesel, blue ammonia, and blue hydrogen—matched almost exactly what the legislation incentivized.

As Henrik Rasmussen explains in the episode , this alignment was not accidental. Topsoe had spent years developing technologies to address the decarbonization challenge—the IRA simply created the economic incentives that turned those technologies into viable commercial projects.

The legislation accelerated project deployment significantly . By providing tax credits and investment subsidies for renewable fuels and hydrogen production, the act removed many of the financial barriers that had previously slowed commercialization. Projects that had been stuck in planning phases suddenly became economically feasible.

From licensing leadership to employment growth

Topsoe's position as a licensor of process technology and catalysts made the company central to this wave of new projects. The company doesn't build production facilities itself; it licenses its proven technologies to energy companies and industrial operators worldwide.

With the IRA in place, the majority of new renewable fuel and blue hydrogen projects being launched in the United States relied on Topsoe's licensed technology . This drove employment across the US economy—from engineering and construction during plant build-out to operations and maintenance once facilities came online.

A key detail Rasmussen explores in this podcast discussion is how different decarbonization pathways follow different timelines. Some technologies, like sustainable aviation fuel, are closer to commercial scale today; others, like green ammonia, require longer development cycles. The IRA supported this entire spectrum, creating a heterogeneous but robust market for clean energy innovation.

From military repair process to scalable manufacturing technology

Kennedy and Committery recognized that the manual military spray technique contained genuine potential, but it lacked the sophistication needed for real production environments. They understood that traditional metal 3D printing solutions were fundamentally uneconomical: the technology worked in principle, but the speed and cost barriers made it impractical for manufacturing at scale .

The pair took the core military process and engineered it into an automated system with embedded software intelligence. This wasn't simply a matter of making the existing technique faster—it was about rethinking the entire value proposition. What are the challenges payroll teams face with software compliance and testing?

Payroll software must be continuously tested and updated to embed all legislative changes —from new tax regimes to national insurance bands and operating limits—to remain compliant. Even though payroll calculations are now software-driven rather than manual, teams cannot simply set the system once; they must conduct ongoing testing to verify the software responds correctly to each regulatory shift.

The challenge runs deeper than a one-time implementation. As Jason Davenport explains in this episode , modern payroll software handles complexity at scale—yet regulatory bodies continuously introduce new thresholds, tax bands, and deduction rules that require the underlying code to be updated and validated before they take effect.

The shift from manual to automated—and the new testing burden

Before software automation, payroll professionals calculated taxes and deductions by hand, which meant they applied regulations directly as they understood them. Software automation introduced a layer of abstraction : the rules must now be encoded, and that encoded logic must be tested to ensure it behaves as the regulation demands.

Each fiscal year or regulatory change—whether HMRC alters national insurance bands or introduces new allowances—requires payroll software to be updated. Those updates are not optional luxuries; they are mandatory compliance tasks. Teams must verify that the new code correctly implements the new rules, often testing multiple scenarios to ensure edge cases are handled correctly.

This is discussed at length in the Inspiring Industry Podcast episode , where Davenport emphasizes that the responsibility for compliance has shifted: it now rests partly on software vendors to push updates, and partly on in-house payroll teams to validate those updates before they process live payroll.

Why ongoing vigilance is non-negotiable

Payroll compliance errors cascade directly to employees and regulators . A single miscalculation in tax withholding or national insurance can create arrears, penalties, and reputational damage. Because the UK alone collected £378 billion in taxes through payroll professionals in the 2022 tax year, the stakes for accuracy are extraordinarily high—both economically and legally.

Payroll teams cannot rely on "set and forget" software deployments. They must allocate time and expertise to test each legislative update, document the validation process, and ensure the system is ready before the effective date. This is a continuous cycle, not a one-off project, and Davenport's conversation underscores how often payroll professionals are overlooked despite the critical nature of this work.

This framework mirrors the certification structures of other chartered institutes for accountants and engineers, recognizing both early-career and seasoned professionals.

The membership structure reflects different career stages. Students entering the profession begin at the foundation level, while junior members are supported in their early roles. As professionals gain experience and expertise, they progress to full member status, demonstrating deeper competency and commitment to the field.

Advancement through these levels is not arbitrary. Each tier is tied to measurable experience and professional development , ensuring that those holding chartered status have demonstrated genuine mastery in payroll and compliance matters. This distinction matters significantly—chartered status carries authority and recognition within the sector, signaling that a professional has met rigorous standards.

As Jason Davenport explains in the episode , the CIPP's membership model has evolved since the organization received its Royal Chartered status in 2011. The fellow designation represents the highest tier of professional achievement within the institute. Fellow status recognizes exceptional contribution and lifetime expertise in the payroll profession, placing individuals at the apex of their field. This multi-tiered approach creates clear incentives for continuing education and professional excellence across all stages of a payroll career.
