How to Live in Everyone Else's Future (with Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke)

How has the cost and nature of software production changed with AI-generated code?

Code used to be the most expensive thing to produce, but now with AI, a lot of code that glues together infrastructure can be treated as a first draft rather than a final product . This shift allows rapid iteration and experimentation—though truly critical systems still demand the same careful engineering and oversight they always have.

The economics of software development have fundamentally shifted. Where engineers once had to write production-ready code because iteration was costly, AI-generated code now enables a different approach: ship quickly, test thoroughly, and refine. As Tobi Lütke explains in this episode , this is "extremely healthy" for how companies approach building.

Infrastructure Code and the Speed-Quality Trade-off

Not all code carries the same risk profile. Infrastructure glue—the middleware, connectors, and scaffolding that ties systems together—benefits most from this new rapid-iteration model. Generated code can serve as a credible starting point , reducing the time teams spend on boilerplate and low-risk integration work, freeing human engineers for the deeper problems.

However, this permission to iterate fast does not extend universally. Code that underpins mission-critical functions—payment systems, security boundaries, core logic that directly affects users—still requires the same level of rigor, testing, and intentionality it always did. The shift is not about lowering standards everywhere; it's about redirecting engineering effort toward the systems where human judgment and accountability matter most.

The Broader Implication: Speed Without Recklessness

The real win is not cheaper code, but faster learning . Teams can now explore more design options, prototype more aggressively, and validate ideas without weeks of engineering runway. This velocity compounds over time—companies that adapt to this new economics will ship more features, gather more user feedback, and iterate faster than those clinging to old models.

Yet the corollary is clear: organizations must develop better judgment about which code deserves scrutiny and which can be treated as provisional. This requires discipline, testing infrastructure, and a culture that respects the distinction between exploratory code and production code.

What mental framework should a technology leader use to evaluate emerging AI capabilities?

Build personal test harnesses called 'evals' — structured folders of prompts with expected and judged results — that you run against every new AI model to identify exactly where its capabilities end and what thresholds it has crossed. This approach gives you a repeatable, systematic method to benchmark each model release against your previous tests and determine whether the technology has genuinely advanced.

Building a systematic evaluation framework

Instead of vaguely testing an AI model or relying on marketing claims, create a real testing apparatus. Think of your evals as batch jobs that repeatedly test the same capabilities and judge the results consistently — much like how unit tests transformed software development in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

The premise is simple: write a set of prompts that probe specific capabilities, define what a correct answer looks like, and then run every new model against that same set. This removes guesswork and emotion from evaluation.

As Lütke explains in the podcast , the core insight is that software now exists that no human wrote — and the only way to truly understand it is to interview it systematically. That interview process is your eval framework.

Testing for capability edges and thresholds

The real value of personal test harnesses is that they help you spot the exact moment a new model crosses into a capability threshold you care about. When Claude or GPT-5 arrives next month, you don't start from scratch — you run it against your existing evals to see what's changed.

This approach also surfaces the edges: where a model is still limited, what it still fails at, and what you still can't reliably ask it to do. That knowledge becomes actionable for product teams building with AI, as detailed in this episode of ACQ2 by Acquired.

The Insane Productivity of Andrew Ross Sorkin

What was Andrew Ross Sorkin's business venture before joining the New York Times full-time?

At age 15, Sorkin created Sports Page Magazine at Scarsdale High School , publishing sports journalism by high school students and distributing it across the country through high schools. He spent roughly a year selling full-page ads at $900 to $1,200 apiece, before the venture collapsed by age 18 due to operational missteps—expensive Federal Express shipping for mailings and severe miscalculations on printing costs with the same printer that produced Wired magazine.

This wasn't just a school project—it was a genuine business model targeting an audience of young male readers through advertising, with circulation happening at a national scale. The operation had real revenue streams and real customers, yet it demonstrated an early lesson that Sorkin would learn before even entering the New York Times at age 18 in 1995: execution and unit economics matter as much as the core idea.

The venture's collapse taught Sorkin a critical lesson about operational discipline that would shape how he approached every project afterward. In the episode, Sorkin reflects on how his early mistakes—underestimating shipping and printing expenses—forced him to understand that even brilliant distribution ideas fail without rigorous cost control.

Federal Express (now FedEx) shipping was a significant operational cost in the mid-1990s for bulk magazine distribution, especially compared to today's digital-first publishing. Sorkin's reliance on this for nationwide distribution of physical copies was one of the core expenses that stretched his margins and contributed to the venture's failure.

What makes this story particularly striking is the contrast with Sorkin's later success. When he launched DealBook in 2001—over a decade before Substack and 15 years before the rise of newsletter culture —he brought these hard-won lessons in cost structure and scalability. That venture, however, succeeded spectacularly: an email newsletter rather than a physical magazine, no Federal Express bills, and ultimately growing to over one million subscribers across 25 years.

What is the role of junior employees and 'jilted' insiders in uncovering major business stories?

The best stories rarely come from senior executives protecting their interests. Instead, they originate from people who have lost something and have no remaining stake in the game —what Andrew Sorkin calls the "jilted somebody." These are junior employees or insiders who lost an auction, were passed over for a major client or deal, or saw years of work stolen by a competitor. With nothing left to protect, they become willing sources.

Sorkin identifies a clear pattern in investigative reporting: great stories begin at very junior levels , not in corner offices. A junior banker who spent months cultivating a client relationship only to watch Goldman Sachs win the account feels betrayed. A business development executive who lost a critical merger to a rival has motive to talk. These individuals are far more inclined to leak information or provide insights to reporters than someone still playing the game and worried about their career.

The mechanism is psychological. When someone has exhausted their options within the system—when they have been rejected, overlooked, or beaten—the only remaining source of control is the ability to upset the situation. For a reporter, this makes them ideal sources. As Sorkin explains in the episode , identifying who has lost something is as important as identifying who has won.

"I think it's important to understand what their motivation is. But ultimately, as long as I can confirm the information typically from two, three, four different sources, or I can get documentation of the issue, I try not to worry too much about what their ultimate incentives are."

This dynamic has powered some of the most significant financial and corporate exposés in recent decades, and it remains one of the most reliable patterns in breaking news about mergers, acquisitions, and boardroom conflict.

How should journalists approach sources who have mixed or ulterior motives for sharing information?

Accept that every source has some self-interested motivation for talking , and don't waste energy worrying about what it is. Instead, focus on verification: confirm the information from two, three, or four independent sources, or obtain hard documentation. If the facts check out, the motive no longer matters—whether that source is a board member trying to derail a deal, a banker seeking revenge against a competitor, or anyone else with an axe to grind.

As Andrew Sorkin explains in this episode , journalism often happens on the back of sources with the worst possible motives. The insight isn't naive—it's pragmatic. Some of the best investigative stories come from people trying to sabotage someone else or advance their own interests , not from truth-seekers motivated by the public good.

The key is shifting the burden of proof. Don't authenticate the source; authenticate the story. A board member leaking details about a merger to stop it? That leak might be pure sabotage—but if you can cross-check those details with another source, with SEC filings, with email records, the source's toxicity becomes irrelevant. The documentation is what counts.

How does a media professional manage multiple demanding roles simultaneously across television, newsletters, and books?

Andrew Ross Sorkin's answer is ruthless time discipline: he wakes at 4:30 a.m., spends roughly one hour before Squawk Box starts (6 a.m.) refining the DealBook newsletter, hosts the live television show from 6 to 9 a.m. Eastern, then manages books, summits, and television work throughout the day—with each project informing the others rather than diluting focus.

The pre-dawn ritual: one hour to lock in DealBook

Sorkin's day begins before most people wake. Between 4:30 a.m. and the start of Squawk Box at 6 a.m., he operates in what amounts to a contained edit window: checking email and messages, then making final tweaks and refinements to the DealBook newsletter before it goes out to over one million subscribers. This is not exploratory work—it is execution of a structure already built by his team.

The newsletter itself has evolved into a distributed operation. Michael Dillamer, who has been with DealBook for approximately 20 years, anchors much of the day-to-day editorial work. Bernard Warner works overnight from Italy, which means the newsletter has real-time global coverage without Sorkin managing every detail personally. This structural delegation allows the 4:30 a.m. hour to be refinement, not creation from zero.

Projects that fuel each other rather than compete

Sorkin's insight—one that separates overwork from sustainable multi-project management—is that his television role, newsletter, books, and the annual Dealbook Summit do not operate in silos. He describes having "the best of both worlds": a television platform that drives newsletter audience and credibility; a newsletter that surfaces stories for deeper book-length investigation; and a summit that generates proprietary interviews and insights flowing back into DealBook.

As detailed in this episode , this approach differs fundamentally from juggling four separate, unrelated jobs. Each project extends the reach and authority of the others, creating leverage rather than duplication of effort.

What this podcast really covers

ACQ2 goes beyond company histories to extract the decision logic that separates enduring market leaders from temporary winners. Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal sit with founders and executives to examine the organizational choices, capital allocation patterns, and competitive positioning that define sustainable business models.

The podcast reveals that durable advantage comes not from single innovations but from systematic organizational choices. Shopify's founder discusses living in "everyone else's future"—building infrastructure and capabilities ahead of obvious market demand. Plaid's CEO examines the discipline required to unwind a $5 billion acquisition and rebuild as an independent company. ServiceNow's leader articulates the complexity of enterprise software sales—the relationship depth, organizational navigation, and customer outcome alignment that separates leadership from commodity suppliers.

Technology inflection points recur throughout ACQ2's analysis. The podcast examines how ARM became the default CPU architecture, how Hugging Face built open-source AI infrastructure with durability, and why AI differs fundamentally from prior technology waves. These conversations reveal when market incumbents maintain advantage and when structural shifts create windows for new builders to establish category dominance.
