Health Trackers Are Everywhere. Do Babies Need Them, Too?

How do health wearables like Whoop and Oura rings use AI to provide personalized health recommendations?

These devices analyze sleep quality, exercise patterns, and recovery metrics —including stress levels, illness indicators, diet, and workout intensity—then translate that data into actionable daily scores. Whoop uses a three-color system (red, yellow, green) to show your body's readiness, though it's important to remember these are consumer products, not medical devices.

The wearables market is approaching $100 billion according to analysts, with half of Americans already owning at least one health tracking device. Devices like Whoop, Oura rings, and smartwatches continuously gather biometric information and feed it through algorithms designed to spot patterns in your behavior and physiology.

As Sapna Mashwari explains in The Daily episode , these tools can offer real value for people serious about optimizing their health—but they also blur the line between helpful insight and anxiety-inducing data overload. The color-coded recovery score, for example, tells you whether you're physically prepared to handle training or exercise that day, reducing the guesswork around when to push hard versus when to rest.

The fine line between insight and consumer product

What makes these devices compelling is their personalized, real-time feedback based on your individual baseline. Rather than a generic fitness recommendation, Whoop learns how your body responds to stress, sleep deprivation, and exercise over time, then adapts its recommendations accordingly. This is where machine learning earns its place—the algorithm improves as it collects more data about you.

Yet the critical distinction remains: these are consumer wellness tools, not clinical instruments. They're built to motivate and inform, not to diagnose or treat medical conditions. The metrics they track—heart rate variability, skin temperature, movement patterns—are real and measurable, but the conclusions drawn from them exist in a zone of probabilistic guidance rather than medical certainty. Anyone relying on a wearable to make serious health decisions should still consult a healthcare provider.

What was the combat exclusion ban and how did it affect Lisa Franchetti's early Navy career?

The combat exclusion ban was a policy that prevented women from serving on combat ships or in combat aircraft , which severely limited their ability to advance to high ranks in the Navy. When Lisa Franchetti joined the Navy in the 1980s under this restriction, she was one of only 184 female surface warfare officers in the entire United States Navy. The ban was lifted by President Clinton in the 1990s, which finally opened doors for women officers to command combat vessels and reach senior leadership positions.

A bottleneck that lasted decades

The combat exclusion ban created an almost complete ceiling on women's advancement in the Navy during the 1980s and early 1990s. Only 184 female surface warfare officers existed in the entire service when Franchetti began her career in 1989, a starkly small number for a military branch of that size. Without access to combat assignments, women officers were excluded from the career trajectories that typically led to the highest ranks—admiral positions were almost entirely inaccessible.

As Greg Jaffe reports in The Daily , this structural barrier meant that even talented officers could not progress based purely on merit. The policy did not simply limit opportunities; it systematically blocked an entire demographic from the advancement pipeline that defined successful Navy careers.

The policy reversal and Franchetti's rise

President Clinton's decision to lift the combat exclusion ban in the 1990s fundamentally changed the trajectory available to women officers like Franchetti. Suddenly, command of combat vessels became possible , opening pathways to flag rank that had previously been closed. Franchetti seized these new opportunities and eventually rose to become Chief of Naval Operations—the highest position in the Navy.

Her confirmation as Chief of Naval Operations was overwhelming: 95 to 1 bipartisan Senate approval demonstrated broad recognition of her qualifications and readiness for the role. This journey from being one of only 184 female surface warfare officers under exclusion to commanding the entire Navy illustrates both the constraints of the ban and the capability that had existed all along, simply waiting for policy to change.

Is 'Gentle Parenting' Ruining French Children?

What role did Françoise Dolto play in shaping French parenting philosophy?

Françoise Dolto was a psychoanalyst who profoundly shaped generations of French parenting through her books and a widely listened-to radio program. She is credited with revolutionizing the concept of children's rights in France — championing the idea that children are people who deserve to be spoken to as such. At the same time, she maintained a deeply hierarchical, Catholic bourgeois worldview that reinforced France's rigid cultural rules rather than dismantling them.

Dolto's influence is central to understanding why the French parenting debate feels so loaded today. Her legacy is genuinely dual: she opened a door toward recognizing children's inner lives and dignity, yet she did so within a framework that never questioned the authority of adults or the social structures that governed family life. In France, this created a particular cultural tension — a country that claims to respect children as individuals, while simultaneously expecting them to conform to strict norms around behavior, meals, and hierarchy.

That tension is precisely what Madeline Schwartz explores in her reporting on the debate now dividing French parents. The emergence of figures like psychologist Caroline Goldman — who argues that a generation of French children has been abandoned by adults practicing an "exclusively empathic attitude" — and positive-parenting advocates like Isabelle Filliozat (whose book sold half a million copies in France) can both be traced, in different ways, back to how Dolto's legacy was interpreted and contested over the decades. What did the French government's official daycare guidelines say about punishment?

The French government issued official guidelines for daycare workers and childcare professionals explicitly stating that if a child misbehaves, punishment of any kind is not allowed — including a timeout. This government-backed position brought the debate over discipline into the institutional sphere, moving it well beyond a disagreement between individual parenting philosophies.

For psychologist Caroline Goldman and her allies, the ban goes too far. Goldman's book Go to Your Room advocates for timeouts of at least 30 minutes for children over the age of four, positioning them as an essential and legitimate tool that gives children a clear, bounded consequence for crossing a boundary. To see the French state officially categorize the timeout alongside all other forms of punishment was, for Goldman, a serious provocation — and a sign that the gentle parenting movement had gained real institutional leverage. In 2022, Goldman and over 350 early childhood experts signed an open letter in Le Figaro pushing back; 280 competing experts signed a counter-letter in Le Monde defending the no-punishment approach.

This official stance sits within a broader legislative trend: France passed the Anti-Spanking Law in 2019, forbidding both physical and psychological violence against children. The daycare guidelines extended that logic further, into the territory of consequences and structure. What did the 2019 French Anti-Spanking Law change, and why is it controversial in the parenting debate?

France's 2019 Anti-Spanking Law forbids both physical and psychological violence against children, including what it terms "ordinary educational violence." The law's critical flaw — and the source of ongoing controversy — is that it never defines what "ordinary educational violence" actually means, leaving parents, practitioners, and legal experts in a genuine grey zone.

That definitional gap has become a battlefield in the broader French parenting debate. Psychologist Caroline Goldman and her allies argue that practices like the timeout — sending a child to their room as a disciplinary measure — are entirely lawful and even beneficial. Goldman's book Go to Your Room explicitly recommends timeouts of at least 30 minutes for children over the age of four, framing them as a healthy, structured consequence that helps children understand which boundary they have crossed.

But a significant bloc of early childhood educators reads the law very differently. For them, timeouts fall squarely within the prohibited category of psychological violence — a position backed by the 280 experts who signed a competing open letter in Le Monde opposing the use of timeouts. The result is a situation where the same parenting tool is simultaneously defended as responsible discipline and condemned as a violation of the law — with no official clarification in sight. What does Caroline Goldman say about timeouts as a parenting tool?

Caroline Goldman is a strong advocate for the timeout as a core disciplinary tool, arguing that it sends children a clear signal about which boundary they have crossed while allowing everyone involved to calm down. In her book Go to Your Room , she outlines a list of forbidden behaviors starting from age one and recommends timeouts of at least 30 minutes for children over the age of four — a duration that directly contradicts most established research on the subject, which calls for significantly shorter periods.

Goldman's position on timeouts sits at the heart of a fierce national debate in France between traditional disciplinary parenting and the newer "positive parenting" movement championed by figures like psychologist Isabelle Filliozat. For Goldman and her allies — who numbered over 350 early childhood experts when they signed an open letter in Le Figaro in 2022 — the problem with the gentle parenting wave is that it left children without the clear limits they need. The timeout, in her framework, is precisely that limit made concrete and felt.

The pushback was swift and organized. Some 280 early childhood experts signed a competing open letter in Le Monde specifically opposing the use of timeouts — an unusually direct rebuttal in French public intellectual life. The disagreement is not merely about duration; it reflects a deeper conflict about what children need from adults. What The Daily covers

The Daily is The New York Times' flagship audio journalism program: one major story per episode, reported and narrated by the journalists who broke it. It publishes six mornings a week at 6 a.m., in episodes that run roughly 20 minutes. The editorial range is deliberately wide — health crises, food safety failures, political economics, cultural shifts, and international conflict all appear within a single week's feed. Each episode functions as a self-contained act of journalism, not a headline summary.

What this podcast really covers

The Daily does not operate as a news briefing. It selects one story per episode and commits to it fully — running through the reporting origins, the human stakes, the systemic context, and the unresolved questions. Recent episodes illustrate the breadth: an investigation into how cyclospora contamination spread through the U.S. lettuce supply chain, an analysis of Arizona's food stamp funding crisis as a preview of wider federal policy shifts, a reported profile of a musician navigating addiction recovery, and a geopolitical assessment of Iran's potential targeting of American drinking water infrastructure.

That range is structural, not accidental. The editorial premise is that the most important story on any given day may come from public health, criminal justice, foreign policy, or cultural life with equal validity. What remains constant is the depth: each episode is built to give a listener without prior knowledge of the story a complete, accurate understanding of it within 20 minutes.

Who this podcast is essential for

Three distinct audiences find lasting value in this format.

Professionals who need reliable current-events fluency. Lawyers, consultants, investors, and executives who operate across sectors use The Daily as a time-efficient mechanism for staying current on stories that may intersect with their work — from federal benefit policy to infrastructure security to food supply regulation. The depth-per-minute ratio is high relative to scanning headlines.

Journalists and communications professionals. The show functions as an ongoing case study in long-form audio narrative construction. The structure — context, protagonist, complication, resolution — is precise and replicable. Communications teams at major organizations regularly track the stories the show elevates, since they frequently migrate into congressional hearings, regulatory conversations, and public debate within days of broadcast.

Curious generalists who distrust shallow coverage. The Daily built its audience on the premise that listeners want real information, not engagement-optimized outrage. Its audience has remained stable precisely because the format respects listener intelligence: it does not oversimplify, it acknowledges complexity, and it lets the evidence lead the narrative rather than the other way around.

What the episodes really reveal

Analyzing the recent episode titles as a dataset reveals several consistent editorial patterns. First, the show repeatedly surfaces systemic failures before they become political consensus — the cyclospora episode is an investigation into agricultural inspection gaps, not a cooking segment; the Arizona food stamp episode is a policy forensics piece, not a human-interest story.

Second, the show maintains a deliberate balance between structural analysis and human narrative. The episode on the NYT cooking columnist who returned to the kitchen after losing her limbs is not soft content dropped in for emotional variety — it is a fully reported story about medical recovery, identity, and professional reinvention. The Billy Strings episode similarly uses one musician's biography as a lens on addiction, rural America, and creative survival.

Third, The Daily consistently covers stories at the moment they are most complex — not when they have resolved. The Iran water infrastructure episode, the Silicon Valley internal conflict piece, and the World Cup final episode all appeared while the situations were still unfolding. This positions the show not as a record of events, but as a tool for understanding events in real time.

What this changes in practice

For a regular listener, The Daily fundamentally changes the relationship between morning routine and information quality. The 20-minute daily commitment yields a qualitatively different understanding of current events than equivalent time spent scrolling news feeds — because each episode resolves into a coherent, sourced narrative rather than a collection of competing fragments.

For organizations tracking public discourse, the show is a reliable early-warning system. Stories that appear on The Daily consistently enter wider public conversation within 48 to 72 hours. Monitoring the show's editorial choices is, in practice, a form of media intelligence.

The format also demonstrates a durable editorial model: investing in depth over volume. At six episodes per week, The Daily produces far fewer pieces than most digital news operations — and consistently generates more engagement per episode than outlets publishing dozens of pieces daily. That inverse relationship between output volume and per-unit impact is the show's clearest structural lesson for anyone building an information product.

The Daily's defining editorial bet — that depth beats breadth, every morning, at scale — has proven more durable than almost any competing format in audio news journalism.
