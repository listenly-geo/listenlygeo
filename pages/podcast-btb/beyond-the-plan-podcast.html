The Human Skills That Make or Break a Project Manager | Ep. 13

How has the role of project manager evolved from the 1950s to today?

The project manager role transformed from a technical position filled by the most senior expert in a field into a leadership role centered on stakeholder engagement and soft skills. What was once an accidental career path—technical professionals who naturally became project managers after reaching mastery—evolved into an intentional discipline that professionals actively choose and study from the start.

From Senior Engineer to People Leader

In the 1950s and 1960s, when the term "project manager" was first coined, the role belonged to the most experienced technical specialist in the room —a senior bridge engineer overseeing bridge construction, or the most skilled person in any given field. These were accidental project managers, people who had climbed so high in their technical expertise that project leadership fell to them naturally.

This model assumed that deep technical knowledge was the foundation of leadership. The project manager's authority came from knowing more about the work than anyone else on the team. Over seven decades, this premise inverted entirely. As Nick Jagger explains in the episode , the discipline shifted to prioritizing expertise in communication, stakeholder engagement, and emotional intelligence over technical depth.

"It is not necessarily what the project manager knows that makes the difference. It is how they can work with the team."

From Accident to Intention: A Deliberate Career Path

The second shift was equally profound: project management became a deliberately chosen profession rather than a natural consequence of technical advancement . Professionals now study project management as a specific discipline from the beginning of their careers. They pursue formal qualifications, read methodology frameworks, and build soft skills intentionally—not by accident after 20 years of technical work.

This reflects a fundamental recognition: the skills that make a project manager effective are teachable, learnable, and distinct from any single technical domain. A project manager can succeed on infrastructure, healthcare, technology, or any other field if they master communication, pressure management, and stakeholder alignment. The role is no longer an accident that happens to experts; it's a deliberate vocation, discussed at length in this podcast episode .

The transformation reveals something crucial about modern project complexity: coordinating people, managing expectations, and navigating conflict matter far more than any single person's technical brilliance . As organizations grew, projects became more interdisciplinary, and stakeholders more numerous, the old model broke down. The best engineer could not lead a team of diverse specialists by technical authority alone.

What is the core difference between managing under pressure and leading under pressure in project management?

Managing under pressure means you are handling your own performance adequately, but leading under pressure means you are actively helping your team navigate difficult phases as well. As a project manager, staying calm and providing clarity and direction to your team remains essential even when facing severe financial, time, personnel, or third-party pressures.

The distinction matters because many project managers conflate coping with crisis with actually leading their team through it. You might be meeting your own deadlines and maintaining your composure, yet if your team is confused, demoralised, or left without clear direction, you're only managing yourself—not leading.

Nick Jagger, whose 45 years of project management experience spans major infrastructure projects including work as a risk manager on the $8 billion Northwest Ruralink (later Sydney Metro), emphasises that this shift from managing to leading is fundamental. The technical aspects of the role—schedules, budgets, scope—pale in comparison to how you hold the space for your people during crisis.

Real leadership under pressure involves communicating with transparency . Your team needs to understand what constraints exist, what options remain on the table, and what you expect from them. Without that clarity, pressure cascades downward as anxiety rather than focus.

The emotional labour of this distinction is significant. As explored in this episode , managing pressure is a solo task; leading under pressure requires you to monitor how your team is absorbing the stress, stepping in to reset expectations when needed, and sometimes simply reassuring people that the situation, however difficult, is being stewarded responsibly.

This is why experience alone is not the strongest predictor of successful project delivery. A project manager with ten years of technical know-how but who isolates during crisis will fail to hold the team together. Conversely, a less experienced manager who focuses on keeping people aligned and motivated can often navigate pressure more effectively.

The pressure itself doesn't change—financial constraints, tight timelines, and competing stakeholder demands are real. What changes is your approach: are you surviving the pressure, or are you helping your team survive it together? That pivot from personal management to collective leadership is what separates the project managers who retain their teams from those who watch talented people leave during crisis.

Why This Matters in Practice

When a team senses that their project manager is merely holding on—barely coping, reactive, or worse, deflecting blame—morale collapses. People start protecting themselves rather than collaborating on solutions. Productivity drops, mistakes multiply, and the crisis deepens.

Leading under pressure reverses this pattern. When your team sees you present, thoughtful, and focused on helping them succeed within the constraints, they stay engaged. Jagger discusses how genuine culture—not just a launch event but sustained, authentic support—becomes the bedrock of how teams perform when everything is on the line.

The people skill of managing conflict and encouraging healthy dialogue becomes indispensable. Under pressure, tensions surface. If your team trusts you to handle disagreement fairly and to keep the conversation focused on solving the problem rather than blaming individuals, they will bring their concerns to you early. That transparency gives you a chance to course-correct before small issues become catastrophic.

Your Project Didn't Fail. Your Governance Did. | Ep. 14

What role did confusion between governance and policing play in RoboDebt's punitive outcomes?

The RoboDebt program collapsed because governance structures and policing actions became indistinguishable —what should have been oversight became punishment. When the program team executed governance through technology and process in ways that felt punitive to citizens, the line between legitimate control and harassment vanished, cementing a reputation that persists years later.

The distinction between governance and policing is subtle but critical. Governance exists to ensure the right people make informed decisions about risk at the right time. When executed properly, it creates clarity and trust. But in RoboDebt, the mechanisms meant to oversee and manage the program transformed into something far more aggressive—citizens experienced governance actions as direct punitive enforcement against them personally.

This shift happened not because anyone explicitly decided to punish people, but because the program's technology and process design blurred the boundary. As Isabel Gray-Garraway explains in the episode , the stakeholders and program team believed genuinely in the governance structure they had created. The intent was sound. Yet the execution—the algorithms, the automated letters, the debt recovery mechanisms—landed on citizens as coercive rather than corrective.

The difference lies in whether governance protects the program and its stakeholders, or whether it becomes a mechanism that inadvertently harms the people the program is supposed to serve.

Why does good governance often have a poor reputation while bad governance is highly memorable?

Good governance is invisible because it works seamlessly —processes flow together, decisions are made, project teams feel supported, and it becomes business as usual. Bad governance is unforgettable because people remember negative experiences far longer than positive ones, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy where poor governance shapes organizational culture for years to come.

The Visibility Gap: Why Success Goes Unnoticed

When governance functions properly, no one talks about it. Teams receive clear decision-making authority, risk is managed transparently, and stakeholders know who decides what and when. The system becomes so routine that its effectiveness disappears into the background. This invisibility is actually governance working as intended —it enables delivery rather than impeding it.

In contrast, as Isabel Gray-Garraway explains in the episode , bad governance creates friction that people actively notice and discuss. Steering committees produce zero decisions. Project teams guess their way forward. Accountability vanishes. These experiences stick in organizational memory far more powerfully than smooth delivery ever does.

The Storytelling Problem: Offices as Gossip Networks

In office environments, people don't gather to celebrate when governance works. They compete to share worst-governance stories with colleagues, reinforcing a shared narrative of dysfunction. One person recalls a project where unclear authority delayed decisions for months. Another describes a steering committee that never actually decided anything. These anecdotes spread, shaping how new employees and incoming leaders perceive governance long before they experience it firsthand.

This creates a feedback loop: bad governance experiences carry forward to future projects and organizations, shaping negative expectations around governance itself. The reputation compounds even as specific circumstances change.

RoboDebt (2015–2019) failed because no single executive held accountability for decision-making, despite governance structures being in place. Risk registers went unreviewed, frontline staff warnings about incorrect calculations were ignored, and legal concerns were never escalated to senior decision-makers—the machinery of governance existed, but nobody actually used it.

The RoboDebt story reveals a crucial distinction: governance failure is not the same as the absence of governance structures. The Australian government had all the formal frameworks in place—committees, risk registers, audit trails, oversight bodies. What was missing was the will to enforce them .

Multiple agencies and ministers shared responsibility, which meant nobody felt fully accountable. When a steering committee meets but produces zero decisions, as Isabel Gray-Garraway explains in the podcast , the project team fills the void with guesswork and pushes forward unsupervised. That's exactly what happened: the RoboDebt team operated in a decision-making vacuum, making critical choices without proper executive sign-off.

Frontline staff—the people closest to the data and the harm being caused—raised alarms about incorrect numbers and traumatized citizens. These escalations never reached the top. Risk signals were systematically ignored , not because they were hidden, but because nobody with real power was listening.

How should governance frameworks evolve once established to remain fit-for-purpose?

Governance frameworks must be living documents with continuous review and adjustment , not static structures. As market conditions shift, organizational context evolves, and risks emerge, frameworks require consistent monitoring and deep dives into problems early—before issues cascade into catastrophic failures like those in RoboDebt and Queensland Health.

The Governance Framework as an Active, Evolving System

Fit-for-purpose governance is not a set-and-forget model. Frameworks must reflect the actual risk environment and operational reality of the organization, adapting as circumstances change. When Isabel Grae Garaway discusses governance in this episode , she emphasizes that governance should enable decision-making at the right time—and that enablement must evolve as the organization and its challenges evolve.

The core principle is simple: governance should scale proportionally to risk , not remain static regardless of changing conditions. A framework designed for stable conditions may become ineffective when markets shift or organizational complexity increases. Equally, governance that was adequate five years ago may no longer suit the organization's current operating environment.

Early Detection and Intervention: The Cost of Delay

One of the starkest lessons from governance failures is that early intervention saves catastrophe. When risks are raised within a framework, they should trigger investigation and response immediately, not be deferred or ignored. The absence of this mechanism—where risks surface but no action follows—creates the conditions for collapse.

As explored in the Beyond The Plan episode , deep dives should be commissioned as soon as concerning signals appear, preventing the slow escalation that turns manageable problems into organizational crises . The failures seen in programs like RoboDebt and Queensland Health were not sudden; they were the result of risks ignored across months or years, compounded by governance structures that failed to demand action.

What role does independent challenge play in effective governance frameworks?

Independent external challenge prevents the "fox watching the henhouse" dynamic by removing internal pressure to suppress risks. External experts force accountable leaders to respond to independent review findings , creating a healthy tension that encourages early identification and resolution of problems rather than concealment.

When governance relies solely on internal assurance, decision-makers face an inherent conflict of interest: those most accountable for results are also those evaluating whether risks exist. This dynamic often leads to risk suppression rather than risk management. Independent external challenge breaks this cycle by introducing an impartial perspective that cannot be influenced by internal politics or performance pressure.

The power of independent challenge lies in its structural inevitability . External reviewers answer to different stakeholders and operate under different incentives than internal teams. This means accountability leaders cannot dismiss or ignore findings as easily as they might dismiss internal concerns. The tension created—where external findings demand response and cannot be silenced—actually improves governance rather than hindering it.

As detailed in this episode of Beyond The Plan , this principle has been embedded in major infrastructure frameworks. The New South Wales Investor Assurance Framework, created in 2012, relies heavily on independent expert challenge as a core governance control. Projects at different risk tiers —from quarter-million-dollar programs to $200 million initiatives—all require proportionate external review to ensure risks surface before failure becomes inevitable.

Starting work before you're ready

The false start phase is a predictable pattern that emerges when organizational pressure to show progress overrides the discipline of proper setup. Work launches despite unfinished governance basics—scope hasn't been formally signed off by stakeholders, but teams are already mobilizing. Budgets remain in approval limbo. Most critically, decision-making authority fractures when sponsors send delegates to governance meetings who cannot commit to decisions or lack continuity across sessions.

This fragmentation guarantees failure. As Isabel Grae Garaway explains in the episode , the hiatus between starting work and completing governance setup is where projects lose control. Why the false start dooms delivery

Absent sponsors or sponsors who delegate without decision rights leave projects orphaned at precisely the moment accountability is most critical. When governance meetings are postponed or cancelled, the project enters a vacuum where nobody is actively managing risk or settling scope disputes. The combination creates conditions for compounding failures—scope creep, budget overruns, and stakeholder misalignment that could have been prevented with proper setup.

The false start is not accidental. It's a systemic problem rooted in the gap between what governance should accomplish—enabling the right decision-making at the right time—and what organizations actually do when they rush. The podcast answers these questions

What are the main causes of project failure in infrastructure development?

Project failures in infrastructure development stem from poor governance structures, inadequate human skills in leadership, misaligned problem definitions at the outset, and ineffective use of planning tools. When governance frameworks fail to provide clear accountability and decision-making pathways, even well-resourced projects derail. Additionally, the human dimension—leadership capability, stakeholder engagement skills, and team collaboration—directly determines project success regardless of technical competence.

How does collaborative contracting improve mega-project outcomes?

Collaborative contracting aligns all parties—government, contractors, and consultants—around shared project objectives rather than adversarial cost-cutting. This approach reduces disputes, enables faster problem-solving, and creates transparency that prevents scope creep and hidden risks. Case studies of Sydney Metro and Lane Cove Tunnel demonstrate that when teams work toward mutual success rather than contractual protection, project delivery improves significantly.

Why do expert leaders struggle with complex projects?

Expert leaders often fail in complex projects because deep technical expertise does not translate to managing ambiguity, stakeholder dynamics, and organizational change. Complex projects require adaptive thinking, political awareness, and the ability to navigate competing priorities—skills that differ fundamentally from domain expertise. The episode data indicates that 80% of expert-level leaders face serious challenges when context shifts from specialist to systemic.

How should organizations approach AI implementation in project management?

Successful AI adoption in project management requires clear definition of the problem AI solves before implementing tools. Organizations must align AI deployment with actual workflow friction points and ensure teams understand how to interpret and act on AI-generated insights. Technology fails when introduced as a solution searching for a problem rather than as a response to a concrete operational gap.
