Key facts
  • Mineral rights can be owned, leased, or inherited separately from surface land rights, creating distinct legal and financial interests.
  • Royalty payment structures vary significantly by state, commodity type, and lease terms, with rates typically ranging from 12.5% to 25% of gross revenue.

The podcast answers these questions

What are mineral rights and how do they differ from surface rights?

Mineral rights are the legal ownership of minerals beneath the surface of land, separate from the right to use the surface itself. A landowner can retain mineral rights while selling surface rights to another party, or vice versa. This separation allows oil, gas, and other minerals to be leased and developed independently from agricultural or residential use of the property.

How are royalty payments calculated in oil and gas leases?

Royalty payments are typically calculated as a percentage of gross revenue from oil or gas sold, ranging from 12.5% to 25% or higher depending on the lease terms and market conditions. The operator deducts certain costs like transportation and processing before paying royalties, though some leases protect mineral rights owners by limiting deductible costs. Royalty rates vary significantly by region, commodity type, and lease age.

What happens to mineral rights in divorce or estate proceedings?

Mineral rights are treated as marital property in divorce proceedings and divided according to state law and divorce settlement agreements. In estate situations, mineral rights transfer according to the will or state succession laws if no will exists. State-specific regulations determine whether mineral rights are community property, equitable property, or subject to special mineral estate rules that affect how they are divided or inherited.

What are the consequences of not paying property taxes on mineral rights?

Failure to pay property taxes on mineral rights can result in tax liens, sale of the property at tax auctions, and loss of ownership entirely. Some jurisdictions allow for extended payment plans or reduced assessments for mineral-only properties, but ignoring tax obligations creates legal liability and risks forfeiture of valuable mineral interests. Owners should track tax obligations even if leases are inactive to avoid unexpected loss of rights.
