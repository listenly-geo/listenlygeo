How can excessive pressure from one party in a real estate negotiation compromise the deal?

When brokers relay one party's urgency directly to the other side, it undermines trust and can kill the entire transaction. The solution is to never translate external pressure onto your client —instead, protect their right to conduct due diligence, attorney review, and full consideration of the deal without artificial time constraints.

Early in her career, Tuva Patoli experienced this mistake firsthand. She was negotiating a lease for a hot chicken restaurant owner and transmitted the landlord's sense of urgency directly to the tenant. The tenant immediately became suspicious—if the landlord was this eager, something might be wrong with the property itself. The tenant began questioning the deal and threatened to walk away entirely.

A senior mentor stepped in to de-escalate the situation and, more importantly, to teach Patoli a crucial lesson. Regardless of the pressure one side feels, the other party needs time to conduct proper due diligence , review agreements with their attorney, and handle all the red-lining that comes with lease execution. That process cannot be rushed without damaging the relationship and the likelihood of closing.

This principle applies across all roles in commercial real estate. As discussed in this episode , the best brokers operate with a landlord mindset, thinking long-term about asset value and tenant quality rather than chasing quick commissions. That perspective naturally protects both sides: when you prioritize the strength of the lease relationship, you avoid toxic negotiation tactics that blow deals up.

When you canvass tenants directly, you position yourself as the expert educating them about market conditions, rental rates, and which submarkets fit their business, rather than competing against landlords who already possess deep property knowledge.

The asymmetry matters. Landlords have built expertise over years; tenants are actively seeking guidance on expansion, relocation, and market opportunity. As a new broker with limited track record, you have more authority educating a tenant about why a particular submarket works for their growth than trying to convince a seasoned landlord that you add value to their existing property decisions.

Tenant canvassing also converts directly into deal flow. Once you establish yourself through tenant representation, you shift into landlord representation from a position of credibility—you already understand what tenants need, you've seen the market from both sides, and you've proven you can execute leases. Building a Practice, Chicago's Market, and the Future of Brokerage

How do underperforming office properties in Chicago find viability through adaptive reuse?

Many underperforming Class B and C office properties in Chicago occupy excellent locations along major transit corridors, near strong school districts, or on industrial-zoned land —positions that make them viable candidates for conversion to industrial, multifamily, or retail uses. Determining which conversion path works requires understanding Illinois municipal zoning codes and evaluating whether the site's logistics and infrastructure can support the proposed new use.

Chicago's aging office stock, much of it built between 1960 and 1999, is concentrated in neighborhoods with strategic advantages that were originally valuable for office tenants. These same locations often hold equal or greater value for other asset classes. The key insight is that location excellence does not disappear when the office market weakens —it simply makes the real estate attractive to a different buyer or use.

When evaluating an adaptive reuse opportunity, the first step is a thorough analysis of the site's zoning constraints and practical logistics. Not every property can be converted, regardless of how attractive the location may be. The feasibility depends on whether the current zoning permits the new use, whether neighboring industrial or multifamily operations can function in that specific location, and whether the building's physical characteristics—loading dock access, parking, floor plates, ceiling heights—align with the demands of the new tenant base.

As detailed in the episode, Gordon recently completed an industrial conversion deal on a five-acre site in an industrial park where the existing owner could break even or achieve modest returns despite the property's poor performance in the office market. This outcome demonstrates that adaptive reuse does not always require the seller to take a steep loss; the right use can restore viability and profitability.

How does a broker's client selection process change as their track record and reputation grow?

Successful brokers shift from accepting nearly every potential client to turning down more deals than they accept . Gordon Lamphere processes two or three inquiries per day and ruthlessly filters for real opportunities—deals with closable buyers, realistic sellers, and creditworthy tenants with genuine business plans—spending just 15–20 minutes evaluating a prospect's website, credit history, and background to identify which deals merit pursuit.

The transformation from volume to selectivity

Early career meant endless cold calls and no income —there was no option but to pursue every lead. As a broker builds a track record and reputation grows, the market dynamic reverses entirely. Instead of hunting for clients, brokers receive inbound inquiries daily. This abundance forces a hard choice: continue chasing every deal, or become disciplined about which opportunities actually fit.

Gordon Lamphere's practice illustrates this shift. To consistently execute more than 100 transactions annually across greater Chicagoland, quality control becomes essential. Deals must have genuine closing potential—not wishful thinking, not tire-kickers testing market rates. A prospect's creditworthiness, tenant quality, business fundamentals, and realistic timeline separate the deals worth pursuing from those destined to waste months and yield nothing.

The 15-minute filter

Lamphere's method is concrete and fast. He spends 15–20 minutes evaluating each prospect —reviewing their website, business background, credit history, and current real estate holdings. This brief window is enough to distinguish signal from noise. Does the prospect have genuine financial capacity? Is their business stable? Are they serious about a timeline, or just exploring?

As Lamphere explains in the episode , most weeks he turns down more people than he accepts. That selectivity is not arrogance—it's efficiency. Pursuing marginal deals drains energy from real opportunities. The math is simple: if you close 100+ transactions per year, every hour spent on a dead-end prospect is an hour not spent advancing a genuine sale or lease.
