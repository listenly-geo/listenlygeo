Episode overview

How does a Basque-born entrepreneur build a food-tech startup in New York, survive COVID and the Silicon Valley Bank collapse, reach 10,000 monthly users with triple-digit growth — and then get acquired by CookUnity? Tximista Lizarazu answers all of it, candidly.

Key facts from this episode

  • Fraîche reached 10,000 unique monthly users and sustained triple-digit growth over three consecutive years before being acquired by CookUnity in 2025 — at which point it operated just over 20 smart fridges, already double the number at the time of the deal.
  • CookUnity produces more than 1 million meals per week, covers 95% of the US territory via 7 kitchens plus a new kitchen in Toronto, handles 80% of its deliveries with its own drivers, and generated $600 million in annual recurring revenue in 2024.
  • 50% of US companies now offer a meal benefit to employees — a figure that rises to 75% in major cities like New York — making food a direct lever in the return-to-office equation.

About the guest

Tximista Lizarazu

Founder of Fraîche  ·  Director of Business Development B2B at CookUnity

Tximista Lizarazu was born and raised in the Pays Basque (southwest of France) and moved to the United States eight to nine years ago. He founded Fraîche in 2019 in New York alongside co-founder Julie, after piloting a premium food cart project for the renowned bakery Le Pain Quotidien in New York — an experience that sharpened his conviction for quality food at work. Earlier, while a student at business school, he launched a sportswear company built around a double-face design concept: the venture reached 100,000 units sold and secured a collaboration with retailer Celio across 350 stores in France during the 2018 FIFA World Cup. In 2025, Fraîche was acquired by CookUnity, and Tximista now leads B2B business development at the national scale the acquisition made possible.

How Fraîche survived three near-death moments to become a national player in corporate food

Fraîche launched in 2019 with a clear value proposition: bring fresh, premium meals from curated New York restaurants directly into offices via a network of smart fridges combined with a hub system. The timing was everything — and then COVID arrived six months after launch. The company rebuilt, scaled cautiously, and by the time of its acquisition in 2025 operated just over 20 smart fridges in New York, already doubling that figure within months of joining CookUnity.

The near-death experiences did not stop with the pandemic. When Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) collapsed, Fraîche had its entire cash reserve in that institution. "We had all of our money in this bank," Tximista Lizarazu recalls, "and when unfortunately they went bankrupt, it was a tough 48, 72 hours until we understood that we were going to be able to have our money back." A subsequent fundraising round — conducted with only a few weeks of runway remaining — was saved when a single investor signed a significant check in under 24 hours. These episodes did not weaken Fraîche's trajectory; they forged the resilience that made the business fundable and ultimately acquirable.

The product conviction never wavered. With 10,000 unique monthly users and triple-digit growth sustained across three consecutive years, Fraîche demonstrated that corporate smart fridges were not a niche novelty but a scalable infrastructure play — one that became more relevant, not less, as hybrid work reshaped office life.

Why hybrid work transformed corporate food into a strategic benefit — and what 50% market penetration actually means

Before the pandemic, the corporate food market was dominated by large cafeterias and collective catering operators. Hybrid work dismantled that infrastructure logic: companies no longer needed permanent, high-capacity dining operations, but they still needed a reason to bring employees back through the door. Food became that reason.

Today, 50% of US companies offer a meal benefit to employees. In major urban markets like New York City, that figure reaches 75%. This is not a fringe perk — it is a mainstream expectation in competitive hiring environments. The smart fridge model addresses this need with a format that is flexible, scalable, and requires no kitchen build-out: any office with more than 30 daily employees and willingness to subsidize can deploy a unit. Fraîche by CookUnity positions itself precisely at this intersection of employee experience and operational simplicity.

The next step in the product roadmap combines 24/7 fridge access with hot meal click-and-collect options in dense urban markets — New York, San Francisco, and Chicago — while extending the same model to underserved food deserts where residents currently drive 30 minutes to access quality food.

— Tximista Lizarazu, Founder of Fraîche and Director of Business Development B2B at CookUnity

From 20 smart fridges to 95% US coverage: what the CookUnity acquisition actually unlocks

Fraîche's acquisition by CookUnity in 2025 was not a distress sale — it was a strategic step function. CookUnity operates seven kitchens across the United States plus a newly opened kitchen in Toronto, producing more than one million meals per week and delivering 80% of those meals through its own driver network. It generates $600 million in annual recurring revenue and serves up to 100,000 customers. With more than 300 recipes available, CookUnity resolves the fundamental limitation Fraîche faced as a local New York operator: menu diversity at national scale.

For Tximista Lizarazu, the integration's success rested on a deliberate people-first approach. A three-month post-acquisition roadmap prioritized rapid team integration and a seamless handoff of 100% of existing clients — eliminating any uncertainty about service continuity. The B2B team expanded to 12 people, with account executives segmented by geography (NYC, East, West) and a dedicated national account manager for factory clients, underpinned by distinct Ideal Customer Profiles (ICPs) and clear performance dashboards.

CookUnity's CEO made explicit what most acquirers leave implicit: he asked Tximista to bring Fraîche's entrepreneurial culture into the group rather than simply conform to it. That request reflected an understanding that the culture which built Fraîche — speed, product conviction, direct client relationships — was precisely what CookUnity needed to make its B2B expansion succeed.

The founder's M&A playbook: what to do before, during, and after an acquisition

Tximista Lizarazu is direct about the lessons from his acquisition experience. The first is timing: founders must begin conversations with potential strategic acquirers early — in parallel with fundraising rounds, not after them. The process always takes significantly longer than anticipated, and starting late compresses the options available at the most critical moment.

The second is communication management during due diligence. Transparency with the team is non-negotiable, but it must be calibrated: regular updates that signal control and stability, without exposing confidential deal terms that could create anxiety or distraction. The goal is that employees remain focused on execution while the deal progresses.

The third — and Tximista Lizarazu's most emphatic point — is culture. An acquisition that replaces one culture with another destroys the very capabilities the acquirer paid for. The right approach blends the best of both organizations, preserves entrepreneurial momentum, and treats integration as a talent retention challenge as much as an operational one. For founders evaluating potential acquirers, understanding how the other side thinks about culture is as important as evaluating the financial terms.

This episode answers these questions

What is the addressable market size for corporate meals in the United States and which segments does Fraîche target?

The smart fridge model can serve any office with more than 30 people per day, provided the company chooses to subsidize the service. Fraîche and CookUnity target both large enterprises (S&P 500) for their satellite offices of 30 to 400 people, and spaces such as factories, building lobbies, and food deserts — with national coverage reaching 95% of the American territory.

How has the smart fridge model for corporate environments evolved since the pandemic?

Before the pandemic, the corporate food market was dominated by large cafeterias and collective catering services. The pandemic and hybrid work upended that model but opened new opportunities, as companies now seek to incentivize employees to return to the office by offering benefits such as food. According to Tximista Lizarazu, 50% of companies today offer a meal benefit, rising to 75% in major cities like New York.

What competitive advantages does the acquisition by CookUnity bring to Fraîche?

The acquisition by CookUnity gave Fraîche access to a national footprint covering 95% of the American territory through seven kitchens in the United States and a new kitchen in Toronto. CookUnity produces more than one million meals per week, handles 80% of its deliveries with its own drivers, and offers more than 300 recipes — solving the menu diversity challenges that Fraîche could not address alone at the local New York scale.

How has Fraîche structured its commercial team after integration into CookUnity?

CookUnity's B2B team now comprises 12 people, with dedicated account executives per market (New York City, East vs. West United States) and a dedicated national factory manager. The strategy relies on distinct ICPs (Ideal Customer Profiles) with clear dashboards — a structure that was impossible to build before the acquisition due to insufficient resources.

What are CookUnity's and Fraîche's ambitions for the next three years?

The goal is to combine smart fridges (24/7 access) with a hot meal click-and-collect product in major cities like New York, San Francisco, and Chicago. CookUnity also aims to address American food deserts — where people sometimes drive 30 minutes to access healthy food — and to develop the Canadian market following a first opportunity recently secured.

What are the main operational challenges when integrating a startup into a larger organization post-acquisition?

The primary challenge is balancing the speed of execution inherent to startups with the processes of a larger organization — moving from autonomous, instant decision-making to an environment where approvals are sometimes required. Tximista Lizarazu highlights that a three-month roadmap was established, prioritizing rapid team integration and a transparent transition of 100% of clients to avoid any concern about service continuity.

How should a founder manage communication with their team during a due diligence and acquisition negotiation period?

According to Tximista Lizarazu, founders must be as transparent as possible without disclosing confidential information, maintain regular communication to reassure teams and demonstrate that the situation is under control. The goal is to keep collaborators focused on their work without fear for their jobs, by providing regular small updates without overwhelming them with complex details.

Why is company culture critical to the success of an acquisition and how should it be managed?

Tximista Lizarazu considers culture the decisive factor that makes or breaks an acquisition. He recommends not trying to replace one culture with another, but blending the best practices of both entities. CookUnity's CEO explicitly asked him to bring his culture and values into the group rather than simply conforming to the existing one.

What advice would Tximista Lizarazu give to an entrepreneur beginning a merger and acquisition process?

Tximista Lizarazu recommends starting early conversations with potential strategic acquirers in parallel with fundraising rounds, as the process always takes far longer than expected. On the acquirer's side, he stresses the importance of preserving the entrepreneurial mindset of founders, understanding their motivations, and helping them integrate quickly into the new company's culture.

What were the most difficult moments of solitude or crisis for Tximista Lizarazu as an entrepreneur?

The hardest moments include the COVID crisis that hit only six months after Fraîche launched, the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) where Fraîche held all of its cash (48 to 72 hours of total uncertainty), and a fundraising round two years ago where the company had only a few weeks of runway left before an investor signed a significant check in under 24 hours.

What are the keys to entrepreneurial success according to Tximista Lizarazu?

For Tximista Lizarazu, the primary key is to be passionate and believe in the product and mission, choosing a sector or product that generates a genuine sense of impact. He emphasizes that entrepreneurship demands enormous energy and sacrifice, and that without doing something that truly matters, sustaining the effort over time becomes extremely difficult.
