What major physical campus transformations has NorQuest College undertaken as part of its master plan?

NorQuest College demolished two buildings on its Edmonton campus to create green spaces, leased four floors of Jasper Avenue Tower for its new Career Skills Centre, and installed distinctive perimeter markers around the education district to forge a unified campus identity as part of its strategic master plan.

From Demolition to Public Space: Building the Modern Campus

The transformation began by repurposing the college's most visible real estate into community assets. NorQuest removed an aging building on the southeast corner and converted that space into Mianook Park , which now serves as a gathering place for the growing education district. Shortly after, the college acquired the Champs building on 109th Street and the Calder Bateman building, demolishing both to create an additional park on campus.

These demolitions were not losses but strategic choices—part of a deliberate shift to prioritize open, accessible space over aging infrastructure. As Carolyn Campbell explains in the Executive Wins Podcast , the college's growth from 5,000 to 10,000 full-time students over a decade required rethinking how physical space could serve educational and community needs simultaneously.

Modern Facilities and Cohesive Campus Identity

To accommodate expanded programming, NorQuest leased four floors of Jasper Avenue Tower at the south end of 108th Street to establish its new Career Skills Centre. The Government of Alberta supported this expansion with $4 million in planning and development funding, positioning the college to train frontline workers across Alberta's growing economy.

Beyond individual buildings, Campbell's leadership team installed colorful perimeter markers around the education district campus. These visual identifiers help establish a cohesive campus identity across multiple properties and signal to students, staff, and the public that the entire downtown corridor functions as one integrated learning ecosystem. Why should confidentiality be a two-way commitment in mentor-mentee relationships?

Confidentiality must flow in both directions because mentors share sensitive real-world case studies, board packages, and live scenarios as teaching tools, which require the mentee to protect them just as the mentor protects the mentee's personal development information. Without this mutual trust foundation, neither party can fully open up.

When a mentor brings authentic business materials into the mentoring relationship—strategic decisions, board-level discussions, financial details— those materials become proprietary information the mentee must guard carefully . The mentee's confidentiality pledge isn't just a courtesy; it's essential protection for real business knowledge being shared.

Equally, the mentee may share career struggles, personal goals, or sensitive feedback about their organization. The mentor's duty is to protect that vulnerability with the same seriousness as the mentee protects the mentor's business insights. When both sides understand this mutual obligation, trust deepens immediately.

As Eric Dillon explains in the Executive Wins Podcast , he addresses this two-way confidentiality expectation directly in preliminary meetings before the mentoring relationship begins. This upfront clarity prevents misunderstandings and signals that both parties take the relationship seriously.

Rather than relying on legal agreements as the primary enforcement mechanism, Dillon views a confidentiality statement as a reinforcement of commitment —a mutual acknowledgment that what happens in the relationship stays within it. He vets mentees carefully and conducts preliminary conversations to ensure cultural alignment before moving forward, making the agreement more about shared values than legal protection.

What framework should leaders use to set up effective mentoring relationships from the first meeting?

Clear structure in the first meeting accelerates trust and learning. Address confidentiality, define goals, ensure transparency with the mentee's direct leader, agree on meeting cadence and agenda responsibility, schedule a feedback check-in after a couple of meetings, and conduct a final assessment —these six elements establish the foundation that lets mentors and mentees reach substantive conversations much faster.

Why Structure Matters Before Substance

Most mentoring relationships drift because they lack intentional design. When mentors and mentees jump straight into conversation without establishing boundaries and expectations, they waste time circling around assumptions and build less durable working relationships. Getting the structural elements clear in the opening conversation eliminates friction and unlocks faster progress on what actually matters.

Eric Dillon, who has tracked over 122 mentoring relationships on his mentoring scorecard, has found that this deliberate setup separates mentoring relationships that harvest significant learnings from those that plateau after a few sessions. As he explains in this episode , the investment of 20–30 minutes in the first meeting to lock down these six elements pays dividends across months of mentoring.

The Six Essential Elements

Confidentiality is the first conversation. Many mentees worry that what they share with a mentor will circle back to their employer or peers. Explicitly agreeing on what stays confidential—and what doesn't—removes this silent barrier and creates psychological safety. Define mentoring goals next : are you focusing on leadership skills, strategic thinking, industry knowledge, or personal growth? Vague goals produce vague results.

Transparency with the direct leader matters , even though confidentiality protects the substance of your conversations. The mentee's manager should know a mentoring relationship exists and what its scope is, so no misunderstandings cloud the workplace dynamic. Agree on meeting cadence and who owns the agenda —whether you're meeting monthly, biweekly, or quarterly, and whether the mentee brings topics or the mentor guides the discussion.

Schedule a feedback check-in after a couple of meetings to assess whether the fit is working. Not every mentor–mentee pairing clicks, and it's better to course-correct or pause early than to let a misaligned relationship limp along. Finally, conduct a final assessment when the mentoring relationship concludes, to evaluate what was learned and how both parties can carry those lessons forward.
