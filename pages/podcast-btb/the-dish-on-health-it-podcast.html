What this podcast really covers

The Dish on Health IT operates at the intersection of healthcare regulation, technology implementation, and clinical operations. This is not a podcast about generalized industry trends; it addresses the specific challenges executives and clinicians face when translating policy into practice.

Recent episodes reveal three recurring themes that define modern health IT work. First, interoperability is no longer theoretical—federal mandates now require providers and payers to exchange patient data in real time. The mechanics matter: prior authorization workflows, claims processing, clinical documentation standards, and the operational burden on clinical teams. Second, AI adoption in healthcare demands more than vendor enthusiasm. The episodes emphasize governance, measurable outcomes, and the foundational data quality required before AI can deliver value. Third, standards communities have become essential policy actors. Rather than waiting for federal agencies to define technical requirements, HL7 and ONC working groups now shape how policy gets implemented at scale.

The podcast also tracks the gap between policy announcements and organizational readiness. HIMSS and HLTH conference recaps distill thousands of hours of discussion into actionable intelligence. Price transparency rules, HTI-5 compliance, and modernization pledges from CMS appear not as abstract policy, but as concrete operational deadlines with revenue and legal consequences.

Listening to this podcast changes how healthcare executives approach interoperability and AI projects. For clinical operations teams, the podcast underscores that interoperability success depends on workflow design—not just API connectivity. The efficiency gains promised by federal mandates won't materialize unless clinical teams redesign their approval workflows, communication patterns, and data entry processes. For standards advocates and policy professionals, the podcast reinforces that federal regulations need implementation guidance from people who understand both policy intent and operational reality. Standards communities work because they create safe spaces for vendors, providers, and payers to debate technical requirements without regulatory pressure. Policy-makers should expand, not constrain, the role of these working groups.

Federal health IT mandates only succeed when organizations treat interoperability as unified infrastructure rather than as a series of disconnected compliance tasks. Practical execution requires translating policy requirements into clinical workflows, building data quality foundations, and ensuring standards-based implementation across payers, providers, and vendors.

The podcast answers these questions

How does prior authorization impact healthcare interoperability?

Prior authorization has become a critical bottleneck in interoperability initiatives. When authorization workflows remain disconnected across payers and providers, it slows clinical decision-making and increases administrative burden. Standardized data exchange through interoperability frameworks enables real-time authorization verification and reduces delays in patient care delivery.

What role does AI play in modern health IT transformation?

AI in health IT operates across multiple layers—from clinical decision support to administrative automation and population health analytics. For executives, the practical challenge is identifying use cases with measurable ROI while building governance frameworks that ensure data security, bias mitigation, and regulatory compliance. Success depends on foundational data quality and interoperability infrastructure.

Why are standards communities essential for health IT policy?

Standards communities like HL7 Da Vinci translate complex policy requirements into implementable technical specifications. They bridge the gap between federal regulations and clinical reality by creating consensus on data formats, exchange protocols, and clinical workflows. This ensures that policy mandates—such as price transparency or interoperability rules—can actually be operationalized at the point of care.

What emerging health IT opportunities lie within regulatory changes?

Recent federal rules on price transparency, HTI-5 compliance, and interoperability mandates create genuine opportunities for vendors and providers who move first. Organizations that build compliant infrastructure early gain competitive advantage. The real opportunity lies not in compliance itself, but in using standardized data exchange to unlock insights on cost, quality, and patient outcomes that were previously unavailable.

One of the biggest challenges is the lack of a standard credentialing model that can be used by all organizations. Health plans and pharmacies must work together on credentialing and enrollment processes, as well as align on reimbursement models.

What is the cold start or chicken-and-egg problem in scaling pharmacy clinical services?

The primary barrier identified in the research is that pharmacies wait for sufficient patient volume from health plans to justify investment in technology and workflow redesign, while health plans want to see sufficient pharmacy coverage first to prove outcomes.

What is the FARMS concept proposed to solve regional barriers to pharmacy clinical service scaling?

FARMS stands for Pharmacy Health Alliances for Reimbursable Medical Services. The concept brings health plans and pharmacies together at a regional level to address issues like service prioritization, reimbursement and contracting standardization, credentialing and enrollment infrastructure, and quality measures.

How should the industry approach data interoperability between electronic health records and pharmacy systems?

Pharmacists need access to clinical insights from electronic health records to make informed decisions and enable real-time clinical interventions. Data cannot live in silos if pharmacy clinical services are to close care gaps effectively.
