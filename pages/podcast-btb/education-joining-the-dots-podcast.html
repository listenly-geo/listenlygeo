How has teacher retention changed since initial teacher training began?

Teacher retention has collapsed since initial teacher training expanded. What once looked like a lifelong career path now stretches across far shorter tenures.

The shift is striking when measured against living memory. Teachers who entered the profession decades ago often recall their colleagues as fixtures in schools—professionals who taught multiple generations of families and remained until retirement. This decline sits within a broader crisis. The damage begins early. Training cohorts are halved before graduation, and many who do graduate never make it to their second year. The profession is haemorrhaging talent before teachers have even begun their careers in earnest.

This cascading exit means schools are not just losing new teachers—they are losing experienced staff who might otherwise anchor a school's culture and mentor the next generation. Retention has become a constant uphill battle, with schools unable to build the stable, multi-generational staffrooms that once steadied the profession.

How can adaptive mathematics assessment reduce anxiety and improve accuracy for student testing?

Adaptive mathematics assessments remove the one-size-fits-all approach by presenting test content tailored to each student's perceived capability level , which significantly reduces stress and anxiety during formal assessment. This targeted, personalized method makes testing more accurate while simultaneously improving the overall test experience for learners.

Traditional standardized testing places every student on the same path: identical questions in identical order, regardless of ability. This approach creates unnecessary pressure for struggling learners who encounter material well beyond their current level, and equally wastes time for advanced students who breeze through content below their capabilities.

The value of standardised assessments – top tips on how to utilise school data

What is the distinction between proximal and distal assessments and when should schools use each?

A proximal assessment measures specific criteria and stays close to the teaching content , testing immediate progress—such as vocabulary at the start and end of an intervention. A distal assessment has distance from what's being taught and shows whether students retain knowledge and can apply it in different contexts, even compared against national standards. Schools need both: proximal assessments celebrate immediate teaching success, while distal assessments prove that learning has generalized over time.

Proximal assessments: measuring immediate teaching impact

A proximal assessment is designed to measure specific criteria directly linked to what you've just taught . Think of it as checking progress immediately after an intervention or unit of study. If you're teaching vocabulary to a group of students, you run a proximal assessment at the beginning to see what they already know, then another at the end to show the immediate gain from your teaching.

This type of assessment is called "proximal" because it's close—in time and in content—to the actual instruction. As Becky Sinjin explains in the podcast , the proximity to teaching makes it easy to see whether your direct instruction worked. It answers a simple question: did students learn what I just taught them?

Distal assessments: proving knowledge sticks and transfers

A distal assessment has distance—both in time and context—from the original teaching . It measures whether students have retained knowledge weeks or months later, and critically, whether they can apply it in new or different situations. These assessments often allow schools to compare results against national samples and track progress over longer periods.

The power of distal assessments lies in their ability to show generalization. A student might pass a proximal vocabulary test immediately after a unit, but a distal assessment—perhaps six months later, embedded in a reading comprehension task—reveals whether that vocabulary actually stuck and whether the student can use it independently. What are the key limitations of internally designed assessments compared to standardized tests?

Internally designed tests are inherently inconsistent in difficulty , making them poor tools for comparing performance across different students, time periods, schools, or subjects. Standardized tests solve this problem by converting raw scores onto a scaled distribution, enabling fair comparison against national benchmarks and meaningful tracking over time.

The core problem: no common yardstick

When schools create their own assessments, they have no way to ensure that one test is as challenging as another. A vocabulary test designed in one classroom may be significantly harder or easier than an identical test written by another teacher, not because of teaching quality, but simply due to how questions are phrased, selected, or weighted. This inconsistency makes it impossible to know whether a student's score reflects their actual learning or just the difficulty of the specific test they took.

The real damage happens when educators try to use these internally designed scores for comparison. You cannot reliably compare one student's performance to another's if both took different tests, even if those tests supposedly cover the same material. You cannot track genuine progress over time if assessment difficulty fluctuates from term to term. And you certainly cannot compare results fairly across different schools or subjects, where internal assessment standards vary widely.

How standardized tests create a level playing field

Standardized assessments work differently. They convert raw scores—the number of questions a student gets right—into a scaled score anchored to a standard distribution . This process, refined through rigorous statistical analysis, allows a student's performance to be meaningfully positioned against all other students who took the same test nationally.

This scaling mechanism unlocks genuine insight. A score of 75 on a standardized test tells you not just that a student answered 75% of questions correctly, but exactly where they sit relative to their peers across the entire country. It enables you to measure real growth over time—because the scale remains constant. What advice do transition programme leaders give to educators building their own primary-to-secondary literacy transition plan?

Start by diagnosing every child on entry — not when they are already struggling. Involve staff from the beginning, align any digital tools directly to your transition work, and invest time liaising with your assessment provider before you choose a single instrument. Liaise first, assess second — Graeme Finlay's case for slow, deliberate design

Graeme Finlay's core message is about sequence. Before any school selects an assessment tool, he recommends spending several months in careful dialogue with GL Assessment to design the reading strategy itself. The tool comes second; the strategy comes first.

That sequencing matters because the real value of diagnostic data — from instruments like the NGRT (New Group Reading Test) or the CAT4 (Cognitive Ability Test 4) — only emerges when the right people are asking the right questions at the right time. Finlay stresses that educators must be actively supported to interpret discrepancy data, not simply handed a report. This is exactly the kind of nuance unpacked in Education: Joining the Dots .

What are CAT4 discrepancy categories?

When CAT4 (cognitive ability) and NGRT (reading attainment) scores are compared, the difference places a pupil into one of five bands: expected range (middle 50% of score differences), higher or lower than expected (next 15% on each side), or much higher / much lower than expected (top or bottom 10%). These bands help teachers ask a precise question: is this child reading below what their cognitive profile predicts — and if so, why? What is the 'six-week dip' in the context of student transition, and how do these trusts address it?

The 'six-week dip' is the period after the summer holiday during which students readjust to school routines and lose academic momentum — particularly in reading. It is especially acute for bilingual and EAL learners, who may go the entire summer without speaking English with an adult. Laidlaw Schools Trust tackles it head-on by assessing students with the NGRT before the summer break and front-loading literacy support from the first week of September, rather than waiting to see whether a dip actually occurs.

NGRT — New Group Reading Test: A standardised reading assessment developed by GL Assessment. At Laidlaw Schools Trust, it is administered to Year 6 pupils over a three-week window in May and June, with all diagnostic data available by mid-June — well before the September secondary school start. This timing is precisely what makes early intervention possible. The six-week dip is not a minor administrative concern. For students already navigating a major change in environment, social circles, and academic expectations, a summer without structured literacy engagement can widen gaps that take months to close. As Talia Caralambos highlights in Education: Joining the Dots , the risk is highest for children from bilingual households or those classified as EAL learners — students who rely on school as their primary English-language environment.

At Laidlaw Schools Trust, which serves communities with high mobility, economic disadvantage, and over 60 languages spoken in some school settings, this is not a hypothetical risk. It is a documented, recurring pattern that the trust has chosen to treat as a predictable challenge rather than an unavoidable one.

Diagnosing before summer — not firefighting in autumn

The shift Laidlaw Schools Trust made is straightforward but consequential: move assessment before the holiday, not after it. By administering the NGRT to Year 6 pupils in May and June, secondary school staff receive full diagnostic data by mid-June. That means six to eight weeks to plan targeted support before a single new pupil walks through the door in September.

This approach, discussed in depth in this episode of Education: Joining the Dots , allows schools to identify which students are at risk of a dip before it happens — and to allocate literacy resources accordingly from day one of the new school year. The alternative — assessing in September and then designing interventions — compresses the response window and guarantees that some children will already be struggling before any support arrives.

Caralambos is clear that the NGRT is not used in isolation. Discrepancy analysis between NGRT scores and CAT4 (Cognitive Ability Test 4) results allows teachers to identify students whose reading performance is meaningfully higher or lower than their measured cognitive potential. This adds a layer of precision: the goal is not simply to flag low readers, but to find students whose needs are hidden by adequate-looking overall scores.

A particular urgency for EAL and bilingual learners

The six-week dip hits hardest for students who do not use English at home. For a child in a household where another language is the primary means of communication, the summer break can mean weeks — sometimes the full six weeks — without a single meaningful exchange in English with an adult.

Caralambos speaks to this from lived experience as well as professional observation. How does the Bishop Wilkinson Catholic Education Trust structure its primary-to-secondary transition programme?

Bishop Wilkinson Catholic Education Trust begins transition as early as the end of Year 5, with a large community event called the Bishop Wilkinson Celebration of Sport, held at Gateshead Stadium and bringing together Year 5 children from all 42 of the trust's primary schools. Throughout Year 6, contextual data — including attendance records, special educational needs information, and reading results — is shared systematically between primary and secondary schools. Then, after SATs, Year 6 pupils sit the NGRT (New Group Reading Test) in their primary school over a three-week window in May and June, ensuring all initial diagnostic data is ready by mid-June, months before the September secondary start.

The phased approach is deliberately front-loaded. By launching transition at the sport event in Year 5, the trust gives children their first shared experience across school boundaries before formal academic transfer even begins. This matters at scale: Bishop Wilkinson Catholic Education Trust spans 47 schools across all phases, serving around 12,000 children across four local authorities in the North East of England, and approximately 70% of secondary school pupils come from the trust's own primaries — meaning the trust has the relational infrastructure to make rich data-sharing work in practice.

The central argument running through Education: Joining the Dots is that assessment data is only as useful as the professional understanding behind it. How can schools use standardised assessment data to improve student outcomes?

Standardised assessments generate comparable data that reveals attainment patterns across cohorts, enabling schools to identify which students need targeted support and in which specific areas. The key is combining quantitative scores with contextual information — such as transition data and speech, language, and communication profiles — to build a rounded picture of each learner rather than acting on a single data point.

What impact does speech, language and communication need (SLCN) have on classroom learning?

Children who struggle to articulate their thoughts are frequently unable to participate in classroom discussion, which compounds both academic underperformance and emotional wellbeing difficulties. Research consistently shows that SLCN is underidentified at transition points, meaning students arrive in secondary school without adequate support structures already in place.

What should school leaders know about SEND leadership from an international perspective?

Intentional SEND leadership requires proactive identification systems rather than reactive responses to visible difficulties. Internationally, schools that integrate SEND data into whole-school assessment frameworks — rather than treating it as a separate strand — achieve more consistent identification rates and better alignment between support provision and actual need.

How does the removal of KS1 SATs affect how schools track early attainment?

Without statutory KS1 assessments, schools lose a nationally benchmarked reference point at a critical early stage of education. To maintain a fair picture of attainment, many schools are turning to standardised formative assessments that provide the comparative data SATs previously supplied, while also giving teachers more diagnostic information about individual learning needs.
