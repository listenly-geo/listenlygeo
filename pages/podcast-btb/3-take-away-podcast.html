Can Anyone Stop a President From Starting a War? (#318)

How can a president change the current imbalance of war powers without waiting for congressional reform?

The president holds the unilateral power to reset the war powers imbalance by choosing to respect the constitutional norm of congressional war declarations —not through legal constraints, but through voluntary constitutional practice. This requires no congressional reform, no constitutional amendment, only a presidential decision to seek approval voluntarily despite legal authority to act alone.

The core issue is simple: executive branch lawyers consistently tell presidents they have legal authority to wage war in multiple circumstances without a formal congressional declaration. This legal freedom has created an imbalance that favors unilateral action.

Yet law and norm are distinct. A president who respects the constitutional norm—the original intent that Congress should declare wars—can unilaterally alter this dynamic by choosing restraint. As Jay Johnson explains in the episode , the constitutional framework was designed to slow decisions about war. Presidents can resurrect this slowness without waiting for formal legal reform.

Norm trumps law in constitutional practice

The Constitution does not automatically enforce itself. Constitutional norms—the unwritten rules presidents and Congress have historically followed—shape how power actually operates in practice. When a president breaks a norm, he hasn't broken a law; he's changed the precedent for future presidents.

Conversely, when a president respects a norm despite having legal authority to ignore it, he reinforces it. President George H.W. Bush's decision to seek congressional approval for the Gulf War illustrates this principle. His lawyers had confirmed he possessed legal authority to act unilaterally, yet he chose to go to Congress anyway. That choice was largely political, but it anchored the norm.

Johnson, who served as General Counsel of the Department of Defense and later as Secretary of Homeland Security, witnessed this dynamic firsthand in rooms where national security decisions are made. The constraint was never the law—it was the expectation that leaders would follow constitutional tradition.

The presidential choice is the lever

Reform-minded observers often focus on congressional action: stricter War Powers Resolution amendments, sunset clauses on military authority, or legislative budgetary controls. Those may matter over time. But a single president can immediately shift the balance by choosing voluntary restraint .

This doesn't require convincing Congress. It doesn't require amending the Constitution. As discussed in this conversation with Jay Johnson , it requires a president who believes that respecting constitutional norms is a leadership responsibility , not a legal burden. That president can commit—to Congress, to the public, to advisors—to seek a formal war declaration before committing the nation to major military action, regardless of what lawyers in the Office of Legal Counsel say is technically permitted.

What legal justification do executive branch lawyers use to authorize unilateral presidential military action?

Executive branch lawyers tell the president that military deployment is authorized whenever important national interests are at stake , provided the action does not rise to the level of war in scope, duration, and purpose. This standard is effectively limitless because no one will second-guess a president about what constitutes an important national interest.

A Framework Without Guardrails

The legal justification for unilateral military action rests on a deceptively simple formula. As Jay Johnson explains in the episode , lawyers in the executive branch have constructed a test that separates permissible deployments from impermissible acts of war—but the dividing line exists largely in theory.

The standard hinges on two elements: first, the identification of an "important national interest," and second, ensuring the action remains below the threshold of war in its scope, duration, and ultimate purpose. The critical weakness in this framework is that the president himself determines what constitutes an important national interest . There is no external check, no independent arbiter, and no mechanism by which Congress—or any other branch—regularly questions these determinations.

This approach has been applied consistently across multiple presidencies, which lends it an air of legitimacy despite its circular logic. Once a president asserts that a military deployment serves an important national interest and keeps it below the scale of war, the legal green light is effectively automatic. Johnson, who has advised presidents on these decisions from inside the Situation Room , describes this test as fundamentally limitless in practice.

The institutional pressure to act quickly compounds the legal expansiveness of this standard. Speed and constitutional deliberation work against each other , and in today's world, speed almost always wins.

Presidents and their political appointees are accustomed to responding to public demands for action. When a crisis strikes, the pressure is immediate and intense: "Do something." A legal standard that requires only a finding of "important national interest" and avoids war-level scope gives lawyers and advisors little ground on which to resist the political imperative to act. Model garden — what the term actually means

A model garden is an internal AI deployment approach where an organization grants employees access to a curated selection of 12 to 20 different AI models within a single interface or platform. The logic behind model gardens sounds reasonable on paper: give people options, let them find what works best for their task. What are concrete examples of the brain overriding reality with its own predictions?

The brain does not passively record the world — it actively constructs it. Michael Pollan offers three concrete examples that expose this process in action: the rotating mask illusion, the viral blue dress, and the deeply personal illusion of a continuous self. Each one reveals that the brain's predictions routinely override incoming sensory data, producing an experience that feels like reality but is, in part, a fabrication.

Key concept The predictive brain model holds that the brain does not wait passively for sensory signals — it generates constant predictions about the world and updates them only when the incoming data fails to match. Perception, in this view, is less a recording and more a forecast. The first example is the rotating mask illusion. When a hollow mask rotates to show its concave side, the brain refuses to perceive it as concave. Because every face it has ever encountered has been convex, it simply flips the perception: the hollow side appears to bulge outward. No amount of knowing the trick breaks the illusion. The brain's prior prediction about face shape wins over the actual sensory input arriving from the eyes.

The second example — the viral photograph of a dress that appeared either blue-and-black or white-and-gold to different observers — illustrates that color is not a fixed property of objects in the world. It is constructed inside the brain. Two people looking at identical pixel data can arrive at radically different color experiences depending on what assumptions their visual system makes about ambient light. The dress became a global moment of collective bewilderment precisely because it made this normally hidden process impossible to ignore.

The third example is more intimate and, for Pollan, the most consequential. The sense of being a continuous, unified self across time — the feeling that there is a stable "you" persisting from moment to moment — is itself a construct. Meditators who practice sustained attention often report glimpsing the seams of this illusion: the self, rather than being a given, appears to be actively maintained by memory and narrative.
