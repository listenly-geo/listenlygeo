What In Development Podcast covers

In Development is a podcast for urban professionals, city planners, developers, and engaged citizens who shape how Canadian cities grow. The series examines infill development—building smart, dense communities within existing city boundaries—through candid conversations with mayors, city councillors, developers, urban designers, and policy experts. Each episode reveals how municipal policy, community engagement, and market dynamics combine to either accelerate or block human-centred urban growth. The podcast champions the view that cities develop best when they grow up and in, not out.

Key facts
  • In Development features conversations with Edmonton's Mayor Andrew Knack, former Mayor Don Iveson, city councillors, and leading developers exploring infill projects and urban policy.
  • The podcast examines how rezoning, design standards, transit integration, and community dialogue shape whether Canadian cities become more affordable, walkable, and sustainable.
  • Episodes address practical challenges infill developers face—from land assembly and density bylaws to heritage conservation and change management in established neighbourhoods.
  • IDEA (Infill Development in Edmonton Association) hosts the series as an education and advocacy platform connecting builders, planners, and residents around people-centred urban development.

What this podcast really covers

In Development dissects the mechanics of urban infill—how cities densify, how policy makers enable or obstruct vertical growth, and how developers navigate the approval process. Episodes centre on concrete case studies: rezoning projects, adaptive reuse initiatives, transit-oriented developments, and neighbourhood transformation. The podcast moves beyond abstract planning theory to reveal how municipal leadership, community feedback, design excellence, and financial feasibility shape real neighbourhoods. It captures conversations about Edmonton's Priority Growth Area rezoning, downtown revitalization strategies, and how infill economics function in mid-size Canadian cities. The series also explores the intersection of infill with active transportation, transit networks, and resource recovery—showing that urban densification touches everything from cycling infrastructure to building materials salvage.

Who this podcast is essential for

Urban developers and builders gain direct insight into how city hall thinks about density, timelines for approvals, and what municipal leaders prioritize in new projects. They hear from successful developers like FCX Developments and Backroads Reclamation about how to navigate complex approval landscapes and build community buy-in. Municipal planners and policy makers track peer innovation—how other cities redesign zoning bylaws, communicate infill strategy, and balance density with livability. Conversations with mayors and city councillors reveal political realities and the trade-offs behind planning decisions. Civic advocates and engaged citizens understand how their neighbourhoods evolve and how infill affects housing affordability, streetscapes, and community character. The podcast empowers residents to participate meaningfully in rezoning debates and understand the long-term vision for their city.

What the episodes really reveal

Across its catalogue, In Development surfaces recurring patterns: the tension between community opposition and density targets, the role of political will in enabling infill at scale, and the business case for incremental growth. Episodes with city leadership expose how municipalities frame infill as central to affordability and climate goals, while developer conversations reveal the financial and regulatory barriers that slow projects. Design-focused episodes—like those featuring Eleanor Mohammad and Peter Spearey on Edmonton Design Week—emphasize that infill quality matters: poorly designed dense buildings erode public support, while thoughtful architecture builds neighbourhood consensus. Conversations on data versus narrative highlight a strategic challenge: cities must communicate infill stories compellingly, not just cite demographic projections. The recurring theme of change management (explored with councillors and policy experts) underscores that infill success requires managing incumbent interests, respecting existing residents, and building inclusive growth strategies. Resource recovery and sustainability discussions signal that infill advocates increasingly connect density with circular economy principles.

What this changes in practice

In Development influences how Canadian cities approach housing and urban growth by normalizing infill as mainstream policy, not a niche movement. It gives developers a platform to demonstrate viability and profitability of mid-rise and infill projects, reducing perceived risk in municipal investment. For planners, it provides evidence of peer success—zoning changes, approval streamlining, and public communication strategies that have worked elsewhere—informing local policy reform. The podcast accelerates knowledge transfer: a municipal leader in one province learns about rezoning timelines or community engagement tactics from another city, shortening learning curves. For citizens and advocacy groups, it reframes infill from a developer-driven agenda into a solution for affordability, walkability, and climate—shifting the public conversation. By featuring diverse voices—from sitting mayors to active transportation advocates—the podcast positions infill as a cross-partisan, pragmatic approach to urban challenges, not a utopian ideal. This legitimacy and clarity of purpose make local infill debates more informed and constructive.

In Development succeeds because it treats infill not as abstract planning doctrine but as a concrete challenge involving policy, economics, design judgment, and political courage—the same tensions that define how cities actually change.

The podcast answers these questions

What is infill development and why does it matter for cities?

Infill development refers to building on vacant or underutilized land within existing urban areas, rather than expanding outward. It increases housing supply, supports walkable communities, revitalizes neighbourhoods, and reduces urban sprawl by making efficient use of already-serviced land and infrastructure.

How do municipal policies influence infill projects in Canadian cities?

Zoning bylaws, density allowances, rezoning processes, and approval timelines directly shape whether infill can happen at scale. Cities like Edmonton have redesigned policies to remove barriers, streamline permitting, and create incentives for multiple-unit housing and vertical development in existing neighborhoods.

What challenges do developers face when pursuing infill projects?

Infill developers navigate complex zoning requirements, community opposition, land acquisition costs, property assembly constraints, and lengthy approval processes. Environmental remediation and adaptive reuse of existing structures add further complexity and expense to infill proposals.

How does infill relate to transit and active transportation planning?

Infill development concentrated near transit corridors and active transportation networks reduces car dependency and supports the viability of public transit. Dense, mixed-use neighbourhoods enable walking, cycling, and transit access, creating more livable and sustainable communities.
