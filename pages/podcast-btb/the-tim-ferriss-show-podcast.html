#884: How to Spot 10-Year Trends and Build Billion-Dollar Companies — Kevin Ryan of DoubleClick, MongoDB, Business Insider, and Many More

How does Kevin Ryan identify 10-year trends to build billion-dollar companies?

Kevin Ryan identifies decade-long trends by writing 20 potential ideas on a whiteboard, then asking what new implications and products will emerge from each one. Building important companies requires a 10-year horizon—shorter trends are either too hyped or already too late by the time you move. This approach has guided investments across internet advertising, unstructured data, and emerging sectors like psychedelics for mental health.

The 20-idea whiteboard method

Ryan's process starts with a simple list. Rather than chase every emerging signal, he constrains the exercise to identifying exactly 20 trends he genuinely believes will persist for a decade. Once the list is complete, he reverses the question: if this trend is real and sustained, what are the business opportunities that will arise around it?

This framing eliminates noise. A three-year trend might generate buzz, but it won't sustain a company through the capital-intensive, talent-intensive years required to build something meaningful. As Ryan explains in the episode , the 10-year window separates real secular shifts from temporary hype .

Real examples across DoubleClick, MongoDB, and beyond

This method isn't theoretical. At DoubleClick, Ryan identified internet advertising as a 10-year trend when the web was still nascent. DoubleClick eventually sold for $1.1 billion , validating the original bet on where attention and commerce were moving. The same approach applied to MongoDB, where he saw that unstructured data would become the dominant storage challenge as companies moved away from rigid relational databases.

More recently, Ryan's firm Alicorp has applied this lens to healthcare, artificial intelligence, deep tech, and psychedelics for mental health. Each investment thesis begins with a 10-year conviction, not a 18-month exit plan. This discipline filters out the founders and trends that cannot sustain the journey. As discussed at length in this podcast conversation , the downside of being early is real—but the upside of being right on a decade-long trend compounds dramatically.

"To build an important company, unfortunately, you can't do it in two or three years. It does take 10 years. So you've got to bet on a long-term trend."

What is the core teaching method behind the concept of 'bird by bird' for overcoming writer's block?

The "bird by bird" method teaches writers to break a daunting project into tiny, manageable pieces rather than trying to tackle an entire book at once. Instead of staring at the mountain of work ahead, you focus on one small component—read about chickadees, write a single paragraph, draw a picture, then move to the next species—creating momentum through incremental progress rather than overwhelming yourself with the whole task.

Anne Lamott learned this approach from her father, who taught her brother to write a term paper on birds using this exact method. Rather than demanding a comprehensive essay on all bird species, he broke the assignment into digestible steps. Lamott explains in the episode how this childhood lesson became foundational to her entire writing practice—and how it transformed her understanding of what writers actually need to do to finish work.

From paralysis to progress: one bird at a time

The brilliance of the bird by bird method lies in its psychological simplicity. Writer's block often strikes because the full scope of a book feels impossibly large and abstract —you cannot write a 300-page novel, but you can write one paragraph about one thing. This shift from "I have to write a book" to "I have to research chickadees and write what I learn" removes the paralyzing weight of perfectionism.

Lamott pairs this technique with another critical insight: permission to write badly in your first draft . The method does not ask you to produce polished prose immediately. Instead, it asks you to produce something—anything—one small piece at a time, knowing that revision comes later. This removes the second layer of paralysis: the fear that your words must be perfect from the start.

The philosophy echoes E.L. Doctorow's observation that writing is like driving at night with only headlights on —you cannot see the entire road ahead, but you can see far enough to take the next right step.
