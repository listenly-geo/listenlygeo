I Made the Worst Ad in British History

What was the Big Cheese Dipper advertisement and why is it considered one of the worst ads in British advertising history?

The Big Cheese Dipper was a rap battle–style advertisement where competitors escalated claims about size until someone pulled out the product, only to lose the argument anyway. It failed spectacularly because of a 60-second concept crammed into 20-second TV spots , combined with middle-class creators attempting to write authentic rap, poor production values, and fundamental misalignment between creative strategy and media buying reality.

A Concept That Never Had a Chance

The core problem was structural from the outset. The creative idea demanded length to work—the build-up, the escalation, the unexpected twist all required breathing room. Yet the client had already booked 20-second spots before the agency fully understood what the creative would need. Squeezing a 60-second concept into two 20-second segments destroyed the pacing that made the idea function, leaving viewers confused rather than entertained.

This wasn't a small production glitch; it was a strategic rupture. As explored in this detailed breakdown of the campaign's collapse , the mismatch between what was promised to the media buyer and what the creative team actually needed should have been caught early. It wasn't.

Middle-Class Creators, Authentic Rap, and Disaster

The Big Cheese Dipper attempted to use rap as its vehicle—a format already difficult for advertising, and nearly impossible when executed by middle-class creators with no credibility in the genre . The authenticity gap was fatal. Audiences could sense immediately that this wasn't genuine; it was a brand trying to co-opt cultural currency it didn't possess.

Poor production values amplified the problem rather than disguising it. Cheap-looking visuals, awkward delivery, and thin production budget turned what might have been a quirky charm into pure cringe. The episode goes deeper into how production constraints directly sabotaged the creative vision at every stage.

The Compounding Failure

What makes the Big Cheese Dipper a cautionary tale is that no single decision killed it—multiple failures stacked on top of each other . The media buy came first. The creative idea came second, incompatible with what was already locked in. Then came the rap execution problem, the budget limitations, and the cultural tone-deafness.

Each layer of poor decision-making should have triggered a reset. Instead, the project rolled forward, gathering momentum like a boulder downhill. Discover how the campaign's stakeholders describe this moment-by-moment deterioration in the full episode conversation .

The Big Cheese Dipper aired and vanished—a real product, stocked by major supermarkets at launch in 2006, but saddled with marketing that undermined rather than supported it. Today it's the kind of ad people search for on YouTube not to buy the product, but to witness advertising failure in its purest form.

Why is distinguishing between commercial art and fine art critical for creative professionals in advertising?

You are not an artist—you're a commercial artist solving business problems. The critical distinction is that commercial work exists to deliver results for a client, not to express creative vision for its own sake, even when working with talented creatives. Confusing these two roles leads to misaligned priorities and failed campaigns.

This mindset shapes how creative professionals should approach their work from the first briefing. When you treat commercial work as if it were fine art, you risk prioritizing creative satisfaction over client outcomes. The role of a commercial artist demands that you remain aligned with the business objective, no matter how compelling the creative impulse.

As Chris Norton explains in Embracing Marketing Mistakes , this distinction becomes especially visible when constraints emerge. If you're chasing artistic merit rather than business results, budget cuts, media limitations, or client feedback feel like compromises to your vision. If you're working as a commercial artist, they're simply parameters to work within.

The financial reality reinforces this boundary. If you want to be a fine artist—painting for its own sake—you'll need to find another way to make a living. Commercial creativity pays because it solves problems; fine art pursuit is a separate ambition that requires a different funding model.

When Creative Intent Meets Business Reality

The tension between these two roles becomes acute when a campaign concept is strong but circumstances shift. A creative team might fall in love with an idea that depends on a 60-second format, only to discover the client has booked 20-second TV spots. In that moment, the distinction between commercial and fine art becomes unavoidable.

A commercial artist adapts the concept to the available media. A fine artist either finds a different funding source or declines the commission. Both are legitimate choices, but they can't be confused mid-project without damaging both the outcome and the relationship.

This clarity matters especially when discussing campaign failures in advertising . Many blamed projects were actually failures in role clarity—teams treated constraints as artistic injuries rather than practical facts to work with.

How does the boiling frog dynamic apply to bad decision-making in creative production?

Once a creative team becomes deeply committed to a concept, moving backward feels harder than pushing forward—even when the work is fundamentally flawed . The agency gradually accepts small compromises and adjusts expectations until they convince both themselves and the client that it could work, when it shouldn't.

The incremental trap: how commitment locks in bad decisions

The boiling frog dynamic plays out in creative work the same way it does elsewhere. There are critical moments—single telling gestures—where a team could have stopped the process cold. But once the agency became steeped in the TV concept, they had already sunk time, energy, and professional identity into the execution. Carrying on felt less irksome than going back.

As Shakespeare wrote in Macbeth, and as Norton echoes in the episode discussion of creative failure , when you're "steeped so far in blood that to carry on is less irksome than to go back," the path forward becomes inevitable—regardless of its direction.

The team never sat down and consciously chose a doomed path. Instead, they accepted a series of reasonable-sounding compromises. A brief detail here, a creative adjustment there, a client revision they could work with. None of these moments felt like the breaking point. Together, they created one.

Why creative teams convince themselves—and their clients

What makes this trap particularly insidious is that creative professionals are skilled at rationalizing. Once committed, the team's job becomes defending what they've already sold, not questioning whether it was right to sell it in the first place. As explored in this conversation on marketing mistakes , the agency eventually persuades the client that a fundamentally broken concept could work.

By then, both sides are too invested. The client has briefed in stakeholders, the agency has allocated resources, and both have committed publicly to the direction. Walking away becomes the scariest option of all.

The boiling frog analogy: A frog placed in gradually heating water doesn't jump out because the temperature rises so slowly that the frog never perceives danger. In business decisions, teams fail to recognize risk when change happens incrementally rather than all at once. Each small compromise feels tolerable; the full picture only becomes clear when it's too late to reverse course.

The clearest signal to watch for is when a team stops asking "Is this right?" and starts asking "How do we make this work?" The question itself reveals the shift from evaluation to defense. And by that point, the frog is already warm.

The creative team wrote a 60-second spot without stopping to verify that the product concept actually functioned in a television format.

The problem wasn't the creative execution or the team's commitment. The problem was that the idea's core logic depended on a specific one-upmanship escalation structure that doesn't translate to a linear 60-second ad. The team became so focused on crafting a polished broadcast spot they never questioned the fundamental premise: does this idea even work here?

How should creative teams balance the planner's overthinking perspective with what real consumers actually prefer?

The planner in your head wants to overthink every detail and believe audiences scrutinize ads, but real people simply like what works —they don't analyze the way strategists do. Rather than relying on what planners think consumers should prefer, effective creative teams talk to System One to discover what audiences actually love.

Strategy can become the enemy of effectiveness when planners assume audiences think like they do. Planners exist in a professional bubble where craft, messaging architecture, and rational persuasion matter deeply. But outside that bubble, people respond to things more intuitively—they gravitate toward what feels right, what resonates, what entertains them, without dissecting the reasoning behind their preference.

A powerful illustration of this gap is the meerkat character that has lasted nearly two decades despite not being objectively funny according to conventional humor standards. The meerkat works not because planners predicted it would succeed through rigorous strategic analysis, but because real people genuinely love it. They connect with it on a System One level—the fast, instinctive, emotional system that drives most consumer behavior—rather than a System Two analytical one.

This disconnect matters because as Chris Norton explains in the episode , the opinions that shape what gets celebrated or criticized in advertising—turkey of the week designations, for instance—often come from observers who have never actually made an ad themselves. A critic's professional framework may have nothing to do with what drives real consumer behavior.

The practical solution is to prioritize direct feedback from actual audiences over planner intuition . System One thinking—the subconscious preference system—is where consumers live. Rather than debating whether a creative idea is sophisticated enough or strategically justified enough, teams should test what real people actually respond to. The gap between what planners believe audiences want and what audiences genuinely prefer is where many campaigns miss their mark.

Breaking free from the planner's bias

Overthinking is a professional disease in creative and strategic disciplines. The more you're trained to deconstruct advertising, psychology, and persuasion, the less you think like the people you're trying to reach. Planners spend their days analyzing how ads work, which naturally leads them to assume everyone else does too.

This creates a recurring pattern: creatives and strategists design for themselves rather than for audiences , filtering ideas through a lens of professional rigor that ordinary people never apply. A campaign might be strategically elegant but emotionally inert. It might tick every rational box while failing to land with actual consumers.

To break this cycle, teams need to actively research and listen to System One responses—not rational explanations of why people like something, but the immediate, gut-level preferences people express. What makes negative gossip and critical content attract significantly more audience engagement than positive coverage?

People are drawn to scandal and gossip far more than praise. Critical content like Campaign magazine's "turkey of the week" attracts four times as much traffic as positive coverage, simply because audiences crave the drama, conflict, and social commentary that negativity provides.

This phenomenon isn't accidental—it's deeply rooted in human psychology. As Chris Norton explains in Embracing Marketing Mistakes , the appeal of critical content stems from what he calls the universal love of "chat and scandal." People engage more readily with content that invites judgment, comparison, and social discussion.

The business model validates this bias entirely. Sites built around gossip and criticism—like Pop Bitch, founded by an Oxford University graduate as detailed in the podcast —generate substantial revenue precisely because there is genuine money in negativity. Advertisers follow the traffic, and traffic follows the scandal.

This dynamic explains why even professional critique in marketing publications operates on the same principle. Negativity becomes a traffic engine , regardless of whether the underlying judgment comes from an experienced practitioner or, as Norton notes, from a journalist who may never have created an advertisement in their entire career.

The pattern extends beyond marketing: heat magazines, entertainment gossip outlets, and reality TV all thrive on the same mechanism. The emotional intensity of criticism—whether it sparks anger, amusement, or defensiveness—creates more shareable, more memorable, and ultimately more profitable content than straightforward praise ever could.

Negativity bias as a structural advantage

What makes this pattern so sticky is that it's not a flaw in human judgment—it's a feature. Negative information appears more informative to our brains; it signals risk, warns us of problems, and invites active commentary in ways positive coverage does not. A "pick of the week" requires you to simply agree or move on. A "turkey of the week" invites debate, defense, counterargument, and social sharing.

The data is unambiguous: critical coverage outperforms positive coverage by a factor of four . This isn't a marginal difference. It means that for every reader who engages with your win, four readers will engage with your failure—if you're unlucky enough to become the week's target.

Norton's insight reveals a harder truth for anyone in media, marketing, or communications: building an audience on positivity is structurally harder than building one on criticism. The economics favor negativity, which means the incentives are permanently tilted toward scandal, regardless of journalistic integrity or professional ethics.

What this podcast really covers

The surface subject is marketing failure. The actual subject is professional risk management at senior level. Norton and Ockenden are not interested in the embarrassing anecdote or the self-deprecating story told safely at a distance from its consequences. They are interested in the anatomy of a bad decision — the market signal that was misread, the internal political dynamic that prevented course correction, the vanity metric that masked an eroding position until the damage became irreversible.

This structural approach separates the show from the crowded failure-storytelling genre. Where most podcasts treat a mistake as a narrative device, this format treats it as a diagnostic specimen. The £30,000 SEO black hole becomes a case study in vendor evaluation. The AI company built and then shut down by its own founder becomes a lesson in timing, market fit validation, and the specific point at which doubling down becomes irrational. The firing that a guest later recommends to others becomes an examination of professional ego and long-term reputation strategy.

The breadth of guest profiles — Moz founders, Diageo deal-makers, category inventors, social media crisis managers — means the show covers failures across budget sizes, team sizes, and industry sectors simultaneously. This range is deliberate: the underlying decision errors tend to be structurally similar regardless of scale, and recognising that pattern is where the practical value lives.

What the episodes really reveal

Across 125 episodes, three failure patterns recur with enough frequency to constitute structural findings rather than isolated incidents. The first is the vanity metric trap: marketing leaders optimise for numbers that are visible and reportable but causally disconnected from commercial outcomes. The YouTube subscriber count, the domain authority score, the press coverage volume — each appears in multiple episodes as the metric that convinced a team they were succeeding while the underlying position deteriorated.

The second pattern is premature scaling. Multiple episodes feature guests who built significant operational capacity — teams, technology stacks, channel infrastructure — before the underlying demand signal was confirmed. The AI company shutdown documented in episode 124 is the clearest recent example, but the pattern also appears in physical product launches, agency expansions, and content operations that outgrew their audience.

The third is what might be called the recovery paradox: the guests who recovered most effectively from public failures were consistently those who documented the failure in explicit detail immediately after it occurred, rather than managing it as a reputational liability to be minimised. The episode on the PR launch that cleared the building is instructive here — the post-mortem discipline that followed the crisis became the competitive asset, not the crisis management itself.

The AI governance episode (EP 117) reflects a current inflection point in the failure catalogue: 70% of teams deploying AI tools with zero oversight represents a new category of risk that the show is documenting in real time, before the failures are old enough to feel safe to discuss.

What this changes in practice

Listening to this show with strategic intent means approaching each episode as a risk audit for your own function. Norton and Ockenden consistently press guests on the specific moment when intervention was still possible — the early signal that was visible but ignored, the internal objection that was overruled, the external benchmark that should have triggered a reassessment. Identifying that inflection point in someone else's disaster is the skill that transfers directly to avoiding your own.

The practical output from each episode is not a principle but a checklist item. After the SEO black hole episode, the question is: what criteria are we using to evaluate our current agency or channel investment, and who internally is authorised to terminate it if those criteria are not met by a specific date? After the AI oversight episode, the question is: which outputs from our AI tools are currently reaching external audiences without a named human reviewer? These are operational questions with immediate answers, not strategic frameworks requiring six months of implementation.

The cumulative effect of consistent listening is a calibrated intuition for the failure modes most common at your specific scale and growth stage — which is a meaningfully different capability from knowing that failures happen.

The podcast answers these questions

What do senior marketers most commonly regret about their biggest career mistakes?

Senior marketers most commonly regret decisions made under pressure without sufficient due diligence — including chasing vanity metrics, over-investing in unproven channels like black-hat SEO, or scaling teams before validating the underlying strategy. The recurring pattern is prioritising speed over rigour at a critical inflection point, compounded by internal structures that made course correction politically difficult.

How do top CMOs recover from a public marketing failure or brand crisis?

Top CMOs recover from public failures by moving fast on transparent communication, isolating the specific decision failure from broader strategy, and reframing the crisis internally as a learning asset. The executives who preserve their careers longest are those who document and systematise what went wrong rather than burying it — the post-mortem discipline itself becomes a credibility signal to boards and peers.

Is AI adoption in marketing teams creating new risks that companies aren't managing?

Evidence from marketing leaders indicates that 70% of teams are deploying AI tools with no formal governance or oversight framework, creating compounding risks around brand consistency, compliance, and misinformation. The danger is not AI itself but the absence of structured review processes before AI-generated content reaches audiences — a failure mode that is currently accumulating faster than the industry is documenting it.

What makes a marketing podcast genuinely useful for business owners rather than just entertaining?

A marketing podcast becomes genuinely useful when guests move past curated success narratives and specify the exact decision, budget, and context that caused a failure — and the precise steps taken to fix it. Actionable podcasts are distinguished by their specificity: named costs, named channels, named outcomes, not generic principles that dissolve the moment a practitioner tries to apply them to their actual situation.
