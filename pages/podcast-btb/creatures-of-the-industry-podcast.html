What role did the monthly scaffold meetings at Trades Hall play in maintaining industry standards and knowledge sharing?

The monthly scaffold meetings at Trades Hall, chaired by Normie Wallace, functioned as a collaborative learning forum built on peer accountability and lived experience . Held in the round with workers seated on wooden benches facing each other, these gatherings became a place to learn custom and practice through debate and discussion, with Wallace ensuring rank-and-file members held each other to standards based on actual hands-on knowledge.

A semicircle of accountability

The physical setup of these meetings reflected their purpose: workers arranged in a semicircle created a family-gathering atmosphere where no one person stood above the rest. This horizontal layout broke down hierarchy and invited equal participation. Everyone could see everyone else, making it harder to hide inaccuracy or dismiss concerns as coming from an outsider.

Normie Wallace's role as chair was not to lecture but to enforce honesty. As discussed in Creatures of the Industry , Wallace was unrelenting at pulling people up when they misrepresented facts or mentioned someone doing something wrong . He did not allow talk to drift into gossip or rumor; instead, he grounded every discussion in verifiable experience and actual practice.

Learning through debate, not decree

These meetings were not training sessions run by management or external experts—they were spaces for workers to learn custom and practice through peer-to-peer debate . A scaffolder sharing a method had to defend it against scrutiny. Someone describing an accident or near-miss faced questions about their own role and judgment.

This approach ensured that industry standards were not just written rules handed down from above. Instead, they emerged from collective wisdom. What structural failures occur when scaffold standards are not staggered properly?

When scaffold standards are not staggered and all joins align on a single level, the entire structure can collapse catastrophically , with standards pulling out of their connections and the scaffold hanging into the street or surrounding area.

This structural principle lies at the heart of scaffold safety, yet it represents one of the most dangerous oversights in construction. Terry Riggs witnessed this failure firsthand during his decades of work across Australian building sites.

A Real Collapse: Russell Street, 1980s

During the 1980s, Terry Riggs observed a scaffolding disaster on Russell Street that exemplified this structural weakness. The scaffold had been erected without proper staggering of the standard joints—meaning every vertical post connection aligned at the same height.

The result was immediate and catastrophic: the standards pulled out of their spigots on the building side , causing the entire scaffolding to hang loose into Russell Street below. This wasn't a minor failure or a minor slump. This was a complete structural breach that exposed workers and the public to severe danger.

Such incidents reveal more than just an engineering mistake. They signal, as discussed in the episode , a loss of the skill and knowledge that had previously governed how scaffolds were properly erected—knowledge passed through hands-on experience and custom practice among experienced scaffolders.

What safety risks emerged from scaffold construction methods using boom lifts instead of conventional hanging methods?

Boom lifts introduced a critical construction flaw: not all fittings were properly completed during assembly. When a scaffold was built using a boom lift rather than conventional hanging methods, the transom fitting could go unfinished while the ledger fitting was secured , leaving tubes vulnerable to collapse.

Terry Riggs witnessed this danger firsthand in Port Hedland around 1998. At a 70-metre tower, a tube dropped because while the ledger fitting had been tightened, the transom fitting remained incomplete. The tube fell with terrifying speed—saved from catastrophe only by a vessel's protrusion that broke its fall. Without that chance obstruction, Riggs and his off-sider would have come off the scaffold entirely.

This wasn't an isolated mishap. The shift from conventional hanging scaffold methods to boom lift assembly fundamentally changed how work was sequenced on site. In hanging scaffold construction, workers had time and physical access to verify every fitting was properly torqued before moving to the next stage. Boom lifts accelerated the process but introduced gaps in the workflow where supervisors might assume fittings were complete without verification.

As discussed at length in this podcast episode , the real danger lay in the assumption. A ledger fitting done up could mask an incomplete transom fitting on the opposite side of the joint, creating an asymmetric load condition that destabilized the entire vertical support.

What were the physical toll and occupational health impacts on contract scaffolders carrying heavy loads?

Most scaffolders wore out their knees or backs from carrying heavy, concentrated loads, typically on one shoulder without alternating. A right-handed scaffolder would carry the load on the right shoulder, putting a big load on the right knee, which eventually failed for most people — the cumulative vertical strain from knees to hips to shoulders meant something in the system would inevitably fail.

The occupational health crisis among scaffolders was rooted in a simple structural reality: 95% of scaffolding work involves carrying tons of steel to or away from the job site . Unlike machinery or tools that distribute load evenly, steel beams and scaffold components are rigid, heavy objects that demand one-sided body positioning across an entire working career.

This wasn't a matter of poor technique or weak workers. Experienced scaffolders like Terry Riggs, who worked on major projects including offshore construction at Barry Beach from 1978 to 1983 , faced the same inevitable wear. The problem was systemic to the work itself—no amount of skill or care could prevent the toll of sustained asymmetrical loading on a single side of the body.

Why the right shoulder and knee failed first

For right-handed workers, the pattern was predictable. Picking up a scaffold tube or steel beam naturally fell to the dominant side, making the right shoulder the primary load bearer. This created a cascade of failure: the concentrated weight stressed the right knee, which bore the gravitational and stabilizing load unevenly , then radiating upward through the hip, spine, and shoulders.

The body's interconnected skeletal system meant that compensating for knee pain shifted strain to the lower back and hips. A worker might favour their left leg to ease right knee pressure, only to develop chronic back or hip issues. The cumulative injury pattern was almost universal among long-term scaffolders, as detailed in the full Creatures of the Industry episode .

The lack of rotation and recovery

Scaffolding work offered almost no mechanism for load alternation. Unlike jobs where workers might rotate between different tasks or switch shoulders mid-day, scaffolding demanded continuous, repetitive carrying of heavy objects in the same body position. There was no built-in recovery time for asymmetrical strain to dissipate .

This relentless, single-sided loading distinguished scaffolding from other physically demanding trades. A concrete worker or bricklayer might vary their movements and body positions throughout the day. A scaffolder, by contrast, executed the same shoulder-load motion thousands of times across decades, with minimal deviation. The interview reveals how Riggs and his peers simply accepted this as the cost of the trade —a silent occupational sacrifice that nobody talked about until bodies failed.

The pathway combined practical apprenticeship with institutional learning. After working on site for the duration of their permit period, apprentice scaffolders enrolled in classes at technical institutes like Swinburne. What were the working conditions and labour practices on a 2,000-person industrial project in Port Hedland during the late 1990s?

The Port Hedland project in the late 1990s had virtually no union representation or organizational structure —no shop committees, no delegate committees, and almost no worker voice despite employing 2,000 people. Workers faced a casualized, de-unionized environment with half a dozen different pay rates , with some receiving cash bonuses collected before leave periods, while scaffolders were among the few who maintained any autonomy within their crews.

A fragmented workforce with no collective voice

On a site of that scale, the absence of formal worker organization was striking. Riggs describes the Port Hedland project as heavily casualized, meaning workers were hired on temporary, short-term contracts with minimal job security. There was no union solidarity whatsoever , even as workers came from all backgrounds—Italian, Greek, Spanish, Irish, English, and Fijian origins all represented on the same job.

The pay structure itself reinforced fragmentation. With workers receiving multiple rates across the workforce , there was no unified wage baseline. This system made it nearly impossible for workers to organize collectively around pay and conditions, as no two workers necessarily shared the same deal.

What impact did the Occupational Health and Safety Act introduced in 1985 have on workplace practices in Australian heavy industry?

The Occupational Health and Safety Act, introduced in 1985, gave workers unprecedented legal power to challenge unsafe practices. Terry Riggs, working as a health and safety representative at Shell Refinery, issued 12 pin notices on safety violations—all but one upheld by WorkSafe—forcing the company to implement major changes and make employers face real consequences for the first time.

Workers gained genuine enforcement power

Before 1985, safety violations in Australian heavy industry were largely unpoliced from the ground level. The new act equipped workers themselves with the authority to document breaches and escalate them to the regulator, fundamentally shifting the balance of power on job sites. Riggs, trained through an associate diploma in occupational health and safety at Holmesglen TAFE, became one of the first generation of safety representatives to operate under the new framework.

At the Shell Refinery during a major upgrade project involving a new cracker unit, as described in the episode , Riggs tested the act's teeth. Eleven of his twelve pin notices were upheld by WorkSafe , meaning the regulator agreed with his findings and forced Shell to correct the violations. That compliance rate was staggering in an industry where management had previously operated with minimal oversight.

A major shock to employer culture

Employers in heavy industry had never experienced this kind of scrutiny before. Riggs recalls the moment as "a big slap around the face" for companies that assumed workplace safety was their exclusive domain to manage—or ignore. The act didn't just penalise unsafe conditions; it empowered workers to name them and demand remediation in real time.

Rather than accept defeat, some employers learned a critical lesson. According to the full episode , management subsequently developed "a few paper skills which covered some of the failings of their systems"—meaning they began documenting and formalizing safety procedures to avoid future challenges.
