Why do financial advisors typically discourage their clients from investing in alternative investments?

Financial advisors actively discourage alternatives because they lack foundational knowledge of this asset class and aren't required to understand it . This knowledge gap feeds fear and misconceptions, leading advisors to portray alts as risky and fringe—a position conveniently protected by their existing conflicts of interest and their commission-based models tied to traditional public markets.

Knowledge gaps and structural incentives

Alternative investments fall entirely outside the standard training and licensing framework for most financial advisors. As Bob Frazier explains in the episode , alternatives are not part of an advisor's database or required curriculum . Without this foundation, advisors rely on stereotypes rather than understanding—they position alts as "wing nutty, edgy, fringy, and super risky" despite the fact that you can equally make poor investments in public stocks.

The deeper issue is structural: most financial advisors earn commissions or assets under management (AUM) fees from traditional investments. Directing a client toward private credit, distressed debt, or other alternatives means losing that recurring revenue stream. The incentive system itself is misaligned with what might genuinely serve the client's portfolio.

A valuable profession hampered by blind spots

Frazier acknowledges that financial advisors do serve a legitimate purpose—managing relationships, enforcing discipline, and keeping clients from making purely emotional decisions. However, very few advisors actually understand alternatives well enough to evaluate them properly . That gap doesn't just limit choice; it actively biases the conversation away from an entire category of investments.

This matters because, as discussed further in this conversation on alternative investment characteristics , professionally managed private credit and distressed debt can offer stability that public markets don't—structured terms, predictable cash flows, and downside protection mechanisms that traditional advisors rarely explain to their clients.

How does the SEC regulation Reg D 506C enable alternative investment offerings?

Reg D 506C removes the ban on general advertising for private investment deals, allowing issuers to publicly market offerings to accredited investors —those meeting specific SEC wealth or income thresholds. Only accredited investors can participate, making it a pathway for alternative investments like private credit to reach a broader audience while maintaining regulatory compliance.

The accredited investor threshold that unlocks market access

The SEC defines accredited investors by clear, measurable criteria: $1 million in net worth excluding your primary home , or $200,000 in annual income (individually) or $300,000 as a combined household income. These thresholds determine who legally qualifies to invest in Reg D 506C offerings.

As Bob Frazier explains in the episode , this regulatory framework is what allows firms like Aspen Funds to market alternative investments directly rather than relying on private networks and referrals alone. The certification removes friction from capital raising by allowing deals to be advertised openly to a defined, qualified pool of investors.

Why marketing permission changes the deal-raising game

Traditional private offerings under other Reg D exemptions required no public advertising whatsoever —all investors had to come through existing relationships. Reg D 506C flips this entirely, permitting issuers to actively promote their deals while the accreditation requirement acts as the legal gatekeeper.

This matters because, as discussed in this conversation on alternative investment strategy , the bottleneck in raising capital is rarely the deals themselves—it's finding investors who have both capital and sophistication. Reg D 506C solves for reach without sacrificing safety, letting firms scale their capital base faster than cold pitching ever could.

They combine low risk with high monthly cash flows, liquidity through quarterly redemption windows, and often deliver double-digit returns—a profile rarely found in traditional real estate or stock investing.

The structural appeal lies in the passivity. Unlike owning commercial property directly, where you manage tenants, renovations, and market timing, private credit funds handle all operations while returning predictable cash distributions to investors. This distinction matters profoundly when market conditions deteriorate.

As Bob Frazier explains in the episode , private credit achieves what many investors chase unsuccessfully: consistent returns without the operational burden. What separates billionaire investors from everyday investors when evaluating investment risk?

Billionaire investors start by asking one question: how do I avoid losing money? Capital preservation comes before chasing returns. Warren Buffett famously said the number one rule of investing is don't lose money, and the number two rule is don't forget rule number one. A volatile portfolio with high returns actually leaves you with less money at the end than a steady, boring one—because losses interrupt compounding.

Why compounding breaks when losses hit

The difference between a billionaire investor and an everyday investor isn't about ambition or risk tolerance—it's about understanding what stops growth dead. When you lose money, you don't just lose the capital itself. You lose all the future compounding that money would have generated. If you're down 20%, you need a 25% return just to break even. Down 50%? You need a 100% gain to recover.

This is why portfolio volatility becomes a silent wealth killer. Two portfolios might show the same average return on paper, but the one that swings wildly—booming one year, crashing the next—will end with less total wealth. The math is ruthless: steady compounding at 8% beats erratic swings between +20% and -10% even when the arithmetic average looks identical.

The risk-first framework that separates the winners

As Bob Frazier explains in the episode , this mentality shows up in how billionaires structure their portfolios. They don't ask "How much can I make?" first. They ask "Where does this break? What's the downside? What happens if everything goes wrong?" Only after stress-testing the worst case do they build the upside on top.

This is the opposite of how most everyday investors think. The common approach is to find a promising opportunity, hope it works out, and cross fingers. Billionaires reverse the order entirely. They design portfolios that can survive a downturn, then stack gains on top of a stable foundation. The result: less volatility, more compound wealth, and peace of mind.

What are the two distinct economic models for career paths, and how do they apply to commercial real estate roles?

Commercial real estate careers split between two economic models: the time-and-effort economy, where income depends on showing up and working your hours, and the results economy, where income is entirely tied to what you produce, regardless of hours spent. The industry has traditionally been results-focused, but it is shifting to include more stable time-and-effort roles like property managers and salary-based positions blending base pay with commission—and choosing the right model for your temperament is essential to long-term career satisfaction.

The two models and how they shape your career

In the time-and-effort economy, you are paid for your presence and labor —salary, hourly wages, or benefits tied to consistent work. This model appeals to people who value predictability, security, and a stable paycheck. You know exactly what you will earn each month, and there is no risk of income collapse if you hit a temporary market downturn or a slow sales cycle.

The results economy operates on the opposite principle: your income depends entirely on outcomes you produce —commissions on deals closed, profits from investments, or revenue from your client base. There is no safety net; if you don't sell, you don't earn. But there is also no ceiling: your income is limited only by your ability to generate results.

Historically, commercial real estate has been built on the results economy . Brokers earn commissions, investors profit from deal performance, developers reap returns from projects. But as discussed in the Commercially Speaking Podcast episode , the industry is evolving. Property managers, asset managers, and increasingly, brokerage firms are offering salary-based and hybrid compensation packages that blend base pay with performance incentives, creating a middle ground for professionals who don't want pure commission risk.

Matching the model to your temperament and life stage

Not everyone thrives under the same system. Results-based roles demand tolerance for uncertainty and the ability to self-motivate without a guaranteed paycheck . They suit people who are competitive, driven by control, and willing to bet on their own productivity. They also require either capital reserves or the freedom to survive lean periods—a significant barrier for early-career professionals or anyone with dependents.

Time-and-effort roles attract people who prioritize security and prefer a predictable income stream . They work well for those managing family obligations, building an emergency fund, or simply wanting to separate their livelihood from the stress of variable earnings. As explained in this podcast , some professionals need this stability to function at their best; others feel constrained by it.

The critical insight is that one model is not superior to the other. Career satisfaction hinges on alignment between your economic model and your personal values . Placing a high-energy, self-directed person in a salaried role can lead to frustration and underperformance. Similarly, forcing a security-minded professional into pure commission work creates constant anxiety. Understanding which economy you belong in is a prerequisite for long-term success in commercial real estate.
