Why does ER Certification not engage in fire door certification despite industry demand?

Fire door certification remains prohibitively expensive and resource-intensive to implement, while ER Certification is already operating at sufficient growth across existing window certification schemes in Europe, North America, and emerging markets. The cost and complexity of fire door testing outweigh the business case for a company already managing multiple established certification programs across Poland, Lithuania, Turkey, and with American certification bodies.

While there is clear market demand for fire door certification services, ER Certification has chosen to focus its operational capacity on window certification schemes where it already maintains strong market presence. As John Warburton explains, the company does provide some fire-rated unit certification through cascaded data from fire glass suppliers , meaning they indirectly support fire-rated products without undertaking full fire door testing themselves.

The scale of investment required for fire door testing infrastructure makes it an unattractive addition to the current business model. Fire door certification demands specialized testing equipment, controlled laboratory conditions, and expert personnel—all significant capital and ongoing operational costs. For a certification body already achieving steady growth in its core window and door markets, diverting resources to fire doors represents an inefficient allocation of management time and technical expertise.

ER Certification's geographic expansion strategy reflects this logic: rather than broaden into adjacent certification categories, the company is deepening its presence in proven markets. This approach has delivered consistent results, and as Warburton discusses in the full podcast episode , the decision keeps the organization lean and focused on services where it can deliver measurable value to clients.

A pragmatic choice: depth over breadth

The decision to bypass fire door certification is ultimately a strategic one rooted in resource allocation and risk management. Many smaller and mid-sized certification bodies face this choice regularly—enter a new market segment that requires substantial upfront investment, or consolidate leadership in existing territories where demand is already proven and operational systems are mature.

For ER Certification, the latter path has proven commercially sound. The company purchased the business from founder Bill Keaton just three years before this interview and has since expanded its scheme coverage and geographic footprint significantly. What is the PLC scheme and how does it differ from other thermal performance certification databases?

The PLC scheme (Part L Compliance) was set up last year to provide detailed, transparent approvals for window and door specifications —showing exactly which frame types, glass configurations, and performance details are approved on a searchable public database. Other certification schemes only confirm that a company can make an A-rated window without publishing the specifics, making the PLC approach fundamentally more useful and verifiable for specifiers and manufacturers.

Unlike traditional certification databases that keep approval details opaque or generic, the PLC scheme publishes granular specification information online with filterable search . This means you can search for the exact frame profile, glass type, seal configuration, and thermal U-value combination you need—and immediately see if it's already been approved and by which testing body.

As John Warburton explains in the episode , the transparency has become the industry standard for Part L compliance verification. The scheme now has approximately 80 approval companies participating, with one major manufacturer holding over 2,500 individual approvals on the platform.

Why are trickle vents problematic in modern thermally efficient windows?

Trickle vents destroy the thermal performance of highly efficient windows by creating holes through them . A government study published in 2019 found they fail to work effectively precisely when they're needed most—at night with curtains closed—because they only function when wind is blowing, which causes occupants to close them anyway.

Modern building regulations mandate increasingly stringent thermal performance standards. Yet the humble trickle vent—a small opening designed to allow passive ventilation—works directly against these goals. It's a contradiction embedded in the building fabric itself.

The core problem is mechanical: trickle vents depend entirely on wind pressure to drive airflow . At night, when buildings are sealed and curtains drawn to minimize heat loss, there is no driving force. Wind conditions are often nonexistent or insufficient. The vents become dormant, unable to fulfill their intended purpose. Worse, occupants who understand their windows' thermal value will actively close trickle vents to prevent draft and heat loss—negating their presence altogether.

As documented in the research discussed in the episode , the 2019 government study quantified this inefficiency , showing that trickle vents simply don't deliver the internal air quality benefits promised under standard operating conditions. The evidence forced manufacturers and regulators to confront a fundamental question: why mandate a feature that neither works reliably nor serves the thermal objectives of modern windows?

This contradiction has become especially acute as the industry pursues higher U-values and airtightness. Every puncture in a modern window frame represents a thermal weak point. A trickle vent is precisely that—a controlled hole, but a hole nonetheless. The thermal cost of that opening often outweighs any intermittent ventilation benefit.

The conversation around alternative ventilation strategies—mechanical heat recovery ventilation (MHRV), demand-controlled ventilation, and other active systems—has gained momentum as certification bodies and builders recognize that passive trickle vents belong to an earlier era of thermal thinking. As John Warburton explains in his work with ER Certification , modern product testing now grapples with how to handle this legacy feature within certification schemes.

What is the typical failure rate for windows and doors during certification testing?

The chances of products passing certification testing without any issues are remarkably slim. Around 70% of windows and doors fail per testing visit when manufacturers submit samples for weather, security, and performance evaluations. These failures stem from fabrication errors, component defects, or other manufacturing issues that only surface under controlled testing conditions.

When windows and doors arrive at certification labs, they face rigorous testing protocols that expose problems invisible during standard factory quality checks. As John Warburton explains in the Glazing Insider episode , the failure rate reflects the complexity of fenestration products—each component must function flawlessly, and a single weak point triggers a failure.

Why fabrication and components matter in testing

The high failure rate isn't random. Most failures trace back to specific issues: seals not positioned correctly, components from suppliers that don't meet spec, or assembly problems that manufacturing teams miss during production. The certification process acts as an unforgiving checkpoint that catches what internal quality control may have overlooked.

A window that seems perfectly assembled on the factory floor can fail air-tightness testing, water resistance, or security assessment once it reaches the lab. This is precisely why repeated visits are common—manufacturers address failures, resubmit samples, and work through iterations until everything passes. The 70% failure rate per visit underscores that getting fenestration right demands precision at every stage.

What is the biggest change in the fenestration industry over the past 40 years?

The biggest change in fenestration over four decades has been driven by standards and regulations pushing product quality much higher . Products from the 1980s were basic and insecure, but today all systems meet requirements of PAS24 and Secure by Design when assembled correctly, giving consumers far better products in both security and energy performance.

From Trial and Error to Certified Excellence

When John Warburton entered the fenestration industry in the mid-1980s at a small PVC window fabricator in Lancashire, the sector operated in a completely different regulatory landscape. There were no technical manuals, no support from system houses, and manufacturers learned by trial and error —building conservatories and making windows with no concept of how they should actually be constructed, then adapting as experience accumulated.

The products that came out of that era reflected this ad-hoc approach: basic, unrefined, and with minimal security considerations. As Warburton explains in the episode , the transformation accelerated when the industry began to standardize. Standards such as PAS24 and Secure by Design emerged as the benchmark, requiring manufacturers to prove their products met specific performance criteria before they could be sold to consumers.

Today, when these standards are applied correctly during assembly, every window and door entering the market offers a level of security and energy efficiency that would have been unthinkable in the 1980s. This regulatory shift has fundamentally reshaped how manufacturers design, test, and certify their products—moving the entire industry away from improvisation and toward measurable, auditable compliance.

The role of certification bodies like ER Certification became central to this transformation , ensuring that products conform to these standards before they reach the market. This shift from unregulated manufacturing to standardized, certified production represents the most profound change the fenestration sector has experienced in four decades.

What is the difference between running a lifestyle business and scaling it into an operational enterprise?

A lifestyle business is owner-managed and designed to support the owners' lifestyle—as long as it ticks over, everyone is happy. An operational enterprise, by contrast, requires hiring people to handle day-to-day operations, investing in infrastructure and facilities, and allowing the owners to work on the business rather than in it. This transition is the most difficult shift many business owners struggle with.

The original Jade business exemplified the lifestyle model. Profit margins were comfortable, the owners drew what they needed, and the rhythm of work aligned with their personal priorities. But this approach has a hard ceiling: the business can only grow as fast as the owners can personally manage it.

When Adam Jones and Sean acquired J Tools, they inherited a similar structure. As Jones explains in the episode , their goal was fundamentally different from their predecessor's. They needed to transform a lifestyle operation into a real enterprise capable of independent growth and continuity beyond the founders.

The shift from doing the work to building the system

The leap from lifestyle to operational enterprise demands three critical changes. First, you must hire people —not just to add capacity, but to replace yourself in every operational role. This is counterintuitive for founders accustomed to hands-on control and intimately familiar with how things work.

Second, you invest heavily in infrastructure and facilities. A lifestyle business operates lean; an operational enterprise requires systems, documentation, training programs, and physical assets that enable delegation. This represents significant capital outlay with no immediate return.

Third—and perhaps most psychologically challenging—you shift from working *in* the business to working *on* it. Jones and Sean took 18 months to make the decision to hire Gareth as Managing Director, transferring day-to-day leadership responsibility to someone else. As described in Glazing Insider , this move was essential to unlocking the next phase of growth.

The result of this transition: a four-million-pound facility, multiple teams across specialized functions, and a business that no longer depends on any single individual's presence. This is the operational enterprise.
