Why is token cost the right metric to optimize for in AI inference rather than other measures?

Token cost is the current North Star for measuring AI inference work because it's the industry standard today —but it's not the final unit of measurement. As the guest notes, tokens will eventually give way to outcome-based pricing, where agents manage their own token budgets dynamically based on task requirements rather than companies imposing fixed monthly token allocations.

The practical reality is straightforward: tokens are what everyone measures right now. They're the common language across all AI infrastructure providers, making it easy to benchmark, optimize, and improve delivery costs. This focus creates a clear North Star for engineers and infrastructure companies alike.

What makes tokens temporary as a metric is the shift already underway toward agentic reasoning at scale . Once agents become the primary unit of work—autonomous systems that run for hours, days, or weeks and decide how much computation they need—the entire cost model inverts. Instead of a company predicting an engineer's token budget for the month, an individual agent will self-allocate tokens based on the difficulty and nature of the task it's solving.

As discussed in this episode of Invest Like The Best , this outcome-based future means companies will eventually stop thinking about token efficiency as the primary constraint. Instead, they'll optimize for whether an agent solved the problem—and how much it cost to do so. The token ceases to be the unit of work; the solution becomes it.

"My job is to make the tokens as cheap as humanly possible, and I will achieve that through every layer in the stack available to me."

The deeper insight here is that token cost optimization is a transitional problem, not an eternal one . It matters intensely right now because it's what unlocks the next phase: making AI cheap enough that agents can be deployed into every workflow without cost being a barrier. Once that threshold is crossed, the industry's focus shifts upstream to agent performance, reliability, and accuracy rather than raw token efficiency.

From fixed budgets to dynamic allocation

Today's token-cost model mirrors enterprise software licensing: companies decide upfront how much computation they'll buy, then distribute it. This breaks down as soon as agents become standard.

Once an agent is solving real problems—running a research task, debugging code, optimizing a workflow—it needs to allocate tokens in real-time based on task complexity , not according to a monthly budget ceiling. A simple lookup query should consume fewer tokens; a multi-step research problem should be able to allocate more without hitting a hard limit.

This dynamic model only works if token costs become truly negligible. If tokens are expensive, agents have to be constrained; if they're cheap, agents can be trusted to self-regulate. The race to lowest token cost is therefore really a race to enable this agent-driven allocation paradigm—and the infrastructure strategy behind it is detailed in depth in this episode.
