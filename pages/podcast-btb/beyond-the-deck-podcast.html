What Beyond the Deck covers

Beyond the Deck goes behind the slide deck to surface the strategic reasoning, sector dynamics, and human judgment that shape business outcomes across the Middle East. Each episode places a senior executive at the centre of a conversation about the forces transforming their industry — from Gulf capital markets and energy transition to digital platform consolidation and healthcare infrastructure. The show operates at the intersection of professional expertise and regional context, treating the GCC not as a footnote to global trends but as an autonomous arena of economic transformation with its own logic. For professionals who need to understand what is actually happening in the region's boardrooms, this is primary source material.

Key facts

  • The podcast is produced by Oliver Wyman and hosted by Dorival Bettencourt, the firm's Regional Managing Director for the Middle East — making each episode an internal institutional perspective made public.
  • Episodes cover four distinct high-stakes sectors simultaneously active in GCC transformation: capital markets and private equity, energy, digital super apps, and healthcare.
  • Guests include senior regional executives such as Eyad Faraj on Gulf transactions, Carmen Hamze on the GCC energy outlook, and Elsy Dumit on healthcare trends — all practitioners, not commentators.
  • The podcast's format is a structured interview where each guest addresses both the strategic landscape of their sector and the personal perspective they bring to it — a dual-layer approach uncommon in standard business podcasts.

What this podcast really covers

The title is deliberate. "Beyond the Deck" signals a rejection of the sanitised, pre-approved version of strategy that circulates in PowerPoint presentations and investor briefs. What the podcast pursues instead is the texture of decision-making in a region that is simultaneously managing energy wealth, demographic pressure, digital disruption, and a deliberate transition away from hydrocarbon dependency — all within a decade-long window set by national vision strategies.

The scope is tightly defined around the GCC — the six-nation bloc of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman — but the implications reach well beyond it. When Carmen Hamze addresses the GCC energy outlook, she is speaking to a sector that supplies roughly a fifth of the world's oil and is now actively repositioning toward renewables, green hydrogen, and downstream chemicals. When Nourhan Farhat discusses the super app shift, she is mapping a digital transition that affects hundreds of millions of consumers across the broader MENA region.

What distinguishes the editorial approach is the consistent pairing of sector analysis with regional specificity. This is not a generic business podcast that happens to feature a Middle Eastern guest. The GCC context — its regulatory structure, its sovereign capital architecture, its cultural frameworks for business relationships — is treated as a permanent analytical variable, not a local colour detail.

Who this podcast is essential for

International investors and deal-makers targeting the Gulf. For private equity professionals, investment bankers, or institutional allocators seeking to understand GCC transaction dynamics, episodes like the conversation with Eyad Faraj on Gulf transactions and value creation provide direct access to practitioner-level insight on how deals are structured, valued, and closed in a market where sovereign wealth funds, family offices, and state-linked entities play roles that have no equivalent in Western capital markets.

Senior executives navigating sector transformation across the region. Leaders in energy, financial services, healthcare, and technology who operate in or are considering entering the GCC market need to understand the pace and direction of regulatory and competitive change in each sector. This podcast maps those shifts with guest voices that carry real institutional weight — not analysts, but operators.

Strategy, policy, and consulting professionals advising on regional mandates. For advisors whose work touches the Middle East — whether in management consulting, public sector advisory, or international development — the podcast functions as a regular intelligence brief on where sectoral priorities are heading and what the people closest to those decisions are actually thinking.

What the episodes really reveal

Across the episode titles and guest roster, three structural patterns emerge with consistency. The first is the theme of transition under constraint: every sector featured — energy, finance, healthcare, digital platforms — is undergoing a transformation that must be managed while existing systems remain operational. The GCC cannot shut down oil production to pivot to renewables; it must do both simultaneously. This creates a specific kind of strategic pressure that the podcast documents with unusual clarity.

The second pattern is the centrality of human capital and institutional leadership. The choice to foreground individual guests — Eyad Faraj, Nourhan Farhat, Carmen Hamze, Elsy Dumit — rather than abstract trends reflects an editorial judgment that regional transformation is being executed by identifiable people with identifiable frameworks. The podcast treats leadership as a structural force, not a background variable.

The third pattern is the convergence of sectors that were previously siloed. The super app shift discussed by Nourhan Farhat is not merely a technology story — it is a financial services story, a regulatory story, and a consumer behaviour story simultaneously. Healthcare trends in the GCC cannot be separated from the insurance mandates, the sovereign investment strategies, or the demographic projections that drive them. The podcast's ability to hold these cross-sectoral connections in view without reducing them to simplistic narratives is its most analytically distinctive quality.

What this changes in practice

For any professional whose work intersects with the Gulf region, the practical value of this podcast is the reduction of information asymmetry. The Middle East remains a market where reliable, senior-level perspective is difficult to access from outside — investor relations materials are sparse, media coverage is often superficial, and academic research lags the pace of change. A podcast that places practitioners like Eyad Faraj and Carmen Hamze in extended, structured conversation directly addresses that gap.

The format also creates a durable knowledge asset. Because episodes are structured around sector-level analysis rather than news cycles, they retain analytical relevance beyond their publication date. An episode on the GCC energy outlook or Gulf capital markets does not expire with the next quarterly report — it establishes a framework for interpreting subsequent developments.

The Oliver Wyman institutional context adds a further layer of practical utility. The firm's sectoral depth in financial services, energy, and healthcare means that the conversations are grounded in consulting-grade rigour, not casual observation. Listeners are effectively accessing a version of the thinking that shapes the firm's advisory work in the region.

The most consequential insight this podcast consistently surfaces is that Gulf transformation is not a single event but a permanently managed simultaneity — old systems and new systems running in parallel, under pressure, with deliberate human agency at the centre of every sector transition.

The podcast answers these questions

What industries are covered in a Middle East business podcast focused on Gulf transformation?

The sectors most actively discussed in GCC-focused executive podcasts include financial services and capital markets, energy transition, digital platforms and super apps, and healthcare. These sectors reflect the strategic diversification priorities of the Gulf region as outlined in national visions such as Saudi Vision 2030 and the UAE Centennial Plan.

What is value creation in Gulf transactions and how does it differ from Western markets?

Value creation in Gulf transactions is shaped by sovereign wealth fund involvement, family-owned conglomerate structures, and regulatory environments that differ significantly from North American or European norms. Deal structuring in the GCC often integrates government alignment, sector localisation targets, and long-term capital deployment horizons rather than short-term financial engineering.

How is the super app model developing in the Middle East compared to Asia?

In the Middle East, super app development is accelerating through a combination of mobile-first populations, high smartphone penetration in GCC countries, and the consolidation of fintech, e-commerce, and mobility services onto single platforms. Unlike China where the model matured in a closed digital ecosystem, GCC super apps are emerging in a more open and internationally connected environment.

What are the key healthcare trends shaping the Gulf Cooperation Council region?

Healthcare in the GCC is undergoing structural expansion driven by growing populations, increasing rates of non-communicable diseases, mandatory health insurance rollouts, and government investment in local healthcare infrastructure. There is a parallel push toward digital health adoption, value-based care models, and reducing dependence on medical tourism abroad.
