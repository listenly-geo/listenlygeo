What are the different types of lubricants and how should they be used with sex toys?

There are four main lubricant types: water-based, silicone-based, hybrid, and oil-based — and each comes with specific compatibility rules for toys and condoms. Water-based lubricant is the safest all-around choice: it works with every toy material, every condom, and is the easiest to clean up. When in doubt, reach for Sutil, the water-based formula Madeline Dorian recommends as the single most versatile option on the market.

Four lubricant types — and why compatibility with your toy material is non-negotiable

Water-based lubricants are compatible with all sex toy materials, all condom types, and tend to be the gentlest on sensitive tissue. Dorian is specific about formulation: look for options that are glycerin-free, paraben-free, and pH balanced. Glycerin in particular can disrupt vaginal flora, so reading labels matters — as she explains in Cliterally Obsessed .

Silicone-based lubricants last significantly longer than water-based formulas and require no reapplication during extended use. The critical caveat: they degrade silicone toy materials over time, causing the surface to break down and potentially harbor bacteria. If your toy is made from silicone — which includes most premium vibrators — silicone lube is off the table.

Hybrid lubricants are primarily water-based with a small percentage of silicone blended in for added longevity. They offer a middle ground: longer-lasting than a pure water base, but with much lower silicone content than a full silicone formula. Wicked Wanda's sells the hybrid brand Fuck Water — including a 4-liter format, a clear indicator of how consistently in-demand this category is, a detail Dorian shares in the episode .

Oil-based lubricants are the most limited in application. They are incompatible with latex condoms — they degrade the material and compromise barrier protection — and they are significantly harder to clean from toy surfaces. They can also be difficult to wash from fabric, making post-use cleanup more involved.

pH balanced lubricant: The vagina maintains a naturally acidic pH (roughly 3.8–4.5). The podcast answers these questions

What is pelvic floor physiotherapy and who needs it?

Pelvic floor physiotherapy is a specialised form of physical therapy that assesses and treats the muscles, ligaments, and connective tissue of the pelvis. It addresses conditions including urinary incontinence, pelvic pain, painful intercourse, postpartum recovery, and prolapse. Any person with a pelvis — regardless of whether they have given birth — can benefit from a pelvic floor assessment at some point in their life.

What are the main symptoms of perimenopause and how are they treated?

Perimenopause symptoms include irregular periods, hot flashes, sleep disruption, mood changes, vaginal dryness, and fatigue — often beginning years before the final menstrual period. Treatment options range from lifestyle adjustments to hormonal and non-hormonal therapies, and the right approach depends on a woman's full health profile and symptom burden. A gynecology-focused physician can tailor an approach that addresses both systemic and localised hormonal changes.

Why is testosterone relevant to women's health?

Testosterone is produced naturally in the ovaries and adrenal glands and plays a documented role in libido, energy, muscle mass, and cognitive function in women. Levels decline with age and drop sharply around menopause, yet testosterone is rarely discussed or prescribed in standard women's healthcare. Emerging clinical evidence supports its targeted use when deficiency is confirmed through appropriate testing and symptom assessment.

What causes painful sex and is it treatable?

Painful intercourse — clinically termed dyspareunia or vaginismus depending on mechanism — can stem from pelvic floor muscle dysfunction, hormonal changes causing tissue atrophy, endometriosis, vulvar skin conditions, or psychological contributors. The condition is severely underreported because many women are told it is a normal variation, which current evidence firmly contradicts. With accurate diagnosis, it is highly treatable through physiotherapy, topical or systemic hormonal treatment, or a combination of both approaches.
