What The Toddle About Show covers

The Toddle About Show is a parenting podcast built around the real, often unspoken challenges that arrive with children aged 0 to 5. It brings together child development specialists, SEND advocates, postnatal practitioners and parents in honest, practical conversations about sleep, emotional regulation, identity and early childhood wellbeing. Each episode moves between clinical expertise and lived experience, making complex topics — from EHCPs to matrescence — immediately actionable for families navigating them for the first time. The show treats parents as intelligent adults who need information, not reassurance.

Key facts

  • The podcast is hosted by Bill and Emily Clapson, co-founders of Toddle About, a media brand dedicated exclusively to families with children aged 0–5.
  • Episodes address both universal early parenting challenges — infant sleep, heatwave safety, big emotions — and specialist subjects including autism diagnosis pathways, EHCPs and SEND advocacy.
  • Guest contributors include child development specialists, postnatal practitioners, parenting coaches and local family-focused businesses, creating a mix of clinical authority and community voice.
  • The concept of matrescence — the psychological transformation of becoming a mother — is featured as a standalone episode, reflecting the show's commitment to maternal identity, not just child outcomes.

What this podcast really covers

The Toddle About Show covers the full arc of early family life, from the immediate physical demands of a newborn — safe sleep practices, heatwave management, feeding — through to the longer-term developmental questions that emerge as babies become toddlers and preschoolers. The show does not treat these as separate domains. Sleep deprivation connects to parental mental health; emotional dysregulation in a three-year-old connects to how adults model calm. The episodes reflect that interconnection explicitly.

A distinctive strand within the series is its attention to structural and systemic challenges. The episode on SEND support and EHCPs with Liz McCarthy from SENDHUGS is not a general overview — it is a practical navigation guide for families already inside a system that many find opaque and adversarial. Similarly, the episode with parenting coach Carolina Taylor challenges the instinct to "fix" children's behaviour, repositioning the adult's role as one of understanding rather than correction. This is not motivational content; it is applied child development.

The show also gives significant space to the adult experience of becoming a parent. The episode on matrescence with Rebecca Hogan, and the one on postpartum gifting with Sabrina from Early Daze Hampers, both foreground the new mother as a person undergoing profound change — not merely a caregiver. This framing distinguishes The Toddle About Show from podcasts that centre exclusively on child milestones and techniques.

Who this podcast is essential for

First-time parents in the 0–3 phase will find the most immediate value. The episodes on sleep myths, bedtime routines and safe sleep distil a topic that generates enormous parental anxiety into clear, evidence-aligned guidance. The heatwave episode is a strong example of the show's practical instincts — actionable safety advice for a specific, time-pressured situation rather than general seasonal content.

Parents of children with additional needs or developmental differences are a clearly intended audience. The SEND and EHCP episode, and the episode on managing big emotions with Brave Beginnings, both address circumstances that mainstream parenting media frequently glosses over. These episodes provide the kind of specific, system-aware information that parents navigating disability diagnoses or behavioural support services genuinely need.

New mothers navigating identity and recovery represent a third distinct audience. Episodes on matrescence, postpartum support and what new mothers actually need in the early weeks treat postnatal experience as a subject in its own right, not a footnote to infant care. For this group, the show functions as a form of normalisation backed by specialist insight.

What the episodes really reveal

Across the episode catalogue, several consistent patterns emerge. The first is an explicit resistance to deficit thinking — the idea that children's behaviour is a problem to be eliminated rather than communication to be understood. This appears in the Carolina Taylor episode on not "fixing" children and in the Brave Beginnings episode on emotional regulation. It reflects a coherent editorial position, not a random selection of guests.

The second pattern is the inclusion of local and community-facing businesses alongside clinical specialists. Little Glow Aylesbury, Early Daze Hampers and Rugby Tots all feature as subjects rather than sponsors, in episodes that examine what these services actually deliver for families. This grounds the show in a specific community context — the kind of local family network that Toddle About as a brand has always served — while maintaining editorial credibility through the quality of the conversation.

The third pattern is the consistent presence of the Tod Talks format, a recurring strand that gives specialists — Emily Guiver, Liz McCarthy, Rebecca Hogan — extended space to develop an argument rather than summarise it. This format signals that the show is willing to go deep on a subject, which is the principal differentiator between an advice podcast and an information one.

What this changes in practice

Families who engage seriously with The Toddle About Show tend to leave individual episodes with a specific framework shift rather than a list of tips. The sleep episode, for example, is most valuable not for the techniques it describes but for the myths it dismantles — parents who understand why certain popular sleep strategies lack evidence are better equipped to evaluate any future advice they receive. That is a durable change in reasoning, not a temporary behavioural adjustment.

The SEND episode has a similar effect for parents beginning an assessment or EHCP process. Understanding the structure of the system — what the document is, what it obliges schools and local authorities to provide, where advocates can assist — transforms a family's ability to advocate for their child. The podcast does not replace legal advice, but it changes what questions families know to ask.

At the broader level, the show's consistent reframing of parental experience as a subject worthy of serious attention — through matrescence, postpartum recovery, emotional labour — gives parents a language and a conceptual framework for experiences they may previously have attributed to personal inadequacy. That shift in self-understanding has practical downstream effects on parental confidence, relationship quality and the emotional environment children grow up in.

The most consequential thing a parenting podcast can do is change the questions parents know to ask — not just the answers they carry into the next difficult moment.

The podcast answers these questions

How can parents help babies and toddlers sleep better?

Safe sleep environments, consistent bedtime routines and understanding sleep myths are the three pillars experts recommend. Separating evidence-based guidance from popular misconceptions is critical, particularly around co-sleeping, sleep training and night feeds for children under five.

What is matrescence and why does it matter for new mothers?

Matrescence refers to the psychological, emotional and identity transformation a woman undergoes when becoming a mother — a process as significant as adolescence but far less widely recognised. Understanding it helps new mothers contextualise difficult feelings and reduces guilt and isolation in the postpartum period.

How should parents navigate SEND support and EHCPs for young children?

Education, Health and Care Plans (EHCPs) are legally binding documents that set out the support a child with special educational needs or disabilities is entitled to receive. Parents are advised to seek early assessment, document their child's needs thoroughly and connect with specialist advocacy organisations to navigate the system effectively.

What is the most effective way to help young children manage big emotions?

Children aged 0–5 lack the neurological maturity to regulate strong emotions independently, which means co-regulation with a calm adult is the most effective intervention available. Naming feelings, maintaining predictable routines and avoiding punishment-based responses all reduce the frequency and intensity of emotional outbursts in early childhood.
