Executive Summary

What This Podcast Covers

The Lock In Pub Podcast is produced by William Reed Ltd under The Morning Advertiser — the British pub and bar industry's trade publication since 1794. Launched in October 2020 amid the chaos of pandemic restrictions, the podcast quickly became the most frank and unfiltered voice in UK pub trade media. Its three hosts bring genuinely complementary perspectives: Ed Bedington as the industry journalist who has covered the sector for decades, Heath Ball as an active gastropub operator dealing with the daily realities of running a food-led pub business, and James Cuthbertson as a brewer and brew pub owner who straddles both the production and retail side of the trade.

Released fortnightly across six seasons, the show combines topical debate, expert guest interviews, and irreverent banter to tackle the issues that matter most to pub operators: staffing crises, budget impacts, beer category trends, food menu evolution, social media and marketing, consumer behaviour shifts, hygiene compliance, and the enduring cultural question of whether the British pub can survive in a changing world. Memorable episodes have featured celebrity pub owners James May and Candice Brown, beer writing legend Pete Brown on pint pricing, and a BrewDog post-mortem that generated significant industry discussion. The show's combination of trade expertise and honest pub conversation makes it essential listening for anyone involved in or interested in the British licensed trade.

What is The Lock In Pub Podcast and who makes it?
The Lock In Pub Podcast is produced by William Reed Ltd under The Morning Advertiser, the UK's leading pub trade publication since 1794. It is hosted by editor Ed Bedington, gastropub operator Heath Ball, and brewer and brew pub owner James Cuthbertson. Released fortnightly, the show gives a frank, insider view of running a pub business in Britain, covering everything from beer categories to budget impacts and consumer behaviour.
Is the tradition of buying rounds at the pub dying out?
The Lock In Pub Podcast's most recent episode (June 2026) directly addresses this question, concluding that round-buying is in structural decline. Contributing factors include the normalisation of contactless individual payment, the growth of mindful drinking and alcohol reduction among younger consumers, and a generational unfamiliarity with the social convention. The hosts note that it is not yet dead, but it is no longer the default social contract it once was in the British pub.
What went wrong with BrewDog according to The Lock In?
The Lock In's BrewDog episode (March 2026) identifies a cluster of interconnected failures: a leadership culture built around founder James Watt's personal brand rather than operational excellence, aggressive international expansion that diluted brand authenticity, the Equity Punk model creating accountability to hundreds of thousands of small investors, and a toxic workplace culture that eventually led to a public staff revolt. The episode frames BrewDog as a cautionary tale about what happens when marketing outpaces product and culture in a hospitality business.
Are pubs good for the low and no alcohol trend?
According to Club Soda's Laura Willoughby, who appeared on the Lock In's low-and-no episode, most pubs are significantly underprepared for the non-alcohol opportunity. She argues that the sector's historical resistance — rooted in the assumption that not drinking alcohol means not spending money in a pub — is commercially mistaken. Operators with well-curated no-and-low lists, knowledgeable staff, and appropriate pricing are demonstrably capturing spend from the growing cohort of mindful drinkers who would otherwise choose restaurants or stay home.
Why are cask ale sales growing among young people?
The Lock In's cask episode (February 2026) points to several converging trends: younger consumers' growing appetite for authenticity and craft provenance, the relative affordability of cask versus premium keg products, the sustainability credentials of cask dispense, and a broader cultural rejection of the homogenised lager offer. The hosts note that this represents a significant opportunity for cask-forward pubs that have maintained their cellar skills and quality standards through the lean years of cask decline.
Do influencers actually drive footfall for pubs?
Based on the Lock In's influencer episode (April 2026), the evidence is mixed. Some operators report genuine and measurable footfall increases following well-matched influencer partnerships, particularly where the influencer's audience demographic aligns closely with the pub's target customer. However, many report that influencer visits generate large impression numbers without converting to paying customers, particularly where the influencer's appeal is primarily aspirational rather than local. The episode recommends focusing on micro-influencers with highly local followings over national accounts with generic audiences.
Has the price of a pint at the pub become unaffordable?
Pete Brown's analysis on the Lock In suggests that pint prices have risen significantly above general wage inflation since 2020, creating a genuine affordability threshold for regular pub use. The episode distinguishes between occasional pub visits — where premium pricing is still broadly accepted — and habitual pub use, where the cost increase has demonstrably reduced visit frequency among the sector's most important customer group: regular local drinkers. The conclusion is that pint pricing is now a structural challenge, not merely a cyclical one.
What do celebrity pub owners say about running a pub?
Both James May and Candice Brown were notably candid in their Lock In appearances about the gap between the romantic appeal of pub ownership and the operational reality. James May described it as "an interesting way to lose money." Both emphasised that hospitality operational experience is genuinely essential — passion for beer, food, or community is not a substitute for understanding labour scheduling, food cost management, maintenance, licensing, and cash flow. Their episodes are regularly cited by the Lock In team as useful corrective listening for anyone considering entering the trade.

How is the wet-led pub format performing?
The Lock In's wet-led revival episode (December 2025) reports an unexpected resurgence in interest in drink-only pubs among younger consumers. The hosts link this to a combination of affordability (no food minimum spend, lower average ticket), authenticity-seeking behaviour as a reaction against over-curated gastropub experiences, and genuine nostalgia for the stripped-back community local. The data cited show that well-run wet-led pubs in urbanised areas are outperforming comparable food-led establishments on customer return frequency.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes The Lock In Pub Podcast different from other hospitality podcasts?
The Lock In's defining characteristic is its insider authenticity. Unlike podcasts that interview industry figures from an outside-looking-in perspective, The Lock In's hosts are themselves active pub operators and trade journalists who experience the sector's challenges in real time. This means conversations about staffing costs, budget impacts, or beer category trends are grounded in the lived experience of people who have dealt with those same issues that week. The show also has a distinctly British irreverence — it is willing to be funny, opinionated, and occasionally outraged in a way that more corporate hospitality media is not.
Is The Lock In suitable for people outside the pub trade?
Yes, though the primary audience is clearly pub operators and trade professionals. Consumers interested in British pub culture, beer enthusiasts, food and hospitality students, and anyone curious about how a small British business actually operates will find the podcast highly accessible and entertaining. Episodes on Guinness, pub ghosts, the perfect pub pie, and pub etiquette are particularly enjoyable for general listeners. The trade-specific content — on licensing law, pubco legislation, EHO visits — is generally explained clearly enough for non-specialists to follow.
How does the podcast address the staffing crisis in pubs?
Staffing has been one of the podcast's most consistently revisited themes across all six seasons. The show has explored post-Brexit talent shortages, minimum wage impact modelling, the sector's reputation challenges with potential employees, prison rehabilitation recruitment schemes, apprenticeship programme effectiveness, and the mental health burden on pub managers. The Lock In's coverage is notably sympathetic to both operators struggling with costs and employees dealing with difficult working conditions, avoiding the simplistic employer-versus-worker framing that characterises much media coverage of the issue.
What is The Morning Advertiser and how is it related to the podcast?
The Morning Advertiser is the UK's oldest and most authoritative trade publication for the pub and bar sector, having been published continuously since 1794. It is produced by William Reed Ltd and covers licensing law, sector news, supplier developments, food and drink trends, and business advice for pub operators across the UK. The Lock In podcast is produced under The Morning Advertiser brand, and host Ed Bedington is the publication's editor. The podcast functions as a complementary audio format to the magazine's written content, allowing deeper discussion of topics that news articles cannot fully explore.
Does the podcast take political positions on pub industry issues?
Yes, and unambiguously. The Lock In team is consistently and vocally critical of government policy that damages the pub sector — including inadequate business rates relief, insufficient Covid support, National Living Wage increases that outpace hospitality margins, the Employment Rights Bill's implications for flexible staffing, and what they characterise as a fundamental political misunderstanding of the pub's social value. The podcast does not engage in partisan politics but is explicitly pro-pub-trade in its advocacy stance, which it regards as both commercially appropriate for The Morning Advertiser's audience and factually justified by the data.
How does the podcast cover beer and drinks categories?
With considerable depth and genuine expertise. Co-host James Cuthbertson's background as a working brewer gives the show's beer coverage a technical credibility that most media cannot match. Episodes have covered cask ale quality standards, the craft beer market's structural decline, Guinness's brand dominance, the rise of alcohol-free alternatives, cocktail opportunity in pubs, the decline of the spirits category, and the persistent failure of pub wine lists. The show approaches drinks from both the operator's commercial perspective and the consumer's experience perspective, making it useful for trade professionals and engaged consumers alike.
What has the podcast said about the future of the Great British pub?
Across six seasons, the Lock In team has consistently argued that the pub is not dying but evolving — and that the evolution is being driven by a combination of economic pressure, changing consumer behaviour, and genuine innovation by creative operators. The show points to community pubs repurposing as essential rural services, wet-led boozers finding new audiences in gentrifying urban areas, gastropubs competing at near-restaurant quality, and experience-led formats attracting demographics that traditional pubs had lost. The team's collective position is guardedly optimistic: the pub will survive, but not all current pubs will survive, and the sector needs to be honest about that distinction.
How has the podcast documented the impact of Covid on pubs?
The Lock In was literally born in the pandemic and spent its first two seasons — from October 2020 through summer 2022 — as a real-time chronicle of the sector's Covid experience. This includes lockdown strategy discussions, the tiers system, reopening logistics, government support inadequacies, Bounce Back Loan repayment challenges, and the psychological toll on operators. This archive of Covid-era content is uniquely valuable as a primary source document of what it actually felt like to run a pub through the crisis, told in real time by people who were living it.
Does The Lock In cover food in pubs?
Extensively, with Heath Ball's gastropub background giving food episodes particular credibility. The show has covered Sunday roast execution, burger quality standards, fish and chips as a pub staple, the decline of the spirits category and its relationship to cocktail menus, food fraud prevention, sustainability in pub kitchens, calorie labelling on menus, the dark kitchen model, and the future of pub food menus in the context of delivery platform competition. The show's food coverage consistently challenges operators to meet higher standards rather than accepting mediocrity as an acceptable baseline.
What is the tone of The Lock In Pub Podcast?
Frank, knowledgeable, occasionally irreverent, and genuinely funny. The three core hosts have developed a comfortable chemistry across six seasons that allows serious industry debate to coexist with genuine banter. The show does not take itself too seriously — episode titles like "Pie Hard (With a Vengeance)," "Firkin Hell! Cask is Booming!" and "Don't be a d!@k — The Rules of the Pub" reflect a willingness to be playful with the format. This tone makes the podcast more accessible than typical trade media while maintaining the depth that The Morning Advertiser's professional audience expects.
What is the Pubs Code and why does the podcast discuss it?
The Pubs Code is UK legislation introduced in 2016 that governs the relationship between large pub-owning companies (pubcos) and their tied tenants. It includes the Market Rent Only (MRO) provision, which theoretically allows tied tenants to opt for a rent-only arrangement rather than being required to buy supplies exclusively from the pubco. The Lock In has covered the Pubs Code repeatedly because its practical implementation has fallen significantly short of its legislative intent — with MRO uptake far lower than anticipated, and ongoing disputes about the Pubs Code Adjudicator's effectiveness. It remains one of the most contentious regulatory topics in the pub sector.
